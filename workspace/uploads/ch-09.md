# VIGILANTE
### Part Two · The System — Chapter Nine: Lakha

The mandi called him by the name the phone had given, and the name checked out, and that was the survey.

It had taken two nights to find the collection yard — the milk came into the old quarter at 2:40 in tankers and left in tempos by 4, and the tempo on the Bhagwanpura routes was driven by a man the loaders called Lakha, and no one called him anything else. His mother's house, wherever it was, would have called him something different. The mandi never learned it. He never offered.

Manav bought a bicycle. A Hero, second-hand, the era's cheapest invisibility — no 44-year-old clerk on a cycle at 3 a.m. has ever been seen by anyone — and for eleven nights he learned the route the way he had once learned claims: by its habits, its pauses, its lies.

The route had a liturgy. Collection at 2:40. First run at 4. Between them, forty minutes at the wheel, and the man did not sleep in that forty minutes — he sat in the tempo's cab with his eyes open and watched the mandi wake, and the mandi dogs came to the window one at a time, and he fed them from a bag he bought with his own money before he ate anything himself. Tuesdays he drove the far side of the ring, and slowed past a lane where bougainvillea grew over a green gate, and did not stop, and had not stopped in three years. Tuesdays he also went to the temple near the timber market and prayed, if that was the word, with his eyes open the whole time — watching the door, watching the sky through the door, watching.

The man did not sleep inside. He slept at the wheel, in the open, under whatever the night was doing. Once, near dawn, he slept for twenty minutes on the tempo's hood, flat on his back in the yard with the dogs around him like unpaid staff, and Manav, thirty meters off in the shadow of the weighbridge, wrote it in the ledger of his head: *the guilty sleep outside. They think walls are where it happens.*

He had expected, if he was honest — and the file under the file was where he kept his honesty — to find a monster or a nothing. He found a man doing the things he himself did. Feeding something that could not testify. Keeping hours no one had asked him to keep. Watching a home he could not enter, on a schedule, on Tuesdays.

*Findings: the subject is not fleeing justice. He is serving a sentence already. Custody would be a transfer.*

He wrote that in the ledger, and then, because findings have consequences, he took out the police file of his own house and read the inventory until the anger came back. It came slower every year now. He had to prime it, the way you prime a dry pump.

On the fifth night the phone lit once, numbered:

*C-6. Two lengths further. He counts faces.*

He counts faces. Of course he counted faces. Three years of counting them. Manav dropped back two lengths, and the gift, as always, had been double-edged: protection for the instrument, and a small cold seminar on how far away the instrument's keeper could see.

The app sent nothing else, all eleven nights. He noted that too. It had given him the name and then stood aside, as if the gift were the whole of its interest and the using was the point.

---

The plan, when it was ready, was a counter.

It was, he was fairly sure, the first plan of its kind in the history of the city's night: three weeks of being a face. The tea stall at the mandi gate, 3 a.m., the same man, the same kurta, until the loaders stopped noticing and Lakha's count of faces absorbed him as furniture — and then, one night, the slide onto the next stool, and *these runs keep you thin*, and the slow bureaucratic miracle of a stranger becoming a person, and a person becoming someone you tell things. He had done it twenty-one years across desks. The insured tell the assessor everything, eventually, because the assessor is the only one who ever asks in a voice with no weather in it.

That was the plan. The claims assessor's patience, not the mask's.

In July the boy turned ten. One breath for the candles, the wish unsaid the way wishes are. Sethi's envelope arrived on the birthday itself, punctual, too much, *from the floor, ha ha* — the floor had now done this three years running — and the cricket kit stood in the room where things lived, used twice. Manav watched his son arrive at double digits over a small cake in a flat where the television kept its vigil, and across the city a man was sleeping at a wheel with his eyes open, and both facts lived in him now, side by side, in separate drawers, not meeting.

The office got his mornings that summer the way it had always had them, which was the whole trick. A man cannot be suspected of a night if his mornings are immaculate.

And Farid — Farid, whose stall sat at the mandi's edge like a fixed star — understood without being told. He had watched the man on the cycle for four nights before the kettle said anything about it, and what the kettle said was two cups. One at the bench. One at the far end, at watching distance. Nobody discussed it. The night city had taken a side, or declined to, which in that city amounted to the same side.

---

On the ninth night, the list came to the mandi.

Manav arrived at 3:10 to a silence he had never heard in that hundred-year gossip of a place — the loaders standing in a ring around a patch of ground near the weighbridge where nothing was. No smoke, no light, no sound, the men said afterward, asked by nobody, telling nobody. Between one load and the next. A weighbridge clerk who had doctored his weights for years, the mandi knew, the whole mandi knew, and the mandi had gossiped about everything under its awnings for a century, and it did not gossip about this. A shoe stood on the empty patch, rightside up, precise. The dogs would not go near it.

Lakha had been parked closest. Lakha had seen.

The tempo sat where it sat, and in its window the man's face was the face of a dropped claim. He did not do the 4 o'clock run. He did the Tuesday drive-past on a Thursday, twice, slow, the bougainvillea going by, and then he fed the dogs the bread he had not touched and sat on the hood and looked at the sky over the old quarter for a long time, with his eyes open, the way he prayed.

A week later the receipt came for the clerk, in the manner of receipts — a weighbridge book, three years of false grams, delivered to a newspaper by nobody — and the mandi read about its own silence in the papers, and said nothing about that either.

That night the app sent nothing, and Manav understood something small and very large, standing in the yard at watching distance with the two-dog night going on around him: it did not correct the list. Nobody corrected the list. Whatever its ledgers held, whatever its minutes were worth, the thing at the mandi had happened in front of its instruments like weather.

The eleventh night, Lakha's route ran ragged and turned, at 3:40, away from the mandi — out past the last of the numbered sectors, to a tower the era had stopped mid-build. Sixteen floors of intention, standing in its scaffolding at the ring's edge, where the money had gotten superstitious. A place a man goes up when the decision is already made and the legs are only informing the body.

The tempo parked at the site gate. The man went in through the shutter with a bottle, on foot, unhurried, at the end of everything.

Manav stood at the bottom of the stairwell with the mask in his fist and did the last audit.

The plan — the stall, the tea, three weeks of being a face — was a plan for a man with time. The man on the stairs had a morning in him somewhere, a bus, a border, and eleven nights of watching said *now or never*, and the mask was the only language that stops a running man in the dark.

A confession to a mask would stand up in no court in the world.

He went up anyway. The court had never been the point; the why was; and there was one more filing, made honestly, in the file under the file, where he kept himself: that a part of him had been waiting since the underpass — since the ribs, since the seventy rupees of chai, since the night the dark produced company — to be the thing the night saw, instead of the thing it happened to.

The stairwell was pitch, then grey, then grey with wind in it. He came up the last flight at ten past four and stepped out onto the gravel, and the city lay away to the dark edge of itself under the sodium sky, and the man was standing at the parapet with his hands open, a bottle set down carefully at his feet, straight-backed, the way men stand for inspections.

He turned. He did not run.

He had been waiting three years.
