# VIGILANTE
### Part Two · The System — Chapter Eight: The Courtship

The courtship, if it could be called that, was conducted between eleven and three, at a kitchen table, between a man with a ledger and a phone that weighed nothing.

June had normalized the era the way June normalizes heat. The list had taken two men that month; the papers printed them below the commodity prices, and the city read both over tea and wondered about neither. The network behind the marble market had gone cautious — lights later, runs fewer, the drop-house counting its own money twice — the dark had management now, and the management was bad for everyone's business, including his. The hunt that had run on five months of bench-work was slowing to nothing, and both parties at the kitchen table knew it.

He had decided, early, on a protocol: numbered messages were its own openings; the answers it gave his questions carried no numbers, as if his questions were not events, only weather. It corrected. It did not converse.

First, the audit. He had spent his life verifying claimants, and he verified this one the only way he knew.

He asked it about a fire in a basement in 2011. It answered with the meter's serial number. He had stood in that meter room himself, in the smoke, twenty-one years of surveys ago, and had never known the meter had a name.

Then he did what he had wanted for years to do to certain claimants: he salted the file. A true story — the jeweller, the November confession — with one false fact set into it like a stitch. *The jeweller broke in the third interview,* he typed.

The answer came back in one word.

*Second.*

It had not been offended. It had not been fooled. It had not let it stand.

Three rules, then, learned the way he had learned everything, at small cost, on purpose. One: it did not lie. Two: it did not let him lie. Three: everything else was a choice, and the choices were its own.

---

He asked it the questions he shouldn't.

*Who are you.* Silence — with a texture he knew from counters: the silence of a party that has weighed the cost of answering and found it above the premium.

*Who pays you.* The same silence, exactly the same, which was itself a datum: the two silences had the same author.

*Why me.*

Nothing. And then, at four in the morning, when he had put the phone down and picked it up once more the way a tongue returns to a tooth:

*Why should I trust you?*

*You shouldn't. You have verified every correction and acted on every one. Trust is not what this is.*

He sat with that. It was the first answer that had given him nothing to check and everything to look at. It answered the way a mirror answers — with himself, arranged slightly truer.

*Does the mask have anything to do with the takings?* — the city's own scripture, put to the only oracle he had.

*That is not a claim. It cannot be audited.*

Not *I don't know.* Not *no.* A refusal of category — the first question it had declined as a thing that could exist. He noted it in the ledger, in the small hand he used for findings that did not fit: *the source's system ends where the sky begins. Or the source is careful. Both filings live.*

One more, that week, at two in the morning, the question underneath all his questions:

*Is anything watching my flat?*

*No. Not even me. Houses are the one thing I cannot enter.*

The relief arrived before the thought did, and the thought arrived anyway: it was the first time in three years anything had told him the flat was his. And underneath that, quiet as a page turning, the other thought, the one he did not file: *it has never once said the boy's name.* Not in correction, not in care, not in the wrist, not in the sleep. He noticed, the way he noticed everything now. He did not ask why. Some not-askings are load-bearing.

The wrist had got its ice in the end — pride lasts until the second swollen morning. The care continued on a schedule of its own choosing:

*C-4. Sleep. You have slept nine hours this week that your body agrees to count.*

He obeyed it the way he obeyed Ustad, the way he sat where Farid could see him, and hated the mechanism by which he obeyed, which he could not find anywhere in the phone.

It occurred to him one Thursday, filing the night away, that the app had now heard more of his voice than any human being had since the hospital. He put that under findings, where it did the most damage, and left it there, because findings, unlike people, stay where you put them.

---

Summer had emptied the mornings of the school van. Aarav was home all day now, and had begun, without announcement, making the evening tea — carefully, measuring the leaf, watching the colour come, the way he did everything, entirely — and at dinner, one night, out of nowhere, over the one television in the city that did not carry the news:

"Papa. Can it see inside houses?"

The *it* needed no antecedent. The era lived in children now, like weather.

"Nobody knows that," Manav said.

"That's still not the same as no," the boy said, and poured his father's tea, and the era and the epistemology and the tea all arrived together, and Manav drank what he was given.

---

And then, one night in late June, at one in the morning, he asked the question underneath all the others, the one he had been not-asking since the wrist:

*The crew. Four men. How many are alive?*

The answer took four seconds, which was long, for it.

*Three.*

Nothing followed. The number stood in the kitchen the way a man stands in a doorway. He typed: *Which one.*

*The one they called Chotu. He died outside Panipat in a card dispute fourteen months ago. The file on him is closed.*

Chotu. He held the phone with both hands. Chotu was a name out of a dark place — out of the underpass, the January of the ribs, a man's voice calling a name into the dark, *Chotu aa gaya*, and the dark producing company. One of the four. One of the four had helped beat him in an underpass, three feet away, over seventy rupees of chai and the price of being seen — and had then lived fourteen more months, and died over cards, and the quarter of the why that lived in his mouth had died with him, unmourned, unaudited, below the commodity prices.

He typed the only thing left:

*You knew.*

*You did not ask.*

He sat back from the table. It was the truest sentence the app had ever sent him, and the first one he could not file without bleeding. Formally correct — he had not asked; it had not volunteered; both parties had acted within the rules of a contract nobody had shown him. And the whole of the wrongness in the world sitting inside the formal correctness, like a settlement that pays out and pays out and changes nothing.

He had known counterparties like this.

He had been, on certain days, a counterparty like this.

He put the phone face-down and did not type again for two days, and the phone, with a patience that felt like the whole of its character, sent nothing at all, and the silence had the texture of a bench: of Farid's bench, of a landing with phones on it — of things that wait without watching.

On the third night, at 1:40, unprompted, numbered:

*C-5. The one they called Lakha. Milk-route tempo, Bhagwanpura side. He sleeps at the wheel between runs. He has not been home since that winter. He was never difficult to find. He was only difficult to name.*

He read it until the words stopped being words, and then he read them again, and the name stood in the kitchen the way a man stands in a doorway.

Lakha.

Two years of nothing, and then three years of nothing, and now a name — after the anger, not before it; after the withholding, not before it; exactly when a gift would bind a claimant most. He noted the timing, the way he noted everything, and noted also that the noting had not made him put the name down.

He did not write it in the ledger.

Paper forgets. This one he would keep in the meat.
