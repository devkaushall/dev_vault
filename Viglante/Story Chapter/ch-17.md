# VIGILANTE
### Part Three · The Watchers — Chapter Seventeen: The Kitchen Table

The bandages were already out.

They were laid on the kitchen table in a row, sorted by size, the way instruments are laid out — gauze, the roll, the brown bottle, the tape in its tin cutter — and the towel beside them had been folded in thirds, like letters she had decided not to send. His hands had learned that fold somewhere without asking him. So, it turned out, had his son's.

The kettle was off the flame and steaming. The first-aid box stood open on the counter with its lid back on its hinge, and the box was not new to this. Things in it were used to being replaced. The gauze was new; the tape was new; someone had been buying gauze, quietly, for how long was one of the questions he would not be asking, because he already knew the shape of the answer.

Aarav stood at the counter with his sleeves already rolled.

"Sit," the boy said.

Manav sat.

---

The boy worked the way he packed a bag: in an order that suggested lists. The shirt had to come off; the boy took it off him the way orderlies take things off men, without ceremony, waiting out the wince at the ribs with a patience that had a schedule. The lip he cleaned first, because it bled. Then the hands.

The boy's hands did not shake. Manav noticed the not-shaking the way you notice a professional's shoes, and filed it, and the file had nowhere to go. He had done this to himself for four years in bathrooms with the lock on, with a mirror's grudging help, clenching his jaw against the sound of his own name in his own mouth. He had never once been tended. The tenderness was precise, and impersonal, and ten years old.

"Beta," he said. "You weren't supposed to see any of this."

The boy did not spend a word on it. He tore the tape with his teeth, a thing he had no business knowing how to do quickly.

"For the register," the boy said, to the ribs, working the strapping, "I'll say you fell on the stairs. Stairs is better than the office. The office sends forms, and after the forms, a man."

The stairs. The family's oldest piece of furniture. *You and Mama always said the stairs* — four years old, that lie, and the boy had carried it since seven, and improved it.

"You've thought about this," Manav said, and heard how a father's voice sounds saying it: like an accusation, when it was a census.

"Since the second winter," the boy said.

The kitchen went very still.

"You heard the chain," Manav said.

"The chain, the pause, the eye at the gap. Every night you go out, I hear all three." The boy smoothed the tape down, twice, the way the wrap is finished. "You think I sleep through the chain, Papa. Nobody sleeps through the chain. I stopped telling you I was awake because you started needing me to be asleep."

He said it without bitterness, which was the worst available way to say it. He reached for the roll of gauze, and Manav looked at the towel folded in thirds, and at the row of dressings sorted by size, and understood that he was sitting in the output of a watch older than the mask. Two watches in one flat, all those years, each keyed to the other's sounds. He had checked the chain at three in the morning and gone out to keep the city safe from his own house, and the house had been awake the whole time, keeping him.

"I've seen worse," the boy said.

He said it to the gauze, matter-of-fact, a worker stating a qualification, and it was four words long, and it closed the door on every conversation Manav had rehearsed on the stairs.

---

"Didi said don't tell," Aarav said. "The last thing she said to me. *Eyes closed. Whatever you hear, it's the TV.*" He wrapped the right hand now, over the thumb, twice at the wrist, competent, a thing learned from a phone, and the thought had a basement in it. "She thought hiding was one night, Papa. She didn't know you'd keep going. She didn't know it would be a job."

The water ticked in the cooling kettle. Down the hall, faint, the boy's television played to an empty room — it had played every night for four years, all night, low; he had stopped hearing it as anything but the house. It had never been the house. It had been a seven-year-old keeping a dead girl's instruction running like a generator, so that anything he heard could still be the TV.

"She came to the almirah twice," the boy said. "The second time she didn't say anything. She just looked in through the slats, at me, and then she went back out and was loud, so they would follow the loud."

The night came in fragments and he let it, and the fragments were small, and each one was load-bearing. The slats, and the light through them lying on the floor in stripes. A face going by close, twice — an eyebrow with a cut in it, a tooth gone black at the side. And the name, because they called him, the way crews call each other, called him twice, like a summons.

"Lakha," the boy said, in the exact voice his mother had used for jokes that were not.

---

Manav sat with the name the way you sit with a sum that comes out even and wrong.

Two years ago, numbered, in the rain month: *C-5. The one they called Lakha. Milk-route tempo, Bhagwanpura side. He has not been home since that winter. He was never difficult to find. He was only difficult to name.*

The app had been wrong in the only way it was ever wrong: in what it chose to finish. The name had never been difficult. The name had been kept — six feet from where the mask was stored, between two closed files, behind a boy's locked door, held for four years under a dead girl's command. The gift had been true. The gifts were always true. That had never once been the complaint.

And the case — his case, the locked room he had bled for — had never been locked at all. Three attempted statements, three weeks running, three findings of *child not fit to be examined*, one signature on all three. The file had called it unfitness. *Fear is rare,* Ustad had said, in a basement that smelled of iron, *loyalty is common — faithful, often to the wrong things. But you cannot fault the faith.* The boy had not been unfit. The boy had been the most fit witness in the city, and he had stood at three interviews and said nothing, seven years old, on duty, because his sister had told him the television would handle the rest.

*Ira surfaced twice. Both times the same sentence, the second time slower, both times her eyes going past him, to the door.* Four years he had read that look as a girl looking for a doctor. It had been a guard checking a door. *Aarav ne kuch nahin dekha.* He had answered it like a question — *yes, beta, of course, he was asleep* — and it had never been a question. It had been a last instruction, issued to a corridor with her eyes on the door, and the whole family had kept it like a constitution: the boy behind the slats, the father at the hospital door, each certain they were carrying it alone.

"Lakha," Manav said, to hear it in his own mouth, and the boy nodded once, a claim assessed and passed.

"He's the one the heaven took," the boy said. "From the roof. It was in the forwards."

"Yes."

"Good," the boy said, without heat; and then, because he was ten and not a machine: "It isn't good. I don't know what it is. It's finished, and I'm still on watch, and I would like that to be noted."

"It's noted," Manav said.

---

The boy closed the box the way he closed his school bag: everything squared, the lid down in one motion. Then he stood there, at the table's edge, in the light, with nothing on his face he hadn't put there on purpose, and Manav looked at his son and at the torn grey-blue thing on the table between them and understood that this was what the rooftop question had really been about, all those months ago. *Are you the one who takes, or the one who watches?* Two masks had just looked at each other across a kitchen, and both of them were ten years old, and one of them had been seven when it was commissioned.

"The rest of that night," Manav began, because a father has to offer, "you don't ever have to—"

"I'm not telling you the rest," the boy said. "Some of it is mine." A breath, and the voice his mother used for jokes that were not: "You don't have to tell me yours either. Didi said don't tell. You didn't tell. That was right. Now I've told. We're even."

"Is it over now?" Manav asked — the boy's own question, handed back across the table, because he had no other honest thing in him.

The boy considered it, the way one considers a claim.

"No," he said. "But it's in the open, Papa. Things in the open, you can work with."

His own line, in his own child's mouth, itemized and returned.

"Go to bed, beta," Manav said. "It's four in the morning."

"Five past," the boy said, who had never once in four years been told the time.

---

The corridor took him with his tea refusal and his squared shoulders, and at the last the door shut the way doors shut in houses where closing doors quietly is a studied skill, and the lock did what it did.

On the table, in the light, the phone lit once, numbered:

*C-14. Your son locks the door at night. Good.*

He read it twice, the way you check a receipt for what it doesn't say. It had both of the era's readings, care and verdict, and the era kept no register of which, and neither — he understood, sitting where the file had sat, where the season's pages had sat, where the bandages had been laid out in thirds — neither did the thing that had sent it, and that was the whole of its confession, and it had made it four years ago in four words and never repeated itself.

He put the phone face-down on the table.

Nothing in the world had moved. The crew was dust. The case was a closed cupboard. The name convicted no one and never would — it had no strategic value at all, and it had changed the whole of his life, and that was the arithmetic, and for once he did not check it twice.

The television kept the house sounding like itself. Down the hall his son slept behind a locked door, on duty, and loved, and Manav Sood sat on in the light of his own kitchen and kept, for the first time in four years, nobody's watch but the house's.
