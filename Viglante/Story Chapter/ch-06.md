# VIGILANTE
### Part One · The House — Chapter Six: The World Breaks

By April he knew the drop-house the way he had once known his own claim ledger: by its habits. A double-storey behind the marble market, a scooter repair shop in front, men who came on Tuesdays and Saturdays with bundles that were never marble. Five months of Farid's bench and the paan counter and the two kinds of walk had taught him the room's grammar — which light meant counting, which silence meant collection, which scooter meant the week's settle. One of the runners moved like the underpass had moved, that night in January; he could not prove it; he kept the page.

Then, on a Thursday in April, at nine in the evening, two men came to the drop-house in a hurry, with loot still warm in its bags — jewellery in a pillow cover, a laptop, a tin that rang — and left again in four minutes, empty-handed and fast, going back the way they had come.

No one goes back for a second trip unless the first one is unfinished. No one leaves a house mid-job unless the house is holding something they intend to return to.

He followed them at the distance the winter had taught him, across two sectors, to a corner house in Sector 14 with a marigold wire on the gate and a child's bicycle lying where a child had left it, trusting.

The front door stood ajar. Second trip. The lights were on in the family room, and a television was playing.

He came to the window to count the men, and that was the end of counting.

---

An old man lay on the floor between the sofa and the show cabinet, breathing wrong, one wrist under him at the angle wrists do not choose. In the kitchen doorway a woman was sitting against the frame with her hands behind her, tied at the wrists with her own dupatta, awake, entirely awake, her eyes doing all the screaming a house like that allows. Somewhere deeper in the flat a baby was crying with the fury of the unanswered. A man he had not yet catalogued moved through the bedroom doorway with a folded bedsheet full of something, unhurried, itemized, patient — a system at work — and on the television a rerun played at low volume and the canned laughter rose at nothing, tinny, in a house with tied hands in it.

He had no memory of that night. He had been elsewhere; his absence had been the one perfect alibi of his life. The freeze gave him the attendance he had missed. Standing at a stranger's window with his hand on the sill, his mind — patient as a clerk, three years patient — finally produced his own night in full: the hallway, the geyser he had never fixed, the almirah the file said, that he had never once allowed himself to see, the sounds given to a boy's imagination as television. He stood at the glass and attended, three years late, his own house, and his body did what bodies do at the scene of their worst file.

It stopped.

The watch on his wrist said ninety seconds. The watch was wrong; ninety seconds is a filing; this was not. What it was, was the laugh track rising and the baby unanswered and the old man's breathing going wronger, and the men moving, unhurried, itemized, through a family's life — and then, because a body can only die once and he had not died at the window, his legs came back to him all pins and needles, like a loan recalled at the worst hour, and he went through the ajar door.

The first minute worked. It was all he had, and it was enough for one man: the bundle-carrier came out of the bedroom doorway into the hallway and Manav took him there, weight forward, inside the arms, the shoulders doing their arithmetic, and the man went down into the wall and the wall participated. The second came with the iron rod from behind the door, and the rod missed and the man did not, and there was a bad half-minute in the family room with the television laughing and the old man on the floor between them like a dropped parcel, and then the rod man saw what he was fighting — a mask, in a house he was robbing, at the hour when masks do not come — and did the street's arithmetic and went out through the door into his own night.

The first man was under Manav, halfway folded, breathing in pieces. He was the catch. He was five months of bench and three years of night, whole, catchable, the first thread of everything, in the hand at last.

In the kitchen doorway the woman's eyes had found her father on the floor, and the old man had found her, and said, to the ceiling, to the mask, to whoever had come — "Bitiya. Bitiya."

Manav opened the hand.

The man under it went out on all fours and then not on all fours, and was gone, and Manav knelt by the old man with his sleeve already taking the blood, and did what hands do. The wrist was wrong. The head had a cut with plenty behind it. The woman got her hands free by herself, pulling at the knot with her teeth, the way people do when the help has gone out of the house, and she reached her father and put her own hands on his face, and the baby cried on, and the television laughed on, and the rod was on the floor, and nobody in that room looked at the mask again, because the hurt had made the mask furniture.

He rang three bells before one opened. The city comes. It is never early. A man in a ganji stood in the first light of his door and saw across the lane a masked figure in a lit doorway with an old man in it, and did not scream, because the old man mattered more, and Manav gave him the number of the house and the two words — *ambulance, police* — and was down the lane before the word police had finished its sentence.

He walked home through a city that did not yet know anything, with another man's blood drying at his wrist, and his hands, when he looked at them under a streetlight, were conducting their own private weather.

---

At four in the morning he sat on the bathroom floor with the mask in both hands, and the sentence went around in him like water in a drained pipe: *enough.* Total loss. No salvage. A man who freezes at a window is not a weapon; a man who opens the hand is not a hunter; and the night had shown him, in the end, the only office he was fit for — arriving after, holding a stranger's head while the city took its time.

It was the sanest sentence he had thought in three years, and that was what frightened him of it.

He was still holding the mask, composing the resignation, when the sky went grey.

---

The boy turned the volume up himself.

That was the first fact of the morning: Manav in the kitchen doorway with the resignation still in his chest, and Aarav on the sofa turning the volume up with the remote in his fist, and on the television, on every channel, a wedding.

The Grand Utsav banquet hall, Vinayak Vihar. Garlands, a mandap, four hundred guests, the official videographer, and a hundred phones held up the way phones are held up now — as witnesses in advance. The footage, which everyone in the country would own by evening, showed the pheras at 11:52 the previous night: the couple circling the fire, the pundit's hand rising, and then — between one frame and the next — the groom is, and is not. No smoke. No light. No sound, except every flame in the hall leaning one way for a moment, like air being used. The garlands fall into the clothes. The clothes fall like a coat off a hook whose hook has been removed.

The bride, twenty-six, a chartered accountant, was holding his hand at the time, and for a week afterward, until the interviews stopped letting her, she would say it was like a word on the tip of the tongue.

The anchors had no word yet. A police spokesman, at nine, used the phrase *unexplained removal of a person*, badly, twice, trying to keep his voice level, and the city — which needed the phrase more than the police did — kept it: Removals. By evening someone had captioned the loop with a joke about the wedding's return policy — *receipt attached* — and the city, which needed the word more than the joke, kept that too.

On the sofa, in the morning light, the boy watched the loop the way Manav watched claims. Entirely.

"Did he do something?" Aarav said.

"Nobody knows yet," Manav said, the father's answer, the only true one.

"That's not the same as no," the boy said, to the screen, and turned the volume up one more number.

The resignation in Manav's chest did not survive the morning. Not because the world breaking was a reason. Because of what came four days later, on the Thursday, which changed the terms of everything, including his.

---

The settlement had been signed in four copies, the way settlements are, nine years ago: a young man, a road, a cyclist who had been somebody's whole income. Three of the copies were accounted for — a lawyer's safe, a cupboard, a court's dead file. The fourth arrived by courier at the gate of a news channel, with an affidavit from a witness who had been paid, and had spent the money, and had kept his copy, and now, in the new weather, could not sleep near it.

The city understood the terms that day, all at once, the way herds understand. It was not the vanishing that was new. It was the accounting. Whatever kept the list had *access* — to settlements, to buried files, to the things that had been paid, patiently, over years, to become un-true. The law had been paid. The list had not.

Four more by the weekend: a ration officer, two moneylenders, a school principal. The early receipts were all men the city had quietly hated for years, and the city fell for the list the way crowds fall for anyone who hates what they hate. By Friday the panels had a theory with a face in it: the masked man. Sightings since winter; Removals since spring. *A coincidence is a rumor that can walk,* someone said on a panel, *and this one is running.* The forwards had found their scripture and were typing it in capital letters: HE IS OUT THERE. HE SEES. HE IS TAKING THEM HOME.

Manav read the scripture at one in the morning at the kitchen table and could not correct a word of it. He alone in the city held the ledger that said otherwise — the Kishanganj financier had gone in January, a full month before any mask walked into Block C — and he could never sign the correction, because the claim could only be filed by a man, and he was a mask. The night had given him somewhere to hide, and somewhere to hide, it turned out, was also somewhere to be silenced.

But it was the other clause of the new terms that unresigned him.

The takers did not explain. The receipt said *what*, and it said it in other people's documents, and it never once said *why* — never which door, never which night, never the shape of the family behind the settlement. If the list took his four men, the why of his own night would go with them, unexplained forever, on both ends.

He had spent three years hunting four men. He was now hunting them against a clock whose face he could not read, run by something that did not file.

---

That morning, at breakfast, the liturgy ran backward. It was the boy who asked first.

"Did you sleep?"

"Yes," Manav said — one lie of four, delivered smoothly — and the boy nodded, the way one nods when a claim has been assessed and passed, and asked the other three questions in their order, and got their answers, and went to the van with his bag, and did not look at the marigold wire on the gate, where the bicycle of Sector 14 still lay in his mind and would not lie down.

After the van had gone, Manav took the mask out of the office bag, out from between the two closed files where it had lived since winter, and put it in his jacket pocket. Carried now, not filed. The work had outgrown one man's eyes, and the night had more in it than men, and the world was going to need, badly, a man with a file.

At night he stood in the doorway of the hall, where his son slept inside the noise, and the noise had a new religion in it now, playing at the volume of memory.

He stood there the way fathers stand — on duty.
