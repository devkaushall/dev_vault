# VIGILANTE
### Part One · The House — Chapter Five: The Strain

In insurance, March is the month the year confesses: every file must close, every story be audited at once. The branch drank chai like a hospital. And for the first time in two years, the season worked for him — the office was handing out alibis for free.

The night had made him a better assessor; that was the joke nobody at the branch was in on. A man who spends three hours at a dark paan counter learning what a walk says about a man cannot help reading the walk of a man across a desk. His files closed like taps turned off. In the year he had begun breaking into the night, the office gave him *exceeds expectations*, and he signed the form in the same hand that had learned, by lamplight, to hold a needle steady.

The shoulder had met a wall in the dark, on a night the office had no knowledge of and the wall has never reported. At breakfast the plum came up out of his collar like a second head.

"Rough day at the office," he said.

It was the new sentence, the small one, the working one. The stairs had been for the face. The office took the body's shifts.

"The office is dangerous this year," Aarav said, to his cereal, in the exact voice his mother had used for jokes that were not.

---

Rakesh Sethi came to the flat on a Sunday with a cricket kit the size of luggage, and stood in the door not coming in, not leaving — a man at the exact center of what he could carry. He talked cricket the way other men talk about the weather at funerals.

"Sood," he said once, and for a moment the tact opened half an inch, like a door on a chain — and shut. "The handle has anti-scuff tape on it. The boys all want it."

"Thank you, Sethi uncle," Aarav said, and carried the kit into his own room — the room that was for things now, not for sleeping — and shut the door on it.

Sethi watched the door shut and did something with his face that had no name in cricket. Then he talked eight more minutes of cricket, standing, and left with the emptiness of a man who had not said the thing he had come to say, and Manav closed the door and could not have said what the thing was, and stood a while with his instrument swinging slowly, pointing nowhere his files went.

---

On the ninth of March she would have been fifteen. They went after school to the sweet shop near the old market — the one that still made the thing she had always ordered, the sweet the whole family could name in one word and no longer said.

Aarav ordered it in a voice that had been rehearsed somewhere private, and they stood at the counter over it with two spoons and no appetite, and the shop's radio played film songs for somebody else's daughter.

"Didi would be taller than me now," Aarav said, to the counter.

He had passed her in the winter. Neither of them had mentioned it. The frozen thirteen, and the boy still growing — the arithmetic of the surviving is not in any actuarial table. Manav put his hand on the back of his son's neck, and the hand said everything it had, which was nothing, and the boy let it stand there, and that was the birthday.

---

The four answers came in the order the four questions came. They had begun coming too smoothly — the way claims do when a story has been through the machine twice. He heard it the way he heard everything now, at forty paces, and he pointed the hearing away from home, and the hearing would not stay pointed.

"You are spending sleep somewhere," Ustad said at the wraps, not a question, the landing rule holding.

Manav skipped rope like a man arguing with his own shadow, and the shadow was winning on points.

---

He went out at eleven with the office bag, as he had gone out at eleven all winter. The society had a fact about him now — *Sood saab walks at night* — and facts like that do a man's work for him; grief is the best ID there is. The thief of Block C had been six feet, and then six-two, and nobody looks up at a five-eight man when a six-two man is on file. He had built that shelter himself, one lie at a chain door, and it held him like a coat.

"Taking your walk," the boy said from the sofa, not a question either, to the ceiling, the television's colors going over his face.

"Ten minutes," Manav said, which was the lie's name for three hours.

He pulled the door shut and was two steps down the landing when the sounds came, inside: the sofa giving up a boy, the feet on the floor, and then the bolts. Not fumbled. Thrown. Chain, top, lower — three small sounds, each one landing like a signature — and then the feet going back, and the sofa taking the boy again.

He stood on the landing with his hand on the rail.

Fear fumbles a bolt. Practice doesn't.

He had explanations, all of them on file: the thief scare, the age, the dark, the counselor's phrase from the parent-teacher meeting, *a healthy vigilance*. He took them out one by one in the stairwell dark, and none of them explained the manner of the sounds. It was the same smoothness as the four answers. He set the two facts down side by side on the rail of his mind — and then, quickly, before they could meet, because facts that meet have children, he put them away in separate drawers, and went down the stairs, and the night took him in like family.

The bench kept him. Farid poured without being asked. The paan counter kept its two kinds of walk, and the runners ran, and he counted, because counting was the trade, and the count went into the book that lived where the mask lived, between two closed files.

---

He came home at ten to three and put his key in, and the key told him what the boy had left: everything on.

He knocked. Softly. A father's knock, two knuckles.

The television's light lay along the bottom of the door. A shadow moved in it. The chain came off first — the pause, the eye at the gap, the check, old habit now at three in the morning — and then all of it, and the door opened on a boy with sleep standing in his eyes like weather.

"I fell asleep on the sofa," the boy said, his cover story, and went back to the sofa, and the television said what it always said.

Manav stood in his own doorway like a stranger who had been let in out of pity.

It occurred to him only then, two years in, that the sofa was the room with the door in it. The thought stood there in the hall and did not go anywhere. He filed it with the click.

He pulled the blanket up over his son. In sleep the boy's fist was closed around the television remote, holding it the way his mother had held things in her fist when she was scolding somebody she loved.

He stood over the two of them, the boy and the noise, and did not do any arithmetic at all.
