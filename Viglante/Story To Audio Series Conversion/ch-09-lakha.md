# VIGILANTE — Audio Series
## Part Two · The System
### Episode 9: "Lakha"

*Narration script · single narrator · est. runtime ~14–15 min (~2,300 words)*
*A patient, procedural episode that turns, at the end, toward a rooftop. Keep the surveillance passages steady and low — the horror is that Lakha is sympathetic, not that he's a monster. The mandi Taking: report it flat and hushed, like the mandi itself refusing to gossip. End on the parapet with stillness. "He had been waiting three years" is a door left open, not a slam.*

---

The mandi called him by the name the phone had given. And the name checked out. And that was the survey.

It had taken two nights to find the collection yard. The milk came into the old quarter at 2:40, in tankers, and left again in tempos by four. And the tempo on the Bhagwanpura routes was driven by a man the loaders called Lakha. Nobody called him anything else. His mother's house, wherever it was, would have called him something different. The mandi never learned it. He never offered.

Manav bought a bicycle. A Hero. Second-hand. The era's cheapest invisibility — because no forty-four-year-old clerk on a cycle at three in the morning has ever been seen by anyone. And for eleven nights, he learned the route the way he'd once learned claims. By its habits. Its pauses. Its lies.

*(pause)*

The route had a liturgy to it. Collection at 2:40. First run at four. And in between, forty minutes at the wheel — and the man did not sleep in those forty minutes. He sat in the cab of the tempo with his eyes open, and watched the mandi wake up. And the mandi dogs came to his window, one at a time, and he fed them from a bag he'd bought with his own money — before he ate anything himself.

Tuesdays, he drove the far side of the ring road. And slowed, going past a lane where bougainvillea grew over a green gate. And did not stop. And had not stopped, in three years. Tuesdays, he also went to the temple near the timber market. And prayed — if that was the word — with his eyes open the whole time. Watching the door. Watching the sky through the door. Just watching.

The man did not sleep inside. He slept at the wheel. In the open. Under whatever the night was doing. Once, near dawn, he slept for twenty minutes flat on his back on the tempo's hood, in the yard, with the dogs lying around him like unpaid staff. And Manav — thirty metres off, in the shadow of the weighbridge — wrote it down, in the ledger inside his head.

*The guilty sleep outside. They think walls are where it happens.*

*(pause)*

He'd expected — if he was honest, and the file under the file was where he kept his honesty — he'd expected to find either a monster, or a nothing.

Instead, he found a man doing the exact things he himself did. Feeding something that could never testify. Keeping hours nobody had asked him to keep. Watching a home he could not enter. On a schedule. On Tuesdays.

*Findings. The subject is not fleeing justice. He is already serving a sentence. Custody would only be a transfer.*

He wrote that in the ledger. And then — because findings have consequences — he took out the police file of his own house, and read the inventory, until the anger came back.

It came slower every year now. He had to prime it. The way you prime a dry pump.

*(short pause)*

On the fifth night, the phone lit once. Numbered.

*C-6. Two lengths further back. He counts faces.*

He counts faces. Of course he counted faces. Three years of counting them.

Manav dropped back two lengths. And the gift, as always, had two edges to it. Protection, for the instrument. And a small, cold seminar — on exactly how far away the instrument's keeper could see.

The app sent nothing else. All eleven nights. He noted that, too. It had given him the name, and then stood aside. As if the gift were the whole of its interest, and the *using* was the point.

---

The plan, when it was ready, was a counter.

It was — he was fairly sure — the first plan of its kind in the history of the city's night. Three weeks of being a face. The tea stall at the mandi gate, three in the morning, the same man, the same kurta, night after night, until the loaders stopped noticing him, and Lakha's count of faces absorbed him as furniture. And then, one night — the slide over onto the next stool. And *these runs are keeping you thin, bhai.* And the slow, bureaucratic miracle of a stranger becoming a person, and a person becoming someone you tell things to.

He'd done it for twenty-one years, across desks. The insured tell the assessor everything, eventually. Because the assessor is the only one who ever asks — in a voice with no weather in it.

That was the plan. The claims assessor's patience. Not the mask's.

*(pause)*

In July, the boy turned ten. One breath for the candles. The wish left unsaid, the way wishes are. Sethi's envelope arrived on the birthday itself — punctual, too much, *from the floor, ha ha.* The floor had now done this three years running. And the cricket kit stood in the room where things lived. Used twice.

Manav watched his son arrive at double digits, over a small cake, in a flat where the television kept its vigil. And across the city, a man was sleeping at a wheel with his eyes open. And both facts lived in him now. Side by side. In separate drawers. Not meeting.

The office got his mornings that summer, the way it had always got them. Which was the whole trick. A man cannot be suspected of a night — if his mornings are immaculate.

And Farid — Farid, whose stall sat at the edge of the mandi like a fixed star — understood without being told. He'd watched the man on the cycle for four nights before the kettle said anything about it. And what the kettle said was: two cups. One at the bench. One at the far end. At watching distance.

Nobody discussed it. The night city had taken a side. Or declined to. Which, in that city, came to the same side.

---

On the ninth night, the list came to the mandi.

Manav arrived at ten past three, to a silence he had never once heard in that place — a hundred years of gossip, gone quiet. The loaders standing in a ring, around a patch of ground near the weighbridge. Where nothing was.

No smoke. No light. No sound — the men said, afterward. Asked by nobody. Telling nobody. Between one load and the next.

A weighbridge clerk, who'd been doctoring his weights for years — the mandi knew, the whole mandi knew — and the mandi had gossiped about everything under its awnings for a century. And it did not gossip about this. A shoe stood on the empty patch. Right-side up. Precise.

The dogs would not go near it.

*(pause)*

Lakha had been parked closest. Lakha had seen.

The tempo sat where it sat. And in its window, the man's face was the face of a dropped claim. He did not do the four o'clock run. He did the Tuesday drive-past — on a Thursday. Twice. Slow. The bougainvillea going by. And then he fed the dogs the bread he had not touched himself. And sat on the hood, and looked at the sky over the old quarter for a long time. With his eyes open. The way he prayed.

A week later, the receipt came for the clerk. In the manner of receipts. A weighbridge book. Three years of false grams. Delivered to a newspaper, by nobody. And the mandi read about its own silence in the papers. And said nothing about that either.

*(pause)*

That night, the app sent nothing.

And Manav understood something. Small, and very large. Standing in the yard at watching distance, with the two-dog night going on around him.

It did not correct the list. *Nobody* corrected the list. Whatever its ledgers held — whatever its minutes were worth — the thing at the mandi had happened, right in front of its instruments. Like weather.

---

The eleventh night, Lakha's route ran ragged. And turned — at twenty to four — away from the mandi. Out past the last of the numbered sectors. To a tower the era had stopped building, half-finished.

Sixteen floors of intention, standing there in its scaffolding at the edge of the ring road, where the money had gotten superstitious. The kind of place a man goes up — when the decision has already been made, and the legs are only informing the body.

The tempo parked at the site gate. The man went in through the shutter, with a bottle, on foot. Unhurried. At the end of everything.

Manav stood at the bottom of the stairwell, the mask in his fist. And did the last audit.

The plan — the stall, the tea, three weeks of being a face — was a plan for a man with time. The man on these stairs had a morning in him somewhere. A bus. A border. And eleven nights of watching said the same thing. *Now, or never.* And the mask was the only language that stops a running man in the dark.

A confession to a mask would stand up in no court in the world.

He went up anyway.

The court had never been the point. The *why* was. And there was one more filing — made honestly, in the file under the file, where he kept himself. That a part of him had been waiting, since the underpass. Since the ribs. Since the seventy rupees of tea. Since the night the dark produced company. Waiting — to be the thing the night saw. Instead of the thing it happened to.

*(pause)*

The stairwell was pitch dark. Then grey. Then grey, with wind in it. He came up the last flight at ten past four, and stepped out onto the gravel. And the city lay away to the dark edge of itself, under the sodium sky.

And the man was standing at the parapet. Hands open. A bottle set down, carefully, at his feet. Straight-backed. The way men stand for inspections.

He turned. He did not run.

He had been waiting three years.
