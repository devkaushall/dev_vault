# VIGILANTE — Audio Series
## Part Two · The System
### Episode 7: "CORRECTIONS"

*Narration script · single narrator · est. runtime ~15 min (~2,380 words)*
*Part Two's opener — the register shifts slightly: the world is bigger, louder, a little colder. The school-mob sequence is a countdown; keep it taut, no wasted air. The app's messages should be read differently from the narration — flatter, clipped, no warmth, like a machine that's right. The final beat is quiet dread, not comfort.*

---

By May, the whole city had started wearing his mask.

Not the cloth — nobody else had cloth. The manners. The sudden, loud, public goodness of a town that had just found out it was being graded. Prayers said where prayers could be seen. Donations announced at volume. Old debts settled by men who wanted the settling witnessed. The rickshaw pullers had a joke with no laugh in it — *bhagwan bhi line mein hai.* God's in the queue too. And the forwards had found a word for what the mobs were doing. They called it a correction.

Meanwhile, half the old night trade had gone quiet. Men who worked in the dark had discovered that the dark had management now. The betting room's Tuesdays had thinned out. The runners ran less. The drop-house behind the marble market kept its lights on longer — counting nothing.

The world hadn't gotten kinder. Violence had just changed its costume. From balaclavas, to processions. And the money was learning to follow.

*(pause)*

Manav's nights had split in two. The old hunt — the network, the fence, the four men — ran on its rails. Slower now. Racing a clock whose face he couldn't read. And the new weather ran along beside it. He'd started, without deciding to, a second ledger.

The mobs were not spontaneous. His count said so. Three corrections in three weeks — and the same grey shirt in the crowd each time. Same scooter. Arriving early. Leaving paid. A mob was becoming a service that somebody purchased. And he followed the service provider the way he'd once followed a blue scooter with a taped mirror. Because a pattern is a door — even when you don't yet know the room.

The fourth mob was at a school.

---

Government senior secondary, Sector 19. Twenty to eight, a May evening. Results season.

Two hundred men, by his count. Growing at the rate rumours grow. And at the gate, sitting on the ground with his back against the bars, chalk still on his fingers — a chemistry teacher. Upadhyay-ji. He'd taught three generations of the district their periodic tables. And now, by the light of a hundred phone torches, he was guilty.

The receipt was a photograph. Forwarded, and forwarded, and forwarded. A handwritten page, supposedly in his own hand, pricing the chemistry board paper at eight hundred rupees a student. It had arrived from nowhere in particular — which is exactly where the era's most trusted documents came from. Nobody had seen an original. Nobody needed one.

The list had taught the city that the paper always comes *after*. And the city had improved on the lesson. Why wait for heaven's audit — when a correction could be held tonight, by hand, at the gate of the guilty?

Upadhyay-ji was saying his own name, to whoever would hold it. And the front rank of the mob — young men, lathis, tilak marks on their foreheads, a whole cohort of the publicly good — had started the small tidal movements a crowd makes just before it closes in.

*(pause)*

Manav stood in the dark by the paan stall, across the road. Unmasked. And did the arithmetic. And the arithmetic came out wrong. Both ways.

Go in as a man — and he was one body, against two hundred. And a man with a face, on top of that.

Go in as the mask — and this was the trap his own winter had built, one lie at a chain door at a time — the mask would arrive like scripture. HE IS OUT THERE. HE SEES. The mob would freeze. The mob would part. The mob would look from the mask, to the teacher, and draw the only conclusion the era allowed. The mask had come for Upadhyay-ji. They wouldn't fight him. They would *help*. They'd push the teacher forward. Offer him up. Stand back to witness the judgment. And if the mask refused to judge — refused out loud, said *this man is innocent*, in a stranger's cloth —

He could see the whole machine of it. Clear as a claim diagram. His myth had no vocabulary for *not guilty*. The mask could not save the man.

The mask could only convict him.

*(pause)*

His hand was already going to his pocket — the mask was carried now, not filed, and carried is closer to worn — when the phone lit up. In the same hand.

An app he had never installed.

White screen. Small grey type. No logo. No greeting. No name — except the one under the icon. Which was the era's own word for a beating.

CORRECTIONS.

*C-1. The mask will convict him. They will offer him to you. Grey shirt, scooter, by the paan counter — paid to lead this. Ask him, in front of them, what he was paid. He runs. Ninety seconds.*

He read it twice. It took three seconds.

Across the road, the crowd's tide had started its closing run. He had eighty-seven seconds left, and no theory at all of where that message had come from. And twenty-one years at a counter had taught him the two kinds of angry man. The one who wants the thing. And the one who wants to be *watched*. The second kind leads mobs.

A mob is a sentence looking for its verb.

He crossed the road as a man. Office walk. Office shirt. A face like a Wednesday. And he walked straight into the middle of the crowd, as though he'd been called up to a counter — because a crowd will always make room for a man who behaves like he has business there. Nobody wants to be the one who finds out what the business is.

"Bhai!" One voice. An office voice. A *next, please* voice. "The grey shirt! On the scooter!" — and every head turned, because the sentence had just found its verb — "Ask him what he was paid! He's been paid to be here! Ask him! Ask him, in front of everyone!"

*(pause)*

A mob is a sentence looking for its verb. And this one had just been handed a better verb than *beat the teacher*.

Two hundred men turned, as one single organism, toward the paan counter. Where a man in a grey shirt was already — before the sentence had even finished — reaching for the handle of his scooter.

He ran.

That was the proof. No innocent man on a scooter leaves a riot he's organized — at a jog, sideways, before the question's even finished being asked. But a paid man does. Because a paid man's job has a clause in it about exactly this.

And the mob watched him go. And the mob understood what it had just been told about itself. And a mob cannot bear to know what it costs.

The circle broke into arithmetic. The producer was gone — and the theatre went looking for the exits. Two colleagues of Upadhyay-ji's came over the gate like men suddenly remembering their profession, and took the teacher under the arms — chalk and all — back into the dark of the school. Where a headmaster was already on the phone to somebody's uncle. Who was a magistrate.

The watch on Manav's wrist said ninety seconds.

This time, the watch was right.

---

The phone lit again in his pocket, on the walk home. At the corner where the streetlights give up.

*C-2. The receipt was drawn by hand, cut from a register. The register is under the quilts in the boot of a white Santro, TG-3, parked behind the school. It belongs to a parent whose son has failed twice. The police will find it at 6:40 tomorrow, if someone tells them where. Anyone can call.*

*Anyone can call.*

He stood under the dead streetlight and understood that that one sentence was the sender's entire character. In three words. It did not *do* things. It corrected. And it left the verb to him.

*(pause)*

He found a PCO in the old quarter. One of the last of them — a cabinet full of black telephones, run by a man too stubborn to die politely. And he called the police control room. From a public phone. On a public street. A citizen's voice, with no name it needed. And he gave them the Santro. The quilts. The register. The school.

Then he went home, and didn't sleep. And at 6:40 in the morning, his phone lit up with the news. The way clocks do.

The register had been found. The forger confessed by nine. Upadhyay-ji was cleared. A small item. Page five. No photograph.

The teacher, the papers said, would be back in his classroom on Monday. And had asked the reporters, politely, to leave his students alone. He'd started, someone wrote, marking in pencil.

---

At the kitchen table, that night, Manav audited the app. The way he'd audited everything since October.

It had no install date. It held no permissions — nothing for the microphone, nothing for the camera, nothing for data. It used no data. It had no size. In the whole ledger of the phone, it weighed nothing, and it had arrived never.

It was the first thing he'd ever held that declined, entirely, to exist on paper.

The era's mobs called their beatings corrections. And here was a stranger, speaking the era's own language, offering corrections for free. Timed to the second. Right about the minute. Right about the register. Right about the grey shirt. And asking for nothing.

Which was the most expensive thing anything had ever asked of him.

*(pause)*

He'd spent his whole life verifying claimants. And there's no such thing as a free surveyor. Every man, at every counter, is being paid by somebody to be there.

The finding was obvious. And he wrote it down, on a fresh page of the ledger that lived where the mask lived.

*Sources. One. CORRECTIONS. Unverified claimant. No face, no office, no address. Two corrections — both paid out to the minute. Motive: unstated.*

*Treat as adverse party.*

He underlined *adverse party*. Twice. And felt the night tilt a little — the way a file tilts, when a party with no name walks into the case.

The phone lit up. Once more. On the table. Face-up. Between him and the ledger.

*C-3. Your wrist. Ice it.*

*(pause)*

The wrist. The left one. The one that had caught a man in Sector 14, and held an old man's head. And had been quietly swelling since Thursday. All of it unmentioned. All of it unwitnessed. Known to no one in the world.

Including — until that exact moment — himself.

He sat for a long time with the phone face-up. Being cared for. By something with no face.

He did not fetch the ice. Obeying, he understood, would be the first payment. So he put the phone face-down, beside the ledger. And sat in the kitchen, with his bad wrist, and his two ledgers, and the beginning of a very long case.

And he did not do any arithmetic at all.

Because the arithmetic, for the first time in three years, was being done for him.
