# VIGILANTE — Audio Series
## Part Three · The Watchers
### Episode 14 (Part 2 of 2): "The Office-Wala" — Sethi

*Narration script · single narrator · est. runtime ~14 min (~2,040 words)*
*Second half of Chapter 14. The farewell party carries dark warmth and irony; the parking-lot confession is the human core — Sethi is ashamed, not evil, so keep his voice small and tired, never a confession-monologue for effect. Manav answers in the claims voice, not with heat. "It was from the floor, Sethi. It always was." should be almost gentle. End very quiet on "he began to count."*

*Recap line (optional, if aired standalone): Manav has walked the tip chain to its source — a ledger in a timber market that priced his own family's Diwali, and knew his Sunday. Only one man in his ordinary life is left at the top of it.*

---

The branch was being folded into the new campus, past the canal. And in the third week of December, the floor held its farewell for Rakesh Sethi. Twenty-six years. Voluntary retirement. His own next desk, a sentence in somebody's email. The floor was scattering like a Diwali. Manav caught the simile at the door, and did not carry it inside.

It had been the best year the branch had ever had. Renewals were up. The weather-adjacent circular had doubled the workload. Nobody at the party said the sentence that explained the bonus. And the bonus said it for them. Through the banquet wall, you could hear the Sin Audit firm's year-end do, singing.

Sethi had one more over-giving left in him. The whole evening had been arranged around it. He went down on one knee — the way you address a wicket — and gave Aarav a bat. Full size. English willow. Meant for a boy two years older, and two careers luckier.

"They say he'll grow into it," Sethi said. "They say that about everything. The bats. The boys."

Aarav — who'd used the kit twice in six years — said, "Thank you, Sethi uncle." And then: "The wickets are slow this winter, uncle." A sentence built perfectly out of nothing, delivered in his mother's exact neutral. And then he held the bat across his knees for the rest of the dinner. Upright. At rest. The way soldiers in films hold rifles.

And there was an envelope. *For the coaching. In the spring.* Too much money, aimed at a spring. Which was the only kind of too-much the man had left.

*(pause)*

A junior AVP toasted him. Twenty-six years — and the man never gossiped. Not one story, in twenty-six years, sahab. What was it — yoga? And the floor laughed. And Sethi laughed at nothing. The laugh that starts, and then thinks better of itself. And Manav — who'd last heard that laugh at four in the afternoon, in the second year of his grief — heard it land differently now.

The floor was toasting the containment as a virtue. They were drinking to the door he had held shut, with both hands. It was the truest tribute the man would ever get. And nobody at the tables knew they were giving it.

Tikku — thirty years at the tallies, the branch's other filing system — kept the lore warm by the chafing dishes. The March of the dengue, when Sethi's cousin sat a week in the waiting chair and did the returns. *A decent soul. Nobody logs a cousin. Why would you.* And then, lower, the phase. "Sahab had a phase, those years. The satta. The betting. And then, one Diwali — it was finished. The cousin cleared it. Family, after all." He said the last two words with the satisfaction of a man closing a file that had never once been opened.

Manav asked him what the debt had been. Roughly.

"Those years? Three and a half. Maybe four. Why, sahab — thinking of betting?"

"The odds at my office were never good," Manav said.

The floor's envelope for Sethi went round at eleven. Manav heard the number his own hand put into it — before he'd approved it — and let it stand. Over-payment. The office's oldest dialect of grief. He'd been speaking it for six years, without an accent.

---

The party thinned the way parties do. Into coats and promises. Outside, the fog had kept everyone's secrets all evening, and was ready to keep more. Sethi's WagonR stood at the far end of the lot — the car he'd kept all through the debt, and, more tellingly, through the clearing of it. The defrost working at the windshield, like an apology.

"Sood saab. One minute." A breath. "You'll think it's the whisky. It's not the whisky. The whisky is only helping it up the stairs."

*(pause)*

Six years ago, a man had stood in a doorway with a cricket kit — opening half an inch, like a door on a chain — and had talked eight minutes of a sport, and taken the unsaid thing home. Tonight, at the tail of the lot, with nothing left to carry it into, the door opened the whole way.

"That Friday," Sethi said. "The Friday before Diwali. Everyone was going to be somewhere. My cousin was buying." He looked at the fog while he said it. "Sood saab, in eleven years, that man never once put his hand in his pocket with me. That night, he bought. The good stuff. And the second round. And his hand never went anywhere. I thought — Mausi's boy has come into something. And a grateful man talks. You know that? A man will tell you anything, if you buy the second round. And I was grateful. And the debt was moving — I'd been promised — and I was light. And I told a story about my weekend."

The voice found its level. The way it did at appraisals.

"I told a story about my weekend, Sood saab. And your house was in it. The office all going away. Gupta to Jaipur. The Malhotras to the hills. And you. Six words."

He said them the way other men recite totals.

"*The whole block will be dark.*"

*(pause)*

"I've counted them since. You count everything, after. It's like stairs in the dark. And two weeks later, the book was closed. Twenty-two Fridays of debt. Gone. Nobody waives a debt like that, Sood saab. I did accounts before I did people — I know what a waiver is. A waiver is a transfer you haven't read." He looked at the windshield. The defrost. The small, cleared half-moon of it. "And I never asked. Six years. A man who asks has to go and live in the answer. So I held the door instead. It's the only heavy thing I ever lifted, at that office. It's why I never — you must have wondered — I never once asked you about that night."

"Ask me now," Manav said. "You're out of the office. Tonight, it's affordable."

Sethi looked at the fog the way you look at a form you've already decided to sign.

"Was it the story?"

"The story was true," Manav said. The claims voice. He heard it arrive, and let it. "The family was going to leave on the Saturday. Ira ran a fever, and they stayed. You told it right, Sethi. That was the problem with it. Nothing in that bar had any value — except that it was true." He watched the sentence do what true sentences do. "And nothing is waived. You did accounts before you did people. You know the entry. The drinks were the drawdown. Somebody drew against your story, the night you told it. And the debt that died — was the interest."

Sethi stood with that. And somewhere behind his face, a sum closed.

"Then the envelopes," he said. "The money. All those years. Sood saab, I only—"

"I know exactly what you only did."

*(pause)*

A pause with weather in it.

"It was from the floor, Sethi. It always was."

Sethi looked at him the way you look at a receipt you know is wrong, and choose — forever — not to correct. He nodded, once. And something in his shoulders was returned to him. Or taken. Finally. From outside the transaction, it wasn't possible to say which.

"Go home, Sethi," Manav said. "Sit very still."

He heard the era come out of his own mouth. And did not take it back. It was the only dialect the city had left, for mercy. And both of them heard the other thing in it, too — the thing the whole city heard, in sentences like that, that winter. And neither of them asked heaven to clarify.

The WagonR went into the fog at walking pace, its taillights becoming rumour. And Manav stood in the lot, with the four pages held against his chest. Six years ago, a man had stood in his doorway, at the exact centre of what he could carry. Tonight, he'd set it down. And the weight had not left the world. It had only finished changing owners. And the last owner was standing in a parking lot — doing the arithmetic he had forbidden himself, for four years.

---

The flat was cold, and smelled of the milk he'd left out. The bat stood inside the boy's door. Upright. On watch. On the kitchen table, the coaching envelope lay where Aarav had set it down. And Manav laid the four pages of the season beside it — where the file had sat, in October. And the table accepted them both, the way tables accept everything. As furniture.

The table was becoming the desk of a case that had no court left.

*(pause)*

Down the hall — small, and certain — the lock did what it did. He stood in the dark until the click had finished. The way you stand for a bell.

The phone stayed dark. It had stayed dark since the train. He took its inventory the way he took inventories now — at the end of things, against the morning. Chotu. Lakha. Zorawar. The state of his stomach, on a hill line. A hundred nights of openings. And not one number — not once — had ever come with a chair attached.

He sat where the file had sat.

And for the first time in four years, he began to count.
