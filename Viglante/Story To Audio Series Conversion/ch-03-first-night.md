# VIGILANTE — Audio Series
## Part One · The House
### Episode 3: "First Night"

*Narration script · single narrator · est. runtime ~15–16 min (~2,420 words)*
*Home stays low. The underpass is the first time this series lets violence in — keep it fast and ugly, no relish, then drop straight back to quiet. The page-seven news item at the end should be read flat, thrown away, like it doesn't matter. It does.*

---

The mask left the flat the way it had gone into the office bag. Quietly. In between other things.

One in the morning. The hall television still murmuring at its lowest number, the boy asleep inside the noise of it. And Manav stood in the doorway with his boots on and the bag over his shoulder, running the arithmetic he let himself run now. School van, seven-twenty. Office, nine-forty. The gym was shut on Sundays. A man could be back by four.

He'd told himself he was only going to *look* at the night. Not go into it. Just look.

*(short pause)*

Two lanes from home, in the dark between two parked scooters, kneeling like a man tying a shoelace — he put it on. For the street.

The city came at him slightly too wide. In stereo. Through eye-holes a fish would've wanted adjusted. His own breath came back warm off the cloth. The cold got in at the jaw. For the first hundred metres, the mask was the only thing he could think about. By the second hundred, the city had swallowed it — and swallowed him with it.

And that was the night's first lesson. Nobody was looking. The night had room.

---

Under the canal flyover, the vegetable market had shut down for the night. And into that empty space, a second market had set up.

Farid's cart, under a blue tarpaulin. A kettle. A transistor radio, playing old songs at the volume of memory. Truck drivers, on a short break from their routes. Two watchmen sharing a bench and a silence. A boy with bundles of newspapers, who ate three biscuits and paid for one cup of tea with the seriousness of a man closing a big deal.

The city's other shift. The one the daytime never counts. Doing its rounds.

Manav sat at the end of the bench, with his back to the tarpaulin wall. And became furniture.

Across the lane stood a paan counter that wasn't only a paan counter. A lit doorway. A staircase going up. Men going in with the walk of people who've already decided something — and coming out with a different walk.

Three weeks of reading his colleagues' burglary files had earned him that bench. There was a pattern, every Diwali week — blocks emptying out for the festival, flats getting hit in the first days of the break. It went back four years. And the office had a name for it, the way offices do — which is a code, in a column, meaning nothing to anyone actually in a position to do something about it.

So he'd taken the code and read the claims the way he'd once read those field diaries. As a map. Sixteen houses in four years. One colony after another, all the way around the ring road. And in a claim from last November — two streets from his own colony — a neighbour's statement that nobody had ever followed up. *A blue scooter, with a taped-up mirror. Seen twice, in the week before.*

He'd found a blue scooter with a taped mirror, parked outside this paan counter, on a Tuesday in January. And he'd been sitting on Farid's bench every second night since. Being a customer. Learning — in the language of the muscles in his own neck — how to hold still. Ninety seconds at a time. Nobody had taught him that. His neck had taught him.

And tonight, at half past one — the scooter had a rider.

---

He followed on foot. And did everything wrong. Gracefully. Like a man performing a dance he's only ever read about.

He crossed when the rider crossed. He stopped when the rider stopped. He checked his phone light twice, for a street that didn't need it. The rider walked the scooter for the first hundred metres, the way you do in the narrow lanes. And Manav held his distance, behind a shuttered egg-seller's stall. And the distance was honest.

And then the rider leaned the scooter against the wall — right where the flyover's darkness began. And went on into that darkness, on foot, with a wrapped bundle under his arm. And Manav followed him in.

And this — he would file it later, in the section for *findings* — this was the moment the claim went bad.

*(pause)*

The underpass, at that hour, is a place where the light has just agreed to give up.

He heard the man's footsteps stop. And then a voice. Unhurried. Saying a name into the dark. "Chotu. Chotu aa gaya." Chotu's here.

And the dark produced company.

There were three of them. And one of him. And the first minute went almost to plan. He got his arms up, the way the basement had taught him. He turned his shoulder, the way the basement had taught him. He could even hear Ustad's breath-count in his ear — paper forgetting, meat remembering.

The problem was the second minute.

Nobody trains you for the second minute. The mitt in the ribs he knew, from the basement. But the mitt *stops*. And the street doesn't. And the shot went into his left side like a letter opener, and took his legs right out from under him. And after that, the night was mostly geography. A boot. The taste of coins in his mouth. The ceiling of the flyover, very far away, ridged like a bedsheet.

One of them got a grip on the mask.

And he held onto it the way drowning men hold onto wood.

He couldn't have told you why. Three men on top of him, and every bit of arithmetic in the situation telling him to give up everything — wallet, phone, the shoes off his feet — and the one thing he would not let the night have was the cloth on his face. He curled around it. Around it and around his ribs. And took what there was to take, on his back and his arms.

And somewhere in the middle of it, he understood something. With the strange calm of a man reading his own claim file while it's still being written.

*They're beating a mask.*

Not a man. There's no name anywhere on a mask. No address. No society. No son. Whatever else the night was going to do to him — it could not do the one thing that actually mattered.

*(pause)*

And then, from the mouth of the underpass, at full parade volume — a kettle announced itself.

"Chai! Garam chai! Oye — thand hai, chai piyo—!"

Farid did not cross the road. He didn't run at anyone. He stood exactly where his cart stood, under his own lamp, and turned the darkness into a shopfront. Calling out. Pouring. Clinking cup against cup. Being *seen* — at industrial strength.

And three men whose entire line of work depends on the opposite of attention did their arithmetic. The street's arithmetic — older than the city itself. And they were gone. Taking nothing.

Because a beating interrupted is a debt uncollected. And debts like that are never forgotten. Only deferred.

The underpass got its echo back. And Farid's radio played on. At the volume of memory.

---

Farid cleaned him up at the back of the cart. With the water he'd been saving for tea — which Manav understood was a thing, and did not say so. He worked the way he poured. No comment. No hurry. Warm cloth, one pass, done. The lamp hissed. The ribs on the left had closed up like a fist, and were not going to be asked to help with anything for a while.

"You're not police," Farid said.

It wasn't a question. And it wasn't the start of a conversation. It was a line item.

"No," Manav said.

Farid nodded. Like some small accounting question had just settled itself. And poured.

At the end, Manav took out his wallet. Farid looked at it the way you look at a document written in a language you don't read.

"First chai is free," he said. "After that, you pay by sitting where I can see you."

Then he wiped down the counter where the blood had reached it — in the manner of a man who'd wiped worse, or knew he would. And he turned the radio up half a notch. And the night went back to its rounds.

*(pause)*

Manav walked home inside the city's *first* shift. Milk cans on a cycle. Bundles of newspapers at the society gate, warm as fresh bread. A scooter carrying two lives somewhere, early.

The audit came for him around the second kilometre. The way audits do. Politely. At an inconvenient hour.

*Findings — if a colleague had signed them. Subject proceeded on the basis of a scooter, a taped mirror, and a feeling. No corroboration. No second source. The accused is a runner for a betting room — not, on any evidence presented, a housebreaker. And the scooter may be any scooter. Claim rejected. Cause of loss: the claimant's own negligence.*

Any assessor in Tejgarh would have thrown it straight out. *He* would have thrown it out. He'd gone out with one loose thread and called it a case. And the thread had snapped back and opened his ribs.

And then, underneath the findings — in the space where an assessor writes down the part he knows but doesn't say:

The mask had worked. Beaten. Emptied out. Seventy rupees lighter, in tea. And nobody had asked his name. There was no claim number on him anywhere.

That fact sat under his ribs, on the side opposite the pain. And it kept him walking the last kilometre home.

---

The school van came at seven-twenty, with its two notes. He had the compress off, and the story on, by then.

"Stairs," he said, answering the boy's look. "The society stairs. Carrying the bucket up."

Aarav looked at the cheek. The way he looked at everything now. Completely.

"You and Mama always said the stairs," he said. And went in to the television, for his cereal.

*(pause)*

Manav stood in the kitchen a while after the van had gone.

Three months ago, at dinner, the boy hadn't been able to finish that sentence. *You and Mama always said the stairs—* and stopped. This morning he'd finished it. At the top of the stairs. With the bucket in it. And he'd paid something small to do it — you could hear the toll in how flat his voice went — and there was no box, in any of Manav's files, for what that meant.

He called the office and told Sethi about something he'd eaten. Sethi told him to take the week. Said *I'll handle the office* — the way he'd been handling the office for two years. And Manav lay down on his good side, and let the boy's television come through the wall. And slept four hours, without the arithmetic coming even once.

Which his body had arranged. Without asking him.

---

It was in the canteen, that week. On page seven. Squeezed between a wedding and a municipal notice.

FINANCIER MISSING FROM KISHANGANJ.

Police were looking into his debts, the brief said. His shop had been shuttered since Tuesday night. Neighbours thought he'd gone back to his village.

Nobody had complained.

Six lines. He read all six — because he read everything now. And filed them under nothing. And turned the page. And the page, the way pages do, just went on.

---

Friday, when the ribs had gone from a fist to a held breath, he was back on the bench. Half past one. Sitting where he could be seen.

Farid poured without being asked, and set the cup down, and said nothing. And the paan counter went on with its lit doorway and its two kinds of walk. And the night went on being roomy.

He had wanted the night to give him a man.

It had given him a bench instead.

So he sat. And drank slowly. And began — at the age of forty-four — to learn the trade he'd actually come here for.

Waiting.
