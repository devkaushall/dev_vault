## Part One · The House
### Episode 1: "Elsewhere"

*Narration script · single narrator · est. runtime ~15–16 min (~2,450 words)*
*Read close and quiet. This is a man talking to you at his kitchen table, not a trailer. Let the pauses do the work — the marked breaks are places to actually stop, not slow down.*

---

Here's the first thing you learn, working in insurance claims.

Everybody grieves differently. But everybody lies the same way.

And here's the second thing. The lie is never really about the money. Money is just the place where people keep their other things.

Manav Sood had been reading other people's losses across a desk for twenty-one years. The last eleven of them at Hansa Assurance — the branch in Arjun Nagar, here in Tejgarh. His cabin had a window that looked out on the car park, and a door that looked out on everybody's worst day. And he was good at it. Good the way quiet men are good at a job that's mostly listening. He could hear a fake receipt from across the room. He knew the difference between the silence that means grief, and the silence that means somebody's doing the math.

These days, though, the people he worked with had a small superstition about him.

Nobody offered him tea anymore.

*(pause)*

The lights went up in his colony — Vishwas Colony — in the third week of October. Same wiring as every year. Same decorators. The same coloured bulbs strung around the gate and the parking pillars. So that at night, the whole society looked like a place that had something to celebrate.

And then, around the fourth day, it would start to empty out. One family after another, leaving for relatives in the older parts of the city. The flats going dark, one by one, all across the week. Like a tide going out.

It happened every Diwali.

It had happened that year too.

*(pause)*

Two years now. Two years gone. And there were still certain sentences that could land on him with all of their original weight — dropping clean through everything he'd built on top of them.

He had become, in the language of his own profession, *careful*.

---

On the Monday, breakfast was eggs and the television.

The television was always on. Aarav kept it on the way some kids keep a nightlight — not really watching it, just living inside the sound of it. And a while ago Manav had stopped turning it down. There were things in that flat that had been decided without anybody ever holding a meeting about them. The volume of the television was one of them.

Somewhere in the middle of a cartoon, the boy laughed. Really laughed — suddenly, at nothing Manav could see. Like a door thrown open in a house that mostly stayed shut.

And Manav kept his back to him. Buttered a second paratha nobody had asked for. Because of what his own face did, when the laugh came.

That laugh was the one thing left in the flat that still worked at full power.

*(short pause)*

Ira's school shoes were still by the door.

For the first six months, he'd kept moving them. To the rack. To her room. Once, into a carton he hadn't been able to bring himself to seal. And then, at some point, he'd stopped. And they stayed. And now they were just — furniture. A house is allowed a few arrangements it can't explain.

At twenty past seven, the school van leaned on its horn. Same two notes as always.

Manav asked the four questions. Did you sleep. Tiffin. Homework. Water bottle. And he got back the four answers, in the same order the questions came in.

Not one of the eight was true.

But that was how the two of them said good morning now. And it held the morning up.

At the door, Aarav stepped around his sister's shoes without looking down at them. He'd been doing that for two years. And Manav had stopped noticing him do it.

Mostly.

---

That week, the desk had two fires on it. A flood the surveyor had stamped *act of God*. And a jeweller's burglary that smelled — right from the first page — like the jeweller himself.

He cleared the fires. He sent the flood back with questions. And he set the jeweller aside for a second reading.

Because every file, he'd learned in twenty-one years, has one page in it that's been rewritten. Find that page, and you've found the whole claim.

In two years, he had never once turned that instrument on the one file that was actually his.

The police had called it a burglary gone wrong. He'd read that phrase — *burglary gone wrong* — maybe four hundred times. The way you say a word over and over until it stops meaning anything. Wrong house. Wrong night. Robbery crew. Untraced.

*(pause)*

Rakesh Sethi came by at four. Hands in his pockets, doing his little impression of a man just happening to pass. He set an envelope down on the desk.

"Diwali," he said. "For the boy. From the floor."

The floor had never signed off on anything. And there was too much money in that envelope. There was too much money in it every single year.

On his way out Sethi laughed at nothing — a laugh that started, and then seemed to think better of itself.

It had been Sethi, that first year, who kept Manav's name off the list when the branch was cutting people. Sethi who'd said, *you've got enough on your plate, Sood saab — let me handle the office.* And he had handled the office ever since.

In two years, he had never once asked Manav about that night. Not a word. Not one *how are you* that leaned in too far.

Manav had always assumed that was just tact.

---

They ate dinner with a single cracker going off early, somewhere out in the lane. One bang. Then nothing.

Neither of them looked up. Not looking up was something they'd both gotten very good at.

The second bedroom stayed shut. Nobody had ever announced it. The door had just — stopped opening.

Inside there was a poster. A half-read library book. A chart of the solar system she'd gotten too bored to finish. And two years of nobody's dust.

Once a month, he'd go in with a cloth. He'd told himself he'd stop the day it stopped helping. It hadn't stopped helping. Whatever it was doing in there, it hadn't stopped.

In the top drawer of the cupboard, her purse still held the list for that weekend. Folded twice, in Meera's handwriting. *Gift for Bua. Ira's syrup. Charge the camera.* The little shopping list for a trip that nobody ever took.

He hadn't been able to move the purse.

And the list had outlived the whole reason it existed.

He hadn't been able to move that either.

---

At night, the arithmetic came. It came every night. Regular as a man showing up to collect a debt.

If the fever had broken two hours earlier — the four of them would have travelled on the Thursday. And the flat would have stood dark, and empty, and safe. And whoever came that night would have found exactly what the police said they'd come looking for. Nobody.

If the interview had been on the Tuesday. If he'd taken the six-twenty flight instead of the eight-forty. If he'd just — not gone.

There were about two thousand ways to run that sum. And every single one of them came out to the same word.

*Elsewhere.*

He had been elsewhere.

Not in a bar. Not with anyone. Not anywhere a person could confess to and feel better. Elsewhere the way a *good* man is elsewhere — in a hotel room, with an ironed shirt hung up for the morning, trying to make his family's life a little better.

And there's no penance shaped like that. You can't say sorry for a promotion.

*(pause)*

*Sunday evening. The airport. Her voice small, underneath all the announcements.*

*The fever's coming down, I think. Ninety-nine and a bit. And the geyser — switch it off before your bath, it trips. Honestly, Manav, you worry like it's your job. Love you, bye.*

And he'd said it back. *Love you, bye.* He'd said it the way you stamp a receipt without reading what's underneath your hand.

And then — because an announcement had swallowed whatever he said first — he'd added: *Haan, fine. Get some sleep. I'll call you after the meeting.*

He never called after the meeting.

*(short pause)*

*He'd put the phone on silent, because of the meeting in the morning. At eleven fifty-two, it lit up. And then it kept lighting up. The neighbour. The second neighbour. The society guard. Numbers he didn't even know. And he slept through all seventeen calls — the deep sleep of a man with a big interview the next day. The hotel phone didn't ring until half past six. A call for you, sir. And then a voice, explaining the world to him. Very, very slowly.*

*They served breakfast on the flight home. He had no memory of eating it. He only remembered the tray going away empty. And some part of him — the part that was still a claims officer, even then — quietly noting it down for the file. Subject was able to eat. Subject's hands still remembered how.*

*(pause)*

*Then the week at the hospital. Green paint on the walls. Crackers going off outside the window all four days, the city celebrating something.*

*Ira surfaced twice. Both times, the same sentence. The second time, slower. And both times, her eyes going past him — to the door.*

*Aarav ne kuch nahin dekha. Aarav didn't see anything.*

*The first time, he'd said — yes, beta, of course, he was asleep, he saw nothing. And she had looked at that door a moment longer. Then she'd gone back under.*

*And she didn't come up again.*

She'd have turned fifteen in March.

She had been thirteen for two years now.

*(pause)*

He had carried that sentence around as mercy. Because that was the kind of sister she was — the kind who takes the one breath she's been given, and spends it building a wall around her little brother's sleep.

He had filed it under *mercy*. And it was the one file he could never keep shut. Every night it opened itself again, quietly. Like a door in a draught.

The police report had said it too. *The minor male child was found asleep.* Asleep. Through all of it.

The file was certain.

The file was kind.

*(pause)*

Inspector Bhatt called every year. On the day itself. Not before, not after. Eleven in the morning, for four minutes. The two of them saying *we're still here* to each other, in the voices of men talking about the weather.

He'd retired eleven months after the case was closed. Voluntary, people said. Good man. Put in his years.

Eleven months. The number didn't mean anything. But it turned up anyway, every year, riding in on that phone call.

Back at the prayer meeting — the shok-sabha, in those days when the flat was full of people saying the wrong beautiful things — a big, heavyset officer Manav had never met before set a packet of biryani down in front of him. And then just stood over him while he ate. Not saying anything. Waving the other people off.

*Eat, beta. You have to eat.*

And Manav had thought — this is what kindness looks like when it's wearing a uniform. He'd thought it gratefully. He was so tired. And the biryani was warm.

---

The week went the way the weeks went.

And then it was Thursday.

Because it was on a Thursday night — two years and a few days after a Sunday — that the rest of it began.

*(pause)*

Aarav was lying on the sofa in the hall. Television on mute. Turning a Spider-Man figure over and over in his hands. One of the boots was missing. He'd gotten it in a trade at school, for two pens and a sticker, and right now it was the most valuable thing he owned.

The boy slept on the sofa these days. That had started in the first month — the second bedroom shut, his own room a room he mostly didn't sleep in. And Manav had just let it become the geography of the house. There were things in that flat decided without a meeting.

"Why does he wear it?" Aarav said. Not looking up. "The mask."

"So nobody knows who he is."

"Why can't nobody know?"

Manav took a breath, and tried to make it sound like nothing.

"Because if the wrong people found out who he really was — they couldn't get to him directly. He's too strong. So they'd go after the people he loves instead. They'd use them, to get to him."

Aarav thought about that. With the serious face — this new, grown-up serious face that had shown up the same week as everything else, and just never left. He stood the little figure up on his chest.

"Even his maa doesn't know?"

And Manav opened his mouth to say the easy thing. *Of course she knows — mothers know everything.*

And he found that the easy thing just — wasn't in the room.

The muted television threw its colours across the ceiling. And down the hall, in the dark, Ira's shoes stood by the door. Keeping their post.

"No," he said. "Not even her."

Aarav nodded. Slowly. Turned the figure over one more time, working the whole thing out.

"That's smart," he said, to the ceiling. "Otherwise they'd just go to his house."

"Yes."

"That's the smartest part."

And then, within a couple of minutes, he was asleep. Fast, and completely — the way he was always asleep within minutes.

And Manav sat there in the dark of the hall.

And he understood something. In two years of answering every hard question with the softest true thing he could find — this was the first time he had told his son how the world actually works.

And the world, the way he'd just described it, had a shape to it.

He was surprised how well that shape fit him.

He sat in the dark, and let himself feel it fit.

---

He didn't go to bed.

Some hours later he was in the kitchen. Just the little light over the stove on. And the photograph out — not the framed studio one. The other one. The last one with all four of them in it.

A selfie Ira had taken on some ordinary, nothing afternoon. Everybody half-ready to go somewhere. Meera caught mid-word. Aarav's thumb over one corner. And Ira's own laughing face filling up half the frame, laughing at something outside of it.

Nobody's looking at the camera.

It's the only photo of the four of them that's completely true.

*(pause)*

He ran the arithmetic one more time. Out of loyalty, more than anything. If the fever. If the six-twenty. If he just hadn't gone.

And somewhere in the middle of it — with no ceremony at all — the sums just stopped running backward.

He noticed the quiet they left behind. The way you notice a fan cutting out. The air still there. But changed.

For two years, every number he'd run had been facing yesterday.

Tonight, for the first time, the sum faced forward.

*If they're never found — then who finds them?*

The question didn't frighten him.

And that — that was the thing that frightened him.

*(pause)*

He stood for a long time with his hand flat on the cold steel of the counter.

Then he went into the bedroom, and took down from the top of the cupboard Meera's sewing box. The tin one. With the peeling little painting of a woman on the lid — the one she'd never thrown out, because, she used to say, the woman hadn't done anything wrong.

He carried it to the kitchen table. Set it down next to the photograph. And opened it.

Her scissors. Her measuring tape. The bent little pot of pins.

And the needle.

The needle was exactly where she'd left it. Still threaded. A length of grey-blue cotton through the eye of it. Like she'd only just stepped out of the room, and meant — when she came back — to finish whatever it was she'd been mending.

He didn't turn on the big light.

The small one was enough.

*[end of Episode One]*

## Part One · The House
### Episode 2: "The Body"

*Narration script · single narrator · est. runtime ~15–16 min (~2,480 words)*
*Same register as Episode One — close, quiet, unhurried. The basement scenes can pick up a little life; everything at home stays low. Marked breaks are real stops.*

---

In the morning, the sewing box went back up on top of the cupboard. Where it had always lived.

In the night, he'd reached for the last thing first. Reached for the mask — the way a child reaches for the toy and not the homework. But the mask could wait. It had already waited two years. What couldn't wait was older than the mask, and simpler.

Nothing in his life — not the school run, not the office, not the four questions at breakfast — had asked anything of his body in two years. And a man cannot go where he was planning to go on the body of a claims officer.

He understood that clearly. The way he understood everything in his work. Without any real hope that understanding it would help.

*(pause)*

The geyser tripped while he was showering. The way it tripped every single morning. And he stood there dripping in the passage and pushed the switch back down.

It had been tripping for two years. He'd never once had it looked at. The electrician was expensive — that was the story he told himself. And some part of him, the part that stamped documents for a living, knew the truth. If somebody had brought him that story across his desk, as a claim?

He would never have paid it.

At the office, Friday closed itself the way files close. A small signature. No sound.

---

Twenty-one years of surveys lived in the steel cupboard in his study. Field diaries. Cloth-bound, from back when the office still paid for cloth. Plastic, after that. A surveyor's handwriting — addresses, electricity meters, staircases, faces. And in twenty-one years, he had never once read them as anything other than work.

On Saturday night, he took them all down. And he read them as a map.

*(short pause)*

There was the bouncer's jaw, X-rayed — round and white, like a little moon. The pawnshop robbed by the owner's own son. The son was sixteen; the father wept, but he wept in numbers. The taxi driver beaten half to death over a forty-rupee fare, who'd sat at the police station holding his torn shirt closed at the collar — like a man holding a coat shut at a wedding.

Twenty-one years. And it turned out he'd been walking the underside of this city the whole time, carrying a lantern, calling it a job. And the underside had been waving back the whole time.

The entry he was looking for didn't stand out. He passed it twice before he stopped.

*Eleventh of March, 2011. Fire. New Model Boxing Club, Bhagwanpura. Cause: meter room, old wiring. Office instruction — negligence, reject the claim. I climbed up and saw the meter myself. Paid.*

The office had wanted that fire to be the club's own fault. They'd sent letters saying exactly that. And then — for reasons the file didn't bother to record — they'd sent a man who climbed the stairs to the meter room and looked with his own two eyes. And the money came through. And the club came back.

He set the diary down on the kitchen table, next to the cold tea. And he looked at that one word — *Bhagwanpura* — for a long time.

In twenty-one years, it was the only address where somebody had spent their whole life teaching other people how to get hit. And how to keep going.

---

Diwali came the way it came now. Which was straight at him.

The society's function happened at the gate, without them. It had happened without them for two years. The invitations kept getting politely slipped under the door, and he kept folding them into the drawer — the drawer where the society kept its faith that one day the two of them would come down.

On the night itself, Aarav asked for the small lights. Not crackers, he said. The boring kind.

And Manav heard his own *yes* come out before he'd even decided anything. The way it does, when a boy asks for one small thing after a long stretch of asking for nothing at all.

They lit six along the balcony railing. Down in the lanes, the city was already going off — gently, block by block. The week of her death, being celebrated right on schedule.

The boy watched the six little flames for a while. Then he watched the sky. Then he went inside, to the television — which was where he slept now.

And Manav sat on the balcony with his tea going cold, and he did not run the arithmetic. Because the arithmetic had a new job now. And the new job faced forward.

*(pause)*

Inspector Bhatt called on the day itself. At eleven. Exactly the way he'd called at eleven the year before.

For four minutes, they were two men discussing weather that neither of them was standing in. No gaps in it, anywhere. Four minutes is a long time to keep a net stretched tight. And Bhatt had kept it tight, two years running.

"Thank you for calling, Inspector."

"Somebody should," Bhatt said. "On the day."

"It's kind of you."

"If anything had ever come up," Bhatt said, "you'd have been the first to know, Sood saab. The first."

It was a kind thing to say. But somewhere in the middle of it — it had also been stamped. Notarized. Manav heard the stamp come down. Heard it from across the room, the way he'd been hearing stamps like that come down across his desk for twenty-one years. And he couldn't have told you exactly what in it was false.

Only this. The signature didn't match the man who was signing.

"We're still here." He didn't say it, this year.

Afterward, he stood at the window while the crackers kept going off outside. And he turned that phone call over in his mind — the way he used to turn a document over, feeling along the edges for the seam.

It was the first time in two years his instrument had swung toward home. It moved on its own. Like a compass needle finding north. And he stood very, very still, and let it point.

He just didn't ask it yet — point at *what*.

---

The old quarter started where the numbered sectors gave up.

Bhagwanpura was smoke, and cycle-repair shops, and the winter light lying down flat in the lanes. A part of town where every third shop fixed something. The address was a staircase, heading down, off a lane full of mechanics — under a signboard so faded that the club's name had to be taken on faith. NEW MODEL BOXING CLUB. Established on a date the peeling paint had carried off with it.

On the landing, a shelf. And on the shelf, a dozen phones. All lying face-down. Like things that had been confiscated.

The club itself was one bulb. A boxing ring older than the boys using it. And a smell of liniment and damp and old smoke that had long since given up trying to leave.

The man holding the pads was sixty-eight, and he moved like a much younger man's memory of himself. He had a name — Iqbal. But nobody had used it since his railway days. Everybody just said Ustad.

"You want?" Ustad said.

"To join."

"You're forty-four."

"I'm aware."

Ustad looked down at his shoes. Office shoes. And let the shoes say the rest.

"There was a fire," Manav said. "2011. There was a survey."

And something changed in the old man's face. Not softening. The opposite of softening — whatever that is, in a man who's just decided to take something seriously.

"They sent letters," Ustad said. "Calling my meter negligence. Then they sent a man who climbed up and looked. The money came. The club came back." He set the pads down on the edge of the ring. "You are that man."

"I'm that man."

"That man," Ustad said, "is owed a favour by this basement." He picked the pads back up. "First month is free. After that, you pay in holding pads and mopping floors. And there's one rule. Whatever you're carrying — it stays up on the landing. With the phones. Everybody leaves something on the stairs."

"And if I can't leave it?"

"Then it trains with you," Ustad said. "And it hits harder than I do."

The boys went back to the ring. Manav stood a moment by that shelf of sleeping phones.

"What are you carrying?" Ustad asked. Not turning around. He asked it the way a man asks a question he's asked ten thousand times before. "Everybody carries something down those stairs."

"A file," Manav said.

"Leave it on the landing."

"It doesn't open."

Ustad nodded. Once. The nod of a man who'd spent forty years listening to men describe the one door inside them that won't open. And who, in forty years, had never once been surprised by what was behind it.

"Then it stays on the landing anyway," he said. "That's the rule. We don't open them here. We skip rope."

---

The weeks went by in the order weeks go by.

He skipped rope badly. And then less badly. He learned to wrap his hands off a boy's phone, at midnight, following instructions in a language he only half understood. And it hit him, tying the first one — that a hand-wrap done right is basically a bandage for a hand that hasn't been hurt yet. Which made it the most honest kind of insurance he'd ever seen.

He held the pads for boys a quarter of his age, who thanked him with their eyes the way young men do — meaning everything, meaning nothing. And he mopped. He wasn't too proud to mop. He'd spent two years finding out exactly how much of pride is just the noise a body makes when it's got nothing to do.

"What are you training for?" Ustad asked one night. Not looking at him.

"To be able to walk at night."

Ustad nodded, like it was the first true thing anyone had said in that basement all week. "Everybody your age who comes down these stairs is training for one man," he said. "Nine times out of ten — it's himself."

Manav said nothing. The count in his head said otherwise.

*(pause)*

One night, Ustad put the mitts on himself. "Let's see your first minute," he said.

And Manav found out that the first minute of being hit is its own country. With its own weather.

The mitt went in, up under his ribs, with a sound he heard about half a second too late. And the floor came up through the canvas. And the whole room rang, like a bell somebody had struck.

He lay there, for the count. Ten numbers. Out loud. In order. At a walking pace.

And it was the first honest piece of arithmetic he'd done in two years. Numbers that faced *him*. Numbers with nowhere to hide. Ten — and the ringing thinned out — and he got up.

"Paper forgets," Ustad said, standing over him, already unwinding the mitts. "Meat remembers. Again?"

"Again."

He didn't have the word for it yet. But it was worship.

Every other hour of his life was just grief, arranged into the shapes of a living person. Breakfast. Tiffin. Water bottle. Four questions, four answers. And these two hours in the basement were the only place where something was allowed to be simply, stupidly true. The rope counted honestly. The heavy bag never lied about what he weighed. His body kept its own ledger, and it was never wrong. And every night, it paid out a little more than he thought he had in him.

There was a boy, Sunny. Nineteen. Held the pads like he was being paid for it. Mopped like he wasn't. One night he caught Manav watching the room, and said, "Uncle. You look at people like you're counting them."

"Occupational hazard," Manav said. Which was true. And was not the joke Sunny took it for.

---

The costs came the way insurance premiums come. Small. On a schedule. Easy enough to pay — and never once free.

He came home at half past midnight with his knuckles wrapped under his sleeves. And once a fortnight, with a mark somewhere — somewhere he'd learned to put it, where his shirt would cover it. At the office, all anyone noticed was that the tea superstition had faded. And that his files were closing faster.

The jeweller's burglary closed in November. The jeweller confessed, halfway through a second interview — almost with relief, the way they almost always do. And Manav wrote up the rejection with something close to tenderness. Because in the end, everybody wants the file closed.

Almost everybody.

Sethi spotted the split knuckle once, at the water cooler. And asked about the cricket instead. Complete tact, as always. And Manav answered about the cricket. And the water cooler did what water coolers do — which is just stand there.

*(short pause)*

"I joined a gym," he told Aarav, over dinner. In the tone men use for confessing to a virtue.

Aarav looked up from his plate. "Good," he said. "You and Mama always said the stairs—"

And he stopped. And put another mouthful in.

"The stairs," Manav agreed. After a moment.

And they finished dinner inside that half of the sentence. Where it was warm.

*(pause)*

Half past midnight, coming home. The hall television murmuring away at its lowest volume. The boy asleep inside the noise of it, one arm hanging off the sofa.

Manav stood in the doorway with his bag, and watched his son sleep inside all that sound. And he didn't turn it off.

He went and washed, before the morning could start asking questions.

---

December. And the cold moved into the flats like a tenant with a signed lease.

Her cupboard opened quietly — the way cupboards do, when nobody's opened them in a season. On the shelf, folded the way she folded everything — in thirds, like letters she'd decided not to send — were her dupattas.

The grey-blue one was third from the left.

In the photograph — the last true one — that one's bunched up in her fist, caught mid-word. She used to hold things in her fist when she was scolding somebody she loved.

He took it down. The cloth held the cold of the shelf. And then, for a moment, it held the warmth of his hands. And he sat on the edge of the bed with it, and let it be a whole winter's worth of grief.

And then he stood up. Because that wasn't what it was for.

*(pause)*

He worked at the kitchen table, after midnight. One earbud in. Some man from somewhere south of the language, teaching strangers how to make masks, at three in the morning.

The first eye-holes came out too far apart. A fish's idea of a face. So he unpicked them and did them again. He used up her needle's thread first, right to the end. Then her spare.

The stitches went in crooked. Then straighter. Then crooked again — every time his mind wandered off somewhere. So that the mask, to anyone who could read sewing — and in this city, who can't — was a record of exactly where his mind had been allowed to go. And where it hadn't.

It took nine nights.

On the ninth, at two in the morning, in the bathroom light — he put it on. And looked in the mirror.

*(pause)*

The man in the mirror was nobody's husband. Nobody's father. Nobody's grief.

For two years, every single eye that landed on Manav Sood had found the thing that happened to him first. The office. The society. The tea nobody would offer him. But the mask had no history. Nothing looked at it and saw the night. Nothing looked at it at all.

It was terrible.

He wore it anyway.

He stood a while at the front door, with the chain in his hand. Down under the window, the city went by — a scooter, a dog with strong opinions, the late vegetable cart. And then he put the chain back.

The body wasn't ready. The mask couldn't carry this on its own. Not yet.

Whatever this was going to be called — it didn't have a name yet.

It had a body first.

He folded the mask in thirds. The way she folded everything — his hands had learned how somewhere, without ever asking him. And he put it in his office bag. Between two closed files.

Where nobody in the world ever opens anything.

*[end of Episode Two]*

## Part One · The House
### Episode 3: "First Night"

*Narration script · single narrator · est. runtime ~15–16 min (~2,420 words)*

"I've seen worse," the boy said.

He said it to the gauze. Matter-of-fact. A worker stating a qualification. And it was four words long. And it closed the door on every conversation Manav had rehearsed, on the stairs.

"Didi said don't tell," Aarav said. "The last thing she said to me. *Eyes closed. Whatever you hear — it's the TV.*"

He wrapped the right hand now. Over the thumb. Twice at the wrist. Competent. A thing learned from a phone. And the thought had a basement in it.

"She thought hiding was one night, Papa. She didn't know you'd keep going. She didn't know it would be a job."

"She came to the almirah twice," the boy said. "The second time, she didn't say anything. She just looked in, through the slats. At me. And then she went back out, and was loud. So they would follow the loud."

The app had been wrong in the only way it was ever wrong. In what it chose to finish. The name had never been difficult. The name had been *kept.* Six feet from where the mask was stored. Between two closed files. Behind a boy's locked door. Held for four years, under a dead girl's command. The gift had been true. The gifts were always true. That had never once been the complaint.

*(pause)*

And the case. His case. The locked room he had bled for. It had never been locked at all. Three attempted statements. Three weeks running. Three findings of *child not fit to be examined.* One signature, on all three. The file had called it unfitness.

The boy had not been unfit. The boy had been the most fit witness in the city. And he had stood at three interviews and said nothing. Seven years old. On duty. Because his sister had told him the television would handle the rest.

*(pause)*

*Ira surfaced twice. Both times, the same sentence. The second time, slower. Both times, her eyes going past him. To the door.*

Four years, he had read that look as a girl looking for a doctor. It had been a guard, checking a door. *Aarav ne kuch nahin dekha.* Aarav didn't see anything. He had answered it like a question — *yes, beta, of course, he was asleep.* And it had never been a question. It had been a last instruction. Issued to a corridor. With her eyes on the door. "Good," the boy said. Without heat. And then — because he was ten, and not a machine — "It isn't good. I don't know what it is. It's finished. And I'm still on watch. And I would like that to be noted."

"It's noted," Manav said.

The boy closed the box the way he closed his school bag. Everything squared. The lid down, in one motion. And then he stood there, at the table's edge, in the light. With nothing on his face he hadn't put there on purpose.

"The rest of that night," Manav began — because a father has to offer — "you don't ever have to—"

"I'm not telling you the rest," the boy said. "Some of it is mine." A breath. And the voice his mother used, for jokes that were not. "You don't have to tell me yours, either. Didi said don't tell. You didn't tell. That was right. Now I've told. We're even."

"Is it over now?" Manav asked. The boy's own question. Handed back across the table. Because he had no other honest thing in him.

"No," he said. "But it's in the open, Papa. And things in the open — you can work with."

His own line. In his own child's mouth. Itemized, and returned.

*(pause)*

The week after was the first ordinary week in four years. And neither of them trusted it. And they did not say so. And that was ordinary too.

The register at school took the stairs, without a form. No man came after the forms. The ribs mended at the speed of ribs. And nobody said anything about that either.

Some instructions, it turned out, could be retired with the officer.

*(pause)*

He took what was left of the mask — torn, grey-blue, one drawstring gone — and folded it in thirds. The way she had folded everything. And put it back on the shelf of her cupboard. Between her things. Where nobody in the world opens anything.

It had left that shelf as a dupatta. And come home as a fact.

He closed the cupboard the way the drawer at Kedarpuri had never closed. And this time, it latched. Which was not the same as healing. And was not nothing.

Outside, the era went on without him. No new footage came. And the city, having none, promoted the mask to weather. THE MASK HAS BLOOD aged into legend, between two festivals. On the ninth day of the ordinary week, at eleven fifty-two, the phone lit. Once. Numbered.

*C-15. The last one. It makes everything else small. I have verified it more than anything I have ever given you. When you are ready.*

He did not open it.

*(pause)*

He was surprised, in the days after, by how much of him it took — to not do a thing. He'd spent twenty years opening things for a living. Opening was the whole trade. Assessment is deciding which losses get paid. And you cannot pay what you will not read. The badge sat on the phone, like a claim in an in-tray. One small number, on a field of dark. And he caught himself reading around it — the school notices, the weather, the fog lifting off the city in strips — the way a man reads everything in a room, except the letter.

And the number went with it. C-15. Unread. Unopened. And *unregretted* is a lie. And the world did not change. And had not been waiting to.
