# VIGILANTE — Audio Series
## Part One · The House
### Episode 4: "First Success"

*Narration script · single narrator · est. runtime ~15 min (~2,320 words)*
*This one has a cruel calm to it — the abuser never raises his voice, so the narration shouldn't either. Play the fight fast and cold, no relish. "And then what?" is the hinge of the whole episode; let the silence sit after it before moving on.*

---

Between Block B and Block C ran a gully. Three feet wide. Built to let in air — and used, like everything built for air in that city, to carry sound.

He'd been hearing it since November. The way everyone had been hearing it since November — which is to say, officially, not at all. A burst. Then a lull. And in the lulls, a television. Domestic violence, it turns out, has a rhythm. The way rain has a rhythm. And a building learns it the way hills learn a river. Nothing to be done. The shape is just the shape of the land.

On the nights he was home to hear it, Manav lay on his good side and listened, along with the whole city block. And did what the whole city block did. Which was to certify the sound as weather.

*(short pause)*

The man was Deepak-ji. Second floor. The flat with the window second from the end of the gully. He was the accounts man on the block committee — the society trusted him with its money, its receipts, the Diwali fund. A certified man.

And he didn't shout. That was the terrible part. What came through the gully was — administrative. Somewhere in those rooms there was a system. Patient. Itemized. And in the lulls, the television played, for the neighbours.

Manav didn't know her name. He knew Deepak-ji's name. Everyone did. That's how that works.

*(pause)*

What finally moved him — on a Tuesday in February — wasn't her sound.

The rhythm changed. Burst. Lull. Burst. And then, in a lull, a third sound. Small. A child's voice, cut short, like a thread. And he was off the bed with his heart already going, before his mind had even voted.

It wasn't her sound that moved him. And he knew what that said about him. And he knew where he filed it. And he picked up the bag.

---

The society's one camera watched the main gate, with the fixed devotion of the badly mounted. He'd studied its aim the way he studied everything now — without deciding to. So he went out the long way, past the water tank, where the camera's faith didn't reach. Block C's stairwell door hadn't latched properly since the intercom died. The whole building's security, in the end, came down to one broken latch. Faithful, like everything else.

Second floor. Second door from the gully end. Behind the inner door of the flat, a study lamp was burning. Exams, the lamp said. February. Boys study through everything. It's the one thing adults never believe.

He knocked.

The door opened two inches. To the chain. A vest. A calm face. A dish towel over one shoulder. Clean eyes. Sober eyes.

The worst news yet.

"Kya chahiye?" What do you want.

"We can hear it," the mask said. "From C. All of us."

It was the first sentence the mask ever spoke, out loud, in the world. And every word of it was false — except the hearing. There was no *all*. There was no *us*. There was one man, in a dead woman's cloth, on a landing at two in the morning. Borrowing a plural. Because the singular was unbearable.

Deepak-ji looked at the mask the way a treasurer looks at an entry that hasn't been audited. "There is no sound," he said. And men that confident don't call for help. Help is for other people. He undid the chain and slid it back and opened the door — to take the intruder by the collar.

And the night moved into the flat.

*(pause)*

It went to plan for exactly one minute. The body did what the basement had put into it. Weight forward, inside the arms, the shoulders doing their arithmetic. Deepak-ji was strong the way committee men are strong — from carrying certified things. And he got one hand on the counter, and came back up with the kitchen knife.

And here the night paused, for its examination question.

The knife moved. And Manav went in *under* it — instead of back. And took a line of fire along his left forearm. The blade caught the jaw of the mask — cloth only, a seam's width. And then the knife was on the floor, and the man was folded over the sink, and the room was over.

Except for the part that wasn't.

Nobody trains you for the second minute. The second minute is where you find out what you came for.

He found out. He hit the folded man twice more than the fight asked for. And he'd have gone to a third. And it wasn't rage, exactly — rage is hot, and this had no temperature at all —

and then the woman was screaming at him to stop. One word. Twice. And the word wasn't for Deepak-ji.

It was for him.

*(pause)*

She was between them. Her arm out. Guarding the man who beat her. Standing there like the last wall of a fort that had already chosen its side.

And the room went still. The lamp burning behind the inner door. And the boy standing in it.

Seven, maybe eight. Eyes completely open. And the boy didn't scream either. The boy watched him — the way children watch when they're filing the world away for later. And then he stepped back. And closed the inner door. Softly. One squeak of the hinge. The way doors get closed in houses where closing a door quietly is a skill you study.

The woman's mouth was bleeding at the corner. And the room around her was squared away and gleaming — cushions squared, the glass of the show-cabinet shining. A flat kept the way flats are kept when the world's been invited in. The violence was already back in its drawer.

And she stood in the middle of her cage, arm out, breath going. Forty-one, maybe. Protecting it.

"I'll call the police," Manav said.

"And then what?"

*(pause)*

He'd prepared nothing after that. There is nothing after that. The nothing that comes after *then* was the exact country he'd been living in for two years. She was only quoting his own file back to him. And she had the better of the argument. And they both knew it.

And Deepak-ji, getting up onto one knee off the floor, smiled at his wife. With blood in his teeth. The smile of a man whose alibi had just walked in wearing a mask.

Manav left the way he'd come. Past the dead intercom. Out through the door that never latched.

---

By morning, the society knew everything. Except what had actually happened.

A thief. Masked. Tall as the door. Six feet, said the messages. And by noon, six foot two. One man said knife. Another said gun. And the mistake became history. The guard was doubled. The gate got a second chain — and it's remarkable, what a second chain does for a building's opinion of itself. The committee expressed its sympathy. For its treasurer.

A constable came, was given chai at the gate, wrote down six feet of description, and promised patrols. The patrolling began that night, and ended that night.

Somewhere, a file was not born.

In the lift, Manav — five foot eight, in shoes, since 1999 — agreed, gravely, that the masked man did sound like somebody.

A few days later, at the gate, the woman passed him with a bag of groceries. And for the length of one question mark, she looked at him. The question got as far as its first word. And then the sentence closed over it — the way sentences do, in that country. And she looked straight through him.

And he let her. She'd seen a body come through a door. That was all. Every body in that society belonged to somebody. And the wrongness of his walk could be filed under nothing.

And it was.

---

At home, at three in the morning, he cleaned the forearm with the water he'd been saving for tea. Because Farid had taught him nothing out loud, and everything by example.

The jaw of the mask had been opened in a clean seam. He sat in the kitchen with her needle and her grey thread, and mended it. And the stitches went in straight. Every one.

Nine nights of crooked and straight — a seismograph of everywhere his mind had gone. And now, an hour after a man's mouth, straight as ruled work.

He sat looking at that for a long time afterward. And the looking was its own finding.

*(pause)*

By every number he owned, the night had been a success. He'd crossed a room. Taken a knife off a bad man with his own body. Won. The numbers sat there, being right.

And underneath them — in the space where an assessor writes what he knows and does not say — the finding that actually mattered.

The second minute had wanted its whole life.

Not the knife. Not Deepak-ji. The *taste*. That was the real danger. And he put it where he put things now — knowing the filing solved nothing.

---

At the gym, one night, over the wraps, he asked Ustad the question sideways. The way you file a query.

"In your experience," he said. "A man who sees a wrong, and says nothing. Is that fear?"

Ustad wound a boy's hands through the turnstile of his fingers. Not looking up. The radio played at the volume of memory. Somewhere up the stairs a scooter went by, with its whole life still ahead of it.

"Fear is rare," Ustad said, finally. "Loyalty is common. Men are not cowards, mostly. They're faithful. Often to the wrong things. But you cannot fault the faith."

Whatever Ustad understood about the new stillness in his student — about ribs that healed in January and a forearm cut open in February — he left it on the landing.

As was the rule.

---

He came home to the television at its lowest number, and the boy asleep inside the noise of it. And he stood in the doorway of the hall, doing the new arithmetic.

In one building tonight, a boy had closed a door so softly that only the hinge knew. In this one, a boy slept inside a sound — for reasons no file had ever held.

*Witnesses become afraid.* The old sentence. Worn smooth as a river stone by two years of handling. He stood there looking at his son. And for the first time, he began to imagine the witnesses in his own file as *faithful* instead. Loyal to something, every one of them. Faithful to the wrong things.

And the file grew heavier. By a page.

He put the page where the mask lived. Between two closed files. Where nobody in the world opens anything.
