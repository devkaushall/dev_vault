# VIGILANTE — Audio Series
## Part Two · The System
### Episode 11: "The Mask Multiplies"

*Narration script · single narrator · est. runtime ~14–15 min (~2,340 words)*
*A survey chapter with a set-piece in the middle. The tone can carry a little dark irony in the "industries" section — the city monetizing its own fear — but pull all the way back for the woman on the roof, and again for the school gate. The Wazir Lane confrontation is the year's image: play it as a man reading a document aloud, not a hero making a speech. End quiet and pointed — the last three words are a decision.*

---

The first copycats were at least honest about it. Three boys in festival masks, at a bus stop, stopping scooters, demanding to see receipts. Collecting fifty rupees a virtue.

It was a shakedown. It was also, in its way, a religion. And by August, the city had both kinds.

There were the freelance masks — kids, mostly — who'd understood the scripture as a business licence. There were the printed vests. The first gang with a uniform. The *Nagrik Rakshak Dal* — screen-printed, and paying dues — who patrolled two markets, "correcting" them. Evil had discovered screen-printing. And the era never got it back.

And then there was the thing he had actually started. Though nobody knew it had started anywhere. Least of all at a bedtime.

*(pause)*

He met that one on a Tuesday. In the lane behind the timber market's smoke.

A woman, on a roof. A woman on the corner at eleven. And at one. And at three. Walking her street the way he'd once learned to walk the night — in loops, in stillness, ninety seconds to a pause. Her mask was a dupatta, wound once, the way women wind them when the wind is up. The cloth was cotton, and old. And he recognized the grammar of it — the way you recognize your own handwriting, in someone else's letter.

He never asked. Some not-askings are load-bearing.

The lane knew her story the way lanes know things. In full. Out loud. And never to her face. Her sister had been a nurse. Taken in June. The receipt had come the way receipts come — a ward ledger, pages of it. Medicines, moving at prices. Signatures. The family had read it, and gone quiet. Because the ledger was true.

And then her sister's colleague had stood up at a prayer meeting, and said the prices were the insurance queue's prices. That the signatures were the matron's signature. That the papers said what they said, because of what the papers were *for*. And the family had understood — that the ledger was true, and the reading was wrong. And that no channel, and no court, and no hotline in the world was interested in the difference.

She'd said one thing, once, to the paanwala. And the lane had kept it the way scripture keeps a verse.

*I am not disputing the paper. I am disputing the reading.*

*(pause)*

Nothing had been corrected on her street since June. The lane couldn't say whether that was her. The lane said nothing about her, out loud, ever. He passed through twice that summer — mask to mask, across the sodium light — and they did not speak.

There's nothing sentries need to say.

---

The industries arrived in the order industries arrive. Religion first. Then compliance. Then paper.

The godman's compound — one diya short since the aarti — had opened an Assurance Line. Assurance was the word. The flyers said *peace*. The small print said *consultation*. And the queue outside the gate was the longest in the city. Because fear will pay anything that stands still long enough.

A firm of ex-chartered accountants in Sector 8 sold Sin Audits. A full examination of your business, for list-exposure. A bound report. A certificate of cleanliness. A certificate that no one, anywhere, had ever asked heaven to accept.

And at the office, in September, a circular came round. A rival had launched a product. The claims department should prepare. And there was, the circular said — in the mild language head offices use for weather — a *definitional difficulty*.

Every policy in the country excluded acts of god. Nobody had ever had to define one. Now the definition was worth more than all the premiums in the world. And all files that were weather-adjacent were advised, until further notice, to be held.

Manav read the circular twice, at his desk. And filed it under *the era* — which was a drawer now. The widest one. The whole city was in assurance. So, in its way, had he always been.

At the gym, Ustad turned away thirty boys in August. "The stairs," he said, "have become popular." The landing rule held. Most of what the boys were carrying had a logo on it.

*(pause)*

And at the school gate, in the ragged hour before the van, Manav arrived once in time to see the game the city's children had invented for themselves. *List-list*, someone called it. A game of tag, where the taken stood still, with their eyes shut, until the mask freed them.

The boys argued over who got to play the mask. It was the good role now.

Aarav did not play the mask. He said it was a stupid game. He said it with a completeness that ended the subject. And the van moved on. And Manav stood at the gate with the founding conversation of his whole second life — *even his maa doesn't know; that's the smartest part* — and understood, in the school-morning light, what he had taught the city, in teaching it to one boy.

He had taught his son that masks protect the ones you love.

The city had learned it in its own hand. Masks protect the ones you hate.

---

The Zorawar thread, all September, ran on patience.

The timber shop kept its rhythm. The man dealt in confirmed-empty the way other men dealt in plywood. And twice a month, after closing, the shutter at the back admitted a visitor — who stayed eleven minutes. A small man. An accountant's bag. Came by night, left the same way. The cousin. Or a cousin of the cousin. The thread to the office-wala, walking in and out of a back shutter, on a schedule. And Manav, who'd learned to read rhythms the way he'd once read claims, had the night marked. The last visit of the month. The one where the accounts were said to be settled.

He was two streets from the timber market when the glow of torches came up over the roofs.

*(pause)*

The lane was called Wazir Lane. And it had two R. Kumars — which the era discovered at twenty to one in the morning.

The Rakshaks were at the kirana shop. The poorer R. Kumar's. Lathis. A vest with printing on it. And a can of kerosene, set down on the step, like a document about to be filed. The receipt the gang carried was on a phone. *R. Kumar, jeweller, Wazir Lane.* Forwarded, and re-forwarded, and certain.

The shop behind the shutter sold dal. The family lived above it — a light on in the stairwell, a child's face at the grill. And the vest with the printing had just picked up the can — when the mask stepped into the torchlight.

The gang froze the way the era froze. Scripture does that.

"Show me the paper," the mask said.

The big one held out the phone. Held it out — the way men hold things out to an altar. And Manav read the receipt by torchlight. In front of the whole lane. Which was watching, from behind every curtain it owned.

And the era got its image of the year.

"This paper says a jeweller." He said it at parade volume — the voice the flats above could not close a curtain on. "This man sells dal. His name is on the ration board. R. Kumar. Provisions. The jeweller is number 22 — the far end, the shop with the grills." He handed the phone back. "Read your own paper."

*(pause)*

Nobody read the paper. The big one was looking at the kerosene can — as if it had been left there by someone else. Some other era.

"The list does not need your help," the mask said. "It has never needed anyone's help. It took three men, in one minute, in three cities, in the dark — without one sound. So what is your lathi for? It is not for them."

He let the lane's silence carry it. The way a counter carries a figure.

"It is for you. Go home. Sit very still. And be glad — be *glad* — that the list reads better than you do."

They went. Not fast. The way men go, when they've just discovered, in front of witnesses, which side of the paper they're written on. The torches left the lane the way a tide leaves — taking the kerosene can with them. And the shutter of the kirana shop stayed shut. And behind the grill on the stair, the child's face went away. And above the shop, a light stayed on all night.

Which is how lanes vote.

*(pause)*

No one had touched anyone. Some animal in him noted that. With disappointment. And he noted the animal, noting it. And he filed them both.

At two in the morning, the scripture had its next verse. HE READS THE LIST, the forwards said. The mask can read the list. And Manav — who had read one ration board, and one phone, by torchlight — stood at the footbridge over the canal, and could not sign the correction. Which was, by now, a permanent condition of his life. The city printing him, nightly. And the man inside the print, unable to correct a word.

---

The ledger entry for the night was short.

Across the city, at the same hour the torches went out of Wazir Lane, a back shutter over a timber shop had admitted a small man with an accountant's bag. And shut. And opened again, eleven minutes later. And the small man had walked away into the era. And would not be back before winter.

He had known the stakes when he chose the lane. Knowing did not make the entry easier to file. But he found, filing it, that he did not regret the ledger's shape. And that finding frightened him more than any kerosene. Because it meant the question had turned. For good. The way a wheel turns once, and is now going the other way.

*(pause)*

He had learned the night to find four men. And the four had been organized out from under him, by heaven, tidily — without one word of why. The why was still loose in the world. It had a cousin in it, somewhere. And an office. And a man who knew timings. And the thread to it ran through a timber shop that had just gone quiet for the season. Because the era frightened even its own predators into honesty.

But the paper trail was not the only trail.

There was one file left in the city, with the why folded up inside it. The case file of a night in October. Three years gone. The file that had been starved before he ever saw it. The one set of documents in the whole world that he had spent four years reading as a widower — and never once as a claims man.

It lived in a steel cupboard. Six feet from where his son slept.

That was over now.
