# VIGILANTE — Audio Series
## Part Two · The System
### Episode 8: "The Courtship"

*Narration script · single narrator · est. runtime ~13 min (~2,060 words)*
*A two-hander between a man and a machine, mostly at a kitchen table. Keep the app's lines flat and clipped — right, never warm — same rule as Episode Seven. The turn on "Three" and "You did not ask" should go very quiet; no drama, just a floor dropping. Don't sell it.*

---

The courtship — if you could call it that — was conducted between eleven and three. At a kitchen table. Between a man with a ledger, and a phone that weighed nothing.

June had normalized the era the way June normalizes heat. The list had taken two men that month. The papers printed them below the commodity prices, and the city read both over tea, and wondered about neither.

The network behind the marble market had gone cautious. Lights on later. Fewer runs. The drop-house counting its own money twice. The dark had management now — and the management was bad for everyone's business. Including his. The hunt that had run on five months of bench-work was slowing down to nothing. And both parties at the kitchen table knew it.

*(pause)*

He'd decided, early, on a protocol. The numbered messages were its own openings. But the answers it gave to *his* questions carried no numbers — as if his questions weren't events at all. Only weather.

It corrected. It did not converse.

First, the audit. He'd spent his life verifying claimants. And he verified this one the only way he knew how.

He asked it about a fire, in a basement, in 2011. It answered with the meter's serial number. He had stood in that meter room himself, in the smoke, twenty-one years of surveys ago. And he had never once known the meter had a name.

Then he did the thing he'd wanted for years to do to certain claimants. He salted the file. A true story — the jeweller, the confession in November — with one false fact set into it. Like a single stitch. *The jeweller broke in the third interview,* he typed.

The answer came back in one word.

*Second.*

It had not been offended. It had not been fooled. And it had not let it stand.

Three rules, then. Learned the way he'd learned everything. At small cost. On purpose. One — it does not lie. Two — it does not let *him* lie. Three — everything else is a choice. And the choices are its own.

---

Then he asked it the questions he shouldn't.

*Who are you.*

Silence. With a texture he knew from the counter — the silence of a party that has weighed the cost of answering, and found it higher than the premium.

*Who pays you.*

The same silence. Exactly the same. Which was itself a piece of data. Both silences had the same author.

*Why me.*

Nothing.

And then, at four in the morning, when he'd put the phone down and picked it up again — the way a tongue keeps going back to a tooth:

*Why should I trust you?*

*You shouldn't. You have verified every correction, and acted on every one. Trust is not what this is.*

He sat with that. It was the first answer that gave him nothing to check, and everything to look at. It answered the way a mirror answers. With himself. Arranged slightly truer.

*Does the mask have anything to do with the takings?* The city's own scripture — put to the only oracle he had.

*That is not a claim. It cannot be audited.*

Not *I don't know.* Not *no.* A refusal of the whole category. The first question it had ever turned down as a thing that could even exist. He noted it, in the small hand he kept for findings that didn't fit. *The source's system ends where the sky begins. Or the source is careful. Both filings live.*

One more, that week. At two in the morning. The question underneath all his questions.

*Is anything watching my flat?*

*No. Not even me. Houses are the one thing I cannot enter.*

*(pause)*

The relief arrived before the thought did. And the thought arrived anyway. It was the first time in three years that anything had told him the flat was his.

And underneath that — quiet as a page turning — the other thought. The one he did not file. *It has never once said the boy's name.* Not in a correction. Not in the care. Not the wrist. Not the sleep.

He noticed. The way he noticed everything now. He did not ask why. Some not-askings are load-bearing.

*(short pause)*

The wrist had got its ice, in the end. Pride lasts until the second swollen morning. And the care went on, on a schedule of its own choosing.

*C-4. Sleep. You have slept nine hours this week that your body agrees to count.*

He obeyed it the way he obeyed Ustad. The way he sat where Farid could see him. And he hated the mechanism by which he obeyed — which he could not find anywhere in the phone.

It occurred to him one Thursday, filing the night away, that the app had now heard more of his voice than any human being had, since the hospital.

He put that under findings. Where it did the most damage. And left it there. Because findings, unlike people, stay where you put them.

---

Summer had emptied the mornings of the school van. Aarav was home all day now. And he'd started, without announcing it, making the evening tea — carefully, measuring the leaf, watching the colour come up, the way he did everything. Completely.

And at dinner one night, out of nowhere — over the one television in the city that did not carry the news:

"Papa. Can it see inside houses?"

The *it* needed no explaining. The era lived in the children now. Like weather.

"Nobody knows that," Manav said.

"That's still not the same as no," the boy said. And poured his father's tea. And the era, and the epistemology, and the tea all arrived at the table together. And Manav drank what he was given.

---

And then, one night in late June, at one in the morning, he asked the question that lived underneath all the others. The one he'd been not-asking since the wrist.

*The crew. Four men. How many are alive?*

The answer took four seconds. Which was long. For it.

*Three.*

Nothing followed. The number just stood there in the kitchen — the way a man stands in a doorway.

He typed: *Which one.*

*The one they called Chotu. He died outside Panipat, in a card dispute, fourteen months ago. The file on him is closed.*

*(pause)*

Chotu.

He held the phone with both hands. Chotu was a name out of a dark place. Out of the underpass. The January of the ribs. A man's voice, calling a name into the dark — *Chotu aa gaya* — and the dark producing company.

One of the four. One of the four had helped beat him, in an underpass, three feet away. Over seventy rupees of tea, and the price of being seen. And had then lived fourteen more months. And died over a game of cards. And the quarter of the *why* that lived in that man's mouth had died with him. Unmourned. Unaudited. Below the commodity prices.

He typed the only thing left.

*You knew.*

*You did not ask.*

*(pause)*

He sat back from the table.

It was the truest sentence the app had ever sent him. And the first one he could not file without bleeding. Formally correct. He hadn't asked. It hadn't volunteered. Both parties had acted within the rules of a contract that nobody had ever shown him. And the whole of the wrongness in the world was sitting inside that formal correctness. Like a settlement that pays out, and pays out, and changes nothing.

He had known counterparties like this.

He had *been*, on certain days, a counterparty like this.

He put the phone face-down, and didn't type again for two days. And the phone — with a patience that felt like the whole of its character — sent nothing at all. And the silence had the texture of a bench. Of Farid's bench. Of a landing with the phones lying on it. Of things that wait, without watching.

*(pause)*

On the third night, at twenty to two, unprompted, numbered:

*C-5. The one they called Lakha. Milk-route tempo, Bhagwanpura side. He sleeps at the wheel between runs. He has not been home since that winter. He was never difficult to find. He was only difficult to name.*

He read it until the words stopped being words. And then he read them again. And the name stood in the kitchen — the way a man stands in a doorway.

Lakha.

Two years of nothing. And then three years of nothing. And now — a name. After the anger, not before it. After the withholding, not before it. Exactly when a gift would bind a claimant the most.

He noted the timing. The way he noted everything. And he noted, also, that the noting had not made him put the name down.

He did not write it in the ledger.

Paper forgets. This one, he would keep in the meat.
