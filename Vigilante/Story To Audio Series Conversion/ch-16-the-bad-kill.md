# VIGILANTE — Audio Series
## Part Three · The Watchers
### Episode 16: "The Bad Kill"

*Narration script · single narrator · est. runtime ~14 min (~2,120 words)*
*The darkest episode. The fight must NOT sound cool — play it ugly, close, breathless, then let it go quiet where the killing happens; the quiet is the horror. No relish anywhere. The cost is the point: Ustad's palm, the city turning, the drawer that won't close. The Architect stays flat. The final threshold — the kitchen light at four — is held breath, not a cliffhanger sting. Stop clean on "He put the key in the lock."*

---

February. The whisper came up the underpass the way water finds the low road. A season was moving again. Wedding-week travel. Houses going empty across the river. Paper changing hands, behind the fog.

The phone had been dark since January. Since C-11. The silence had the old bench-taste, and he'd read it — when he read it at all — as the thing letting the meat remember.

The claims man in him priced the tip the way he'd once priced four answers at a kitchen table. Too smooth. Claims that arrive too smoothly have been through the machine twice. He knew the tell. He held the tell in one hand, and the season in the other. And the season was a list of kitchens. Some corridor of flats, dark for a week in March. Some street's worth of almirahs. And the tell weighed nothing.

C-11 stood open in the phone. Like a door he had personally closed. *Ask me anything.* He did not ask. That had been a decision. And decisions, he knew from the trade, are the only things with premiums.

---

The tower stood where the empty-block tide had left it. Nineteen floors. Six of them lit. A guard who watched the road, and not the stairwell. The flat was on the fourth. A corner. The door left on the latch. The whisper had said the season's paper was being copied there, before it moved on.

He'd planned it the way he planned everything now. Entry. Read. Exit. No touching. And the plan held for as long as plans hold. Which is until the second minute.

*(pause)*

In the hall, arranged on a folded newspaper — a pair of shoes. Laced. Size eleven. Set square to the wall. The way soldiers, and nothing else, set shoes.

Inside, the flat had been emptied of everything, except dust sheets, and one man. He sat on a sheet, against the wall, with the patience of furniture. A canvas bag open at his knee. And he was not the myth's size. The myth had grown six inches, over two years of forwards. And the man on the dust sheet had given every inch back.

He looked at the mask come through the door the way assessors look at a claim that has finally walked in. Without surprise. Without pleasure. The paperwork already half-done, in the head.

"The pages," he said.

It was the only sentence he spent that night.

---

The first minute belonged to the basement.

The footwork held. The shoulder turned the way it had been taught. The jab went out, and came back, and went out again. And the wraps did what wraps do — which is lie faithfully, about what hands are. The man on the dust sheet did not swing. He *arrived.* Elbows short. Chin gone. Feet never crossing. A man conserving his motion the way the old conserve water. And somewhere in it, Manav heard Ustad's breath-count, in his ear. Paper forgetting, while meat remembered. And for a minute, he was excellent. And the minute knew it. And ended.

*(pause)*

The second minute is where you find out what you came for. He'd known the sentence for four years. And he had always believed it was about him.

The professional stopped receiving. And started collecting. A forearm across the mouth. The wall's plaster, gritting at the back of his skull. His own breath going from an instrument, to a noise. He got his chin down, and his hips back, the way the basement taught. And the basement was right. And being right was not enough. Because the street does not stop when the mitt stops. The forearm settled in. Patient. A man closing a claim.

And the mask — caught between the arm and his face, dragged as he twisted — left him. The way a stamp leaves an envelope. Torn. And in another man's hand.

The hall's cold found his whole face at once. Four years. And no living creature had seen it, in this work.

His hand went past the forearm. Past the shoe-square. Into the open bag. And the knife was in his fist — before any decision had been signed.

*(pause)*

What came after was not the knife's fault. There was more of it than the work required. He knew the number. He would not be writing it down.

The professional's last look was not fear. It was arithmetic. A man revising, at speed, the one assumption he had priced wrong. He'd trained for the myth — six-two, and chained, and doubled. And what was killing him was five foot eight, and forty-six years old, and bleeding from the mouth. A clerk of losses, with iron in his teeth.

The revision did not finish.

*(pause)*

The taste of coins. He knew it, from a flyover floor, four years ago. And he knew the new thing, under it. The quiet thing. The thing the animal had noted once, with disappointment, at not being touched. The second minute had wanted its whole life. And it had finally been fed. And the feeding had been quiet. And that — not the knife, not the man going from furniture to fact on the dust sheet — that was the thing he would carry up whatever stairs came next.

He tried, once, sitting in the dark of the sheets, to file the animal. There had been a drawer for it, once. Under four years of audits. The drawer would not take it. It stayed out. The way blood stays on.

---

He sat against the wall, over the body, for one hour. Because the era had taught him the etiquette. A taking comes within the minute. The pressure — like a train arriving, somewhere behind the sky. The gravel leaning toward it. He waited for the train.

Nothing came.

Nothing came for an hour. And that was the era's newest cruelty. And he understood it slowly, sitting over what he had made. You could no longer tell acquittal — from schedule.

In the dead fist, a scrap of grey-blue cloth. He did not take it back. The era would need the exhibit.

---

At the footbridge, the phone was warm in his fist before he even reached the rail. He'd thrown it from here once — in every way but fact. He typed standing. A claims man, filing from a loss site.

"You knew. The quiet, since the shutter."

*C-12. Yes. Since the shutter closed. A man was engaged for the pages. Gurgaon. Ex-Army. He does not panic, and he does not confess. You have met the type, in files.*

"You watched me walk into him."

*You did not ask me to stop you.*

The river worked under the bridge. He typed nothing. And it came once more. Separately numbered. In the way of things that have waited their turn.

*C-13. You didn't ask.*

*(pause)*

Four years ago, on a night with rain in it, it had sent him four words. *You knew. You did not ask.* The words had come home now. The way paper does. Marked — return.

"The bag," he typed. "Tape. Plastic. A knife."

*The bag was for paper. Folders. A clipboard. One blade, for opening. He was sent to retrieve. Not to remove. You were the complication. Not the errand.*

"Was he on the list?"

*He was on three lists. None of them heaven's.*

"Am I now."

The longest pause it had ever taken. Eleven seconds, somewhere in January, had been the record.

*I do not know. One hundred and six. And none of them made by hands. Tonight, I hold one hundred and seven facts. And no model.*

*Go home. Sit very still.*

He read the last four words twice. He had said them to a lane with torches. And to a good man, with a defrost working at a windshield. And the era kept no register of who the sentences were for.

---

The gym was lit. It was always lit before five. Iron does not keep office hours.

Ustad was in the doorway with the first tea of the morning. And he saw him pass — over the rim of the glass, along the lane. A man walking at the pace of his own ribs. And he did not call out. He lifted one hand. Palm flat. Fingers together. The gesture trainers make from the corner, when the answer is *no more.*

*Enough, beta. Enough.*

And went inside. And the sound of the gym door closing followed him up the lane. Like a verdict, with no reasons attached.

*Paper forgets. Meat remembers.* He'd been told that by a man with red hands, standing over him, unwinding the wraps, in the second year. He had filed it, at the time, as gym philosophy.

It was an audit finding now.

*(pause)*

By Friday, the city had the rest. No one claimed the body — the network's men don't get funerals. They get settlements. And the channels had their loop. A scrap of grey cloth, in a fist. And under it, in the era's largest font: THE MASK HAS BLOOD.

HE READS THE LIST stopped being forwarded, that week. The city had liked its god bloodless. It had spent two years being certain of him. And certainty, in the era, turned out to be a renewable resource. The Rakshaks said they'd always known. The woman's lane said nothing at all. Which was its own verdict. And he did not go to find out which.

---

His own stairs took him slowly. The left side had taken a letter opener's education once, four years ago. And it remembered the syllabus.

The kitchen light was on. At four in the morning.

*(pause)*

Through the frosted glass, a shape moved at the counter. Small. Unhurried. Not a shape that had just woken. A shape that had been keeping a watch of its own. Standing the way one stands at a counter — when one has been waiting a long time, and has stopped pretending otherwise.

He stood in the corridor with the torn remainder of the mask against his chest, and the taste still open in him, like a claim. And he understood that whatever happened next was going to happen in the light.

His hands would not go into his pockets.

He put the key in the lock.
