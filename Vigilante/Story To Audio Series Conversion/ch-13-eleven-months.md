# VIGILANTE — Audio Series
## Part Three · The Watchers
### Episode 13: "Eleven Months"

*Narration script · single narrator · est. runtime ~15 min (~2,360 words)*
*Part Three opens. A two-man conversation on a cold verandah — most of the episode is Bhatt talking, so break his lines with breath; he is a tired, honest old man confessing, never a villain performing. No heat from the narrator. Let "Yet" and "I attend" sit in silence. The ending is quiet dread, not a sting.*

---

The school was going to the hills in November. Four days. A bus, one teacher for every nine children. Aarav packed the bag himself, in one evening, in an order that suggested lists. Torch. Two books. One sweater, rolled the way the camp sheet said to roll it. And at the very last, without explaining it, he took the small brass bell down from the puja shelf, and put it in the side pocket, with the torch.

Manav did not ask about the bell. The house had stopped asking about things. It was how the house prayed.

The next morning he called Inspector Bhatt. Retired. Kedarpuri. And said he'd be passing through, on a survey. A small lie of transit, professionally laid. And Bhatt was quiet for exactly as long as an old policeman is quiet when a lie is being laid on him. And then said, "Come, Sood saab. I'm always home."

Four years of kettle, in that one sentence.

*(pause)*

Kedarpuri in November was cold the way river towns are cold. From the feet up. Bhatt's house stood two lanes off the ghat. The colour of tea. Marigolds in old drums along the wall — he grew them himself, it turned out. Guilt keeps a garden, like anything else keeps its habits.

His wife opened the door. Looked at Manav once. Long. The look wives keep for their husbands' unfinished business — filed away, four years ago, under *one day, someone will come.* She set out two cups. And went to the neighbour's. Which is what a wife of thirty-eight years does, when she recognizes a conversation by its shoes.

For an hour, they talked pension. Knees. The price of diesel. The town's new guest house. Manav let it run, the way he'd let a thousand counters run. The man would arrive at it himself, or the hour would do it for him.

The hour did it. The light went long on the verandah. And Bhatt refilled the cups, and sat, and folded his hands, and waited — like a man before a bench he had appointed himself to.

Manav took the pad of yellow squares out of his bag, and laid them on the table. In order. Face up. Without commentary.

It was the only courtroom he had ever trusted.

Bhatt put on his glasses, and read them the way he'd written the file four years ago. Carefully. At reading pace. An officer checking work he'd forgotten he'd kept honest — in all the wrong ways. He read all five. Then he took the glasses off, and laid them down on top of the squares, and said:

"Ask. I've been waiting since the biryani."

---

The first square was the knock.

"You found it in four years, and two hundred readings," Bhatt said. "I found it on day two. A papered job. I'd seen that signature eleven times in three years — from the inside. That's what you were paying me for, Sood saab. To know, instantly, whose work a thing was. It worked perfectly. I knew instantly."

He looked at the marigolds.

"Four of them were my own registered informants. I'd bought them tea, at that very corner, in August. And in October, they were in a flat in Vishwas Colony. And the file came to my desk. And the knock told me, in one line. And there was never, from that moment on, any version of my life in which I did not know."

*(pause)*

The second square was the plate.

"I never told the guard to forget. Nobody tells. That isn't how it's done. You schedule a re-examination. And then another. And the third time a man says *cannot say* — the file begins saying it for him. Asking is a way of erasing."

He turned his cup a quarter turn.

"You know this, actually. You ask for a living. Ask any claimant three times—"

And he stopped. Because he'd heard himself. And the two of them sat there, inside the shape of the mirror he'd just walked into. Until the old man inclined his head. Accepting a point. Against himself.

*(pause)*

The third square was the input.

"Unsigned, yes. Inputs are unsigned. That's what they're for. *No established syndicate in the sector.* Nine words. I killed your case with nine words, and went home, and ate."

He said it without drama. The way men say the worst true things — at a table, to the person most owed them.

"Eleven men, Sood saab. Three years, climbing a ladder that was eating villages. Actual villages. Stripped twice. I can name them. You never met those families. I did. And those families do not get a telephone call, at eleven in the morning, every year, on the day, asking how they are. Nobody has ever asked them how they are."

He was quiet a moment.

"I chose the eleven. And I'll tell you the truest sentence I own. I would choose them again. And it's a worthless sentence — because it's a sentence about a world where nobody's wife died on my arithmetic. In the world where she did — I made the same choice at eight in the morning, and I could not sleep by eight that night. And I have not slept at eight at night, in four years."

*(pause)*

The fourth square said: *Three unfitnesses. Evenly spaced. The kindness had a schedule.*

Here, Bhatt was quiet the longest.

"A child who hides like that is fit," he said, finally. "Any doctor in the state would have certified him inside a week. Children like that are fit for exactly one thing. And it is the thing you and I needed him for. I filed him unfit three times — because every week of unfitness was a week his face did not enter the file. And the third time — the third time, he sat in my office, and watched the ceiling fan, the way he watches everything. And watched me — the way one watches a fan—"

Bhatt's hands were folded very tightly now. A man holding his own hands the way you hold a stranger's.

"I was relieved. Twenty-six years of service. And God sent a seven-year-old to do my sin for me. And I signed the receipt." He looked up. "You can hate me for the trade, Sood saab. Everyone trades. If you must hate one thing — hate that I was grateful to a child."

*(pause)*

The fifth square said: *Eleven months.*

"Eleven months is what a commitment takes, in this state. I stayed for the commitment. A man should eat what he kills."

---

"Say the rest," Manav said. "The part you've been saving four years."

"You want a sentence for it. There is no sentence. There's only the arithmetic. And you do it too — every day of your quiet life. Every day you don't burn the city down, Sood saab — you're signing it too. Every man with a stamp knows it, in his bones. You've simply never had a stamp."

A pause. And the old man looked at him, with the whole of twenty-six years in his eyes. And added the one word Manav would carry home on the train, like a stone.

"Yet."

*(pause)*

They sat with that. The light went off the wall.

"And your list," Bhatt said, into the quiet, "will not come for me. You want to know if I'm afraid of it. I *want* it to. And it won't. And that is the only fact of the new weather that has made me want to walk into that river."

He turned his cup again.

"Look who it took, from your own case. And look who it left. The four were documented only in my secret ledger — so heaven took them. My eleven are documented in a charge-sheet — so heaven will feed them dal for fourteen years, and release them to their grandchildren. Documented men are safe. The list is a great believer in settlements, Sood saab. It punishes the ones the papers can't reach. It's a policeman's god. It reads what's in the file."

"Then who does it read?" Manav asked. The question of the era. Put to the one man who'd spent his life writing files.

"Nobody," Bhatt said. "That's my professional assessment. And I've made assessments my whole career. And this is the only one that ever frightened me."

*(pause)*

He got up to refill the water. Came back. And stood a moment at the rail of the verandah, his cardigan buttoned one short of the top.

"You came for the why. I'll give you the why's shape — since I cannot give you the why. The papered lists were a commodity. Somebody, upstream, was manufacturing absences. Whole seasons of empty houses. Gathered. Itemized. Sold. Three years, I climbed toward it. And I got as far as the purchase order. Never the factory. The timber shop was my last rung. Above Zorawar, the paper changes hands — in places with better chairs."

"Offices."

"Offices," Bhatt said. "I starved your file, Sood saab. And that will be on my stone. But I did not write the season that killed her. That was written at a desk."

---

The day's weight ambushed him the way sleep ambushes soldiers. The confession had been four years in arriving — and it arrived all at once. He sat back on the sofa, and was gone for twenty minutes. And woke under a chatai that had been laid over him, by hands he had not heard.

Bhatt was in the garden. Dead-heading the marigolds, in the last of the light. The man who had once stood over a clerk at a prayer meeting, saying *eat, beta, you have to eat* — now standing over a sleeping father. And letting him.

At six, they walked down to the ghat. The bells were beginning. The river was the grey of old coins. And the pilgrims were doing what pilgrims do — which is lend the hour a shape.

"Do you pray?" Manav asked him.

"No," Bhatt said. "I attend."

*(pause)*

At the lane where the paths divided — the station path, the house path — the old man buttoned his cardigan, the last button, and made his goodbye the way policemen make a goodbye. Which is a briefing.

"Sood saab. One thing. Off every file. Your boy is ten now. And ten is the age they start keeping their own ledgers. Whatever your nights are — and a man who sleeps the way you slept on my sofa does not have ordinary nights — keep him out of every notebook in that city."

He held Manav's hand one second past the handshake.

"Including the ones you keep on yourself. Children in paperwork are how cases die. I am the world's leading authority."

---

The train went down the valley with the river in its ear, for three hours.

Somewhere past the second tunnel, the phone lit. Numbered.

*C-9. Eat something. You are carrying a man's confession on an empty stomach.*

He had stopped asking how it knew where he was. That, too, had not been a decision.

He opened the file under the file — the one he kept himself in. And put Bhatt's Tuesday in it. Next to his own Sunday. And the drawer closed the way it always closed.

Which is to say — it didn't.
