# VIGILANTE — Audio Series
## Part Two · The System
### Episode 10: "The Rooftop" — Turn 2

*Narration script · single narrator · est. runtime ~14 min (~2,180 words)*
*The midpoint. The confession is the heart of the whole serial — Lakha is a plain, exhausted man, not a poet; let his lines be simple and let the silences between them carry the weight. The Taking must be reported very calmly, almost tenderly; "it edited" is the coldest idea in the episode precisely because the voice stays even. Do not rush the after. "Almost, beta" is barely spoken.*

---

Lakha straightened when the mask cleared the stairwell door. The way a man straightens for an inspection he's rehearsed.

"You came," he said. "Three years, I thought it would be bigger. It's the size of a man."

He didn't come at the mask. And he didn't run. He stood at the parapet with his hands open, the bottle set down at his feet with the care of a man who's decided to stop needing it. And he asked the era's own question. First. Before anything else. The way a man asks the doctor the real question, before the small ones.

"Are you the one who takes? Or the one who watches?"

"I'm the one who asks," the mask said.

Lakha considered that. And something in his shoulders let go. The way it lets go, in men at a counter, when the clerk — at last — asks.

---

"The job was papered," he said. "You pay extra for papered. Confirmed empty, three days. The whole block gone, for the festival. The cousin brought it. Zorawar. Timber market. He deals in confirmed-empty the way other men deal in timber. I never met the man above him. Zorawar said he had a man, whose cousin sits in an office. And the office man knew the block. That's all I ever knew. And I swear it on the dogs. That's all."

He looked at his hands.

"I held the door. I'm not telling you that as a defence. Nothing comes to a house that doesn't come through a door first. And the door was mine. The others went in. What was in the rooms — I wasn't in the rooms. I was in the hall. And the hall is what I'll tell you. Because the hall is mine to tell."

*(pause)*

"We stood at the door first, and we listened. That's how it's done. You listen for the house. A television was playing inside. And a television doesn't sound like an empty house."

He said it slowly. A man laying out his one exhibit.

"But the paper said empty till Monday. And the paper had been right before. And people leave televisions on when they go away — to make the house sound like itself. So we knocked. For the sound to stop.

"It didn't stop."

He waited. Somewhere below, the wind moved the scaffold cloth.

"An empty house pauses nothing. And a sleeping child also pauses nothing. We couldn't tell the difference. Between an empty house — and a sleeping boy."

He said it again. Quieter. As if the first time had been for the mask, and this time was for the roof.

"That was the whole night, bhai. We could not tell the difference.

"And then a cupboard clicked. Somewhere inside. Small. Like a lock."

His hands closed.

"I hear that click every night since. In the yard. At the wheel. You cannot know what a small sound costs."

*(pause)*

"The girl said something to the cupboard. I didn't hear the words. I heard the tone. It was a mother's tone. From a girl."

He looked at the mask then. And Manav asked — in the voice with no weather in it — the only question he had come for.

"Who gave Zorawar the paper?"

"Zorawar said the office-wala knew the family. Knew the house. He'd done the timings himself — not a guess, *timings* — puja, right through to Monday. Zorawar said the office-wala knew the man of the house personally. He knew his —"

*(beat)*

At 4:31, between one syllable and the next, the ears went first.

---

A pressure. Like a train arriving, somewhere behind the sky.

The gravel moved. Not scattered — leaned. All of it. One grain deep. Toward the stairwell door. The way iron filings lean toward a stone they've already agreed to. The plastic bag from the bottle stood up off the gravel, empty, at attention. The sodium light on the crane didn't flicker. It hummed a different note. A note with work in it.

And between that hum and the next — Lakha stopped.

Not fell. Stopped. The way a sentence stops when the page is lifted out of the typewriter.

His slippers stayed where his feet had been. Side by side. Squared to the parapet. His clothes let go of him in the order clothes go — collar last — like a coat off a hook when the hook has been taken away. And the wind that was not wind — the using wind — folded the kurta once. Tidily. And was done.

The roof held what was left. Gravel. Slippers. A bottle. A folded kurta. Trousers, arranged the way a man arranges trousers on a chair before sleeping. Nothing else. Nothing of him, else.

The order of it was the worst part. And Manav understood the era's whole aesthetic — at 4:31, on a gravel roof, at the edge of the city.

It did not destroy.

It edited.

---

He stood a long time. Three years of violence in him. And nowhere to put it.

The sentence hung in the air, where the man had been. *He knew the man of the house personally. He knew his —* Eight words of a name. A name that would have explained the paper, the timings, the empty-until-Monday. And heaven had taken the rest of the sentence, with the rest of the man.

He'd known — since the mandi, since the weighbridge clerk and the shoe — that the era would come for the four. He'd been racing it. He'd just believed the race was long.

He threw the bottle off the roof. As far as the arm could ask. It was the only violence the night had left him. And it was nothing. And it didn't even make a sound.

---

He rode the cycle to the yard before the loaders came, and fed the dogs from the bag — at watching distance from the shutter — and left the tempo where the tempo was.

It wasn't his mercy to give. It was a debt transfer. The night city runs on them.

---

The city woke to three.

One in the godman's compound, mid-aarti — the diya still burning, in the space where a hand had been holding it. The image of the summer. Maybe the decade. One in a card room, outside Panipat. And one — unpublic, on a roof — known only to a mask, and a wind.

One minute, the anchors kept saying. Checking and rechecking the timestamps. One minute. Three men.

The receipts came by Thursday. For the card room: a tally book. Twelve years of it. For the temple: the compound's collection ledger — the second book. The one the godman's own accountants didn't know the godman kept. And for the man on the roof: pawn slips. Gold. Hallmarked. The items listed, with dates. The branch. The weights. The winter of that year.

He did not read past the third item.

*(pause)*

By Friday, the city knew whose night the receipts had come from. VISHWAS COLONY CASE CLOSED BY HEAVEN, one channel put it — and was prouder of the phrasing than of anything else it did that summer. The society sent a card to the flat. It said the matter had been resolved. People stood an extra second or two at the gate when he passed. And the tea superstition at the office came back, in a warmer form. And none of it — not one line of it — contained a single word of *why*.

The list had done, in one minute, what three years of the law had not. And it had done it the one way he had never wanted it done. As a verdict, with no reasons attached. The receipts said *what*. Heaven, it turned out, also rejected the claim of explanation.

The scripture got its new verse. The mask had found them, the forwards said, and the list had followed. HE FINDS THEM. HE TAKES THEM HOME. He read it at the kitchen table, at one in the morning, and could not sign the correction. And the correction sat in him all night. Like a sentence with no page.

*(pause)*

At dinner, the boy put his spoon down.

"Papa. They took the men who did that night."

"Yes."

"Is it over now?"

"Almost, beta."

The boy nodded. The way you nod when a claim's been assessed, and passed. And Manav could not see the file behind the passing. And did not try.

Some drawers are stronger than the man who built them.

---

For six nights, the phone said nothing. The silence had a new taste to it. Not the bench-taste of waiting — but the taste of a thing that had watched its instrument survive an operation, and was letting the meat remember.

Then, at two in the morning. Numbered.

*C-8. The thread did not break. Zorawar, timber market. When you are ready.*

For the first time, Manav thought about the canal. About the phone's small weight. The black water. The whole relationship, finished in one arc of the arm.

He walked to the footbridge, and stood at the rail a long time, the phone warm in his fist.

He did not throw it.

And standing there, not throwing it, he understood that the not-throwing was information too. And that the thing at the other end of it — whatever it was — had modelled the not-throwing, before he'd even reached the bridge.

---

The old arithmetic was over. Four men had organized his life. And one minute had organized the four. And what was left was not a case. It had never been a case. And he'd known that — in the file under the file — since a night at a chain door, in Block C.

He stood at the kitchen window, with the city's doors stacked away into the dark. Thousands of them. Each one with somebody's Tuesday behind it. The list working through them, at its own pace. Tidily. The law slower than the list. The list answerable to no one.

And no one — he understood this now with the whole of his body, which was the only organ that had ever told him the truth — no one was coming to any of them in time.

He had learned the night to find four men.

The night was full of doors.
