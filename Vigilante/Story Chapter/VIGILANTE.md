# VIGILANTE

*a novel in three parts*

---

## PART ONE — THE HOUSE

*the fall, the birth of the mask*

### Chapter One: Elsewhere


The first thing you learn in claims is that everyone grieves differently and everyone lies the same way. The second thing you learn is that the lie is never about money. Money is only where people keep their other things.

Manav Sood had been reading loss across a desk for twenty-one years, the last eleven of them at Hansa Assurance, Arjun Nagar branch, Tejgarh. His cabin had a window that faced the car park and a door that faced everybody else's worst day, and he was good at the work in the way quiet men are good at work that is mostly listening. He could hear a false receipt at forty paces. He knew which silence meant mourning and which silence meant arithmetic. Since the thing that had happened, his colleagues had developed a superstition about offering him tea.

The lights went up in Vishwas Colony in the third week of October — the same wire, the same decorators, the same colors around the gate and the parking pillars as every year, so that at night the society looked like a place celebrating something. By the fourth day the block would begin to empty: everyone away to relatives in the older parts of the city, the flats going dark one by one over the week, like a tide going out. It happened every Diwali.

It had happened that year too.

Two years gone now, and there were still sentences that arrived with their original weight, dropping through whatever he had built on top of them. He had become, in the language of his profession, careful.

---

On the Monday, breakfast was eggs and the television. The television was always on — Aarav kept it on the way other children keep a nightlight, not watching it, just living inside the sound of it — and Manav had stopped turning it down. There were things in that house that had been decided without a meeting. The volume of the television was one of them.

Somewhere in the middle of a cartoon the boy laughed — really laughed, suddenly, at nothing Manav could find, a door flung open in a house that stood shut — and Manav kept his back to him, buttering a second paratha nobody had asked for, because of what his face did when the laugh came. It was the one thing in the flat that still worked at full power.

Ira's school shoes were still by the door. For the first six months he had kept moving them — to the rack, to her room, once into a carton he had not been able to seal — and then he had stopped, and they had stayed, and now they were furniture. A house is allowed a few arrangements it cannot explain.

The school van leaned on its horn at 7:20, the same two notes as always. Manav asked the four questions — did you sleep, tiffin, homework, bottle — and received the four answers, in the order the questions came. None of the eight was true. It was how the two of them said good morning now, and it was load-bearing. At the door Aarav stepped around his sister's shoes without looking at them, which he had also done for two years, and which Manav had also stopped noticing. Mostly.

---

That week the desk held two fires, a flood the surveyor had stamped *act of God*, and a jeweller's burglary that smelled, from the first page, like the jeweller. He processed the fires, queried the flood, and put the jeweller aside for a second reading. Every file, he had learned in twenty-one years, has one page that has been rewritten. Find the page and you have found the claim.

In two years he had not once turned that instrument on the one file that was actually his. The police had called it a burglary gone wrong. He had read the phrase four hundred times, the way you read a word until it stops being one. Wrong house, wrong night. Robbery crew. Untraced.

Rakesh Sethi came by at four with his hands in his pockets, did his impression of a man happening past, and set an envelope on the desk. "Diwali. For the boy," he said. "From the floor." The floor had never signed anything. There was too much money in it; there was too much money in it every year. Sethi laughed at nothing on his way out, a laugh that started and then thought better of itself.

It had been Sethi, that first year, who kept Manav's name off the list when the branch trimmed — Sethi who said *you have enough on your plate, Sood saab, let me handle the office* — and who had handled the office ever since. In two years he had never once asked about that night. Not a syllable, not one *how are you* that leaned too far. Manav had always supposed that was tact.

---

They ate dinner with a single cracker going off early, somewhere in the lane — one, then nothing. Neither of them looked up. Not looking up was a thing they had both gotten good at.

The second bedroom stayed shut. Nobody had ever announced this; the door had simply stopped opening. Inside it were a poster, a half-read library book, a chart of the solar system she had been too bored to finish, and two years of nobody's dust, and once a month he went in with a cloth. He had told himself he would stop when it stopped helping. It had not stopped helping, whatever it was doing.

In the top drawer of the cupboard, her purse still held the list for that weekend, folded twice in Meera's handwriting — *gift for Bua, Ira's syrup, charge the camera* — the stationery of a trip that was never taken. He had not been able to move the purse. The list had outlived its reason. He had not been able to move the list either.

---

At night the arithmetic came. It came every night, punctual as a creditor. If the fever had broken two hours earlier, the four of them would have gone on the Thursday, and the flat would have stood dark and right, and whoever came that night would have found what the police said they had come for: nobody. If the interview had been on the Tuesday. If he had taken the 6:20 and not the 8:40. If he had simply not gone. There were two thousand ways to run the sum and every one of them came out to the same word: *elsewhere.* He had been elsewhere. Not in a bar, not with anyone, not anywhere a confessor could get purchase — elsewhere the way a good man is elsewhere, in a hotel with an ironed shirt, improving the family's position.

There is no penance shaped like that. You cannot repent a promotion.

*Sunday evening, the airport, her voice small under the announcements. The fever's coming down, I think. Ninety-nine and a bit. The geyser — switch it off before your bath, it trips. You worry like it's your job, Manav. Love you, bye.* He had said *love you, bye*. He had said it the way you stamp a receipt without reading what's under your hand. And then, because the announcement had taken whatever he said first: *Haan, fine. Sleep. I'll call after the meeting.*

He had not called after the meeting.

*He had put the phone on silent because of the morning's meeting. At 11:52 it lit, and went on lighting — the neighbor, the second neighbor, the society guard, numbers he did not know — and he slept through all seventeen, the sleep of a man with an interview, and the hotel phone did not ring until 6:30. A call for you, sir. And then a voice explaining the world very slowly.*

*They served breakfast on the flight back. He had no memory of eating it. He remembered the tray going away empty, and some part of him — the part that was still a claims officer — noting it for the file: subject was able to eat. Subject's hands remembered how.*

*The hospital week. Green paint. Crackers going off outside the window for all four days, the city celebrating something. Ira surfaced twice. Both times the same sentence, the second time slower, both times her eyes going past him, to the door: Aarav ne kuch nahin dekha. Aarav didn't see anything. The first time he had said yes, beta, of course, he was asleep, he saw nothing. She had looked at the door a moment longer. Then she had gone back under, and had not come up again.*

She would be fifteen in March. She had been thirteen for two years.

He had carried her sentence as mercy. That was the kind of sister she was — the kind who spends the one breath she is given building a wall around her brother's sleep. He had filed the sentence under mercy, and it was the file he could not keep shut. Every night it reopened itself quietly, like a door in a draught.

The police file had said: *the minor male child was found asleep.* Asleep. Through all of it. The file was certain. The file was kind.

Inspector Bhatt called every year on the day itself. Not before, not after: at eleven in the morning, for four minutes, the two of them saying *we are still here* to each other in the voices of men discussing weather. He had retired eleven months after the case closed — voluntary, people said, good man, put in his years. Eleven months. The number meant nothing. It arrived anyway, every year, with the call.

At the shok-sabha, in the days when the flat was full of people saying the wrong beautiful things, a heavyset officer Manav had never met before had set a packet of biryani in front of him and stood over him while he ate, saying nothing, waving the others off. *Eat, beta. You have to eat.* Manav had thought: this is what kindness looks like in a uniform. He had thought it gratefully. He had been so tired, and the biryani had been warm.

---

The week went the way the weeks went. And then it was Thursday — because it was on a Thursday night, two years and some days after a Sunday, that the rest of it began.

Aarav was lying on the sofa in the hall with the television on mute, turning a Spider-Man figure over in his hands. One of its boots was missing; he had got it in a school trade for two pens and a sticker, and it was currently his most valuable possession. The boy slept on the sofa. That had started the first month — the second bedroom shut, his own room a room he mostly did not sleep in — and Manav had let it become geography. There were things in that house decided without a meeting.

"Why does he wear it?" Aarav said, not looking up. "The mask."

"So nobody knows who he is."

"Why can't nobody know?"

Manav took a breath that he tried to make sound like nothing. "Because if the wrong people found out who he was, they couldn't get to him — he's too strong. So they'd go for the people he loves instead. They'd use them to reach him."

Aarav considered this with the seriousness the nine-year-old now brought to everything — this new, grown seriousness that had arrived in the same week as everything else and had never left. He stood the figure on his chest.

"Even his maa doesn't know?"

And Manav opened his mouth to say the easy thing — *of course she knows, mothers know everything* — and found that the easy thing was not in the room. The television threw its muted colors across the ceiling. Down the hall, Ira's shoes stood by the door in the dark, keeping their post.

"No," he said. "Not even her."

Aarav nodded slowly. He turned the figure over once more, working the mathematics of it through.

"That's smart," he said, to the ceiling. "Otherwise they'd just go to his house."

"Yes."

"That's the smartest part."

And then he was asleep within minutes, quickly and completely, the way he was always asleep within minutes — and Manav sat in the dark of the hall and understood that in two years of answering every question with the softest available truth, this was the first time he had told his son how the world actually worked. And the world, as described, had a shape. He was surprised how well the shape fit him.

He sat in the dark and let it be measured.

---

He did not go to bed. Some hours later he was in the kitchen with the stove light on and the photograph out of the drawer under the telephone directories — not the framed one from the studio, but the other one, the last one with all four of them. A selfie Ira had taken on some nothing afternoon: everybody half-ready, Meera mid-word, Aarav's thumb over one corner, Ira's laughing face filling half the frame at something outside it. Nobody is looking at the camera. It is the only photograph of the four of them that is entirely true.

He ran the arithmetic once more, out of loyalty. If the fever. If the 6:20. If not gone. And somewhere in the middle of it, without ceremony, the sums stopped running backward. He noticed the silence they left the way you notice a fan cutting out — the air still there, but changed.

For two years, every number had faced yesterday. Tonight the sum faced forward. *If they are never found — who finds them?*

The question did not frighten him. That was what frightened him.

He stood for a long time with his hand on the cold steel of the counter. Then he went into the bedroom and took down from the top of the cupboard Meera's sewing box — the tin one, with the peeling painting of a woman on the lid that she had never thrown away because, she said, the woman had done nothing wrong.

He set it on the kitchen table beside the photograph and opened it. Her scissors. Her measuring tape. The bent crock of pins. And the needle — the needle was where she had left it, still threaded, a length of grey-blue cotton through its eye, as if she had only stepped out of the room and meant, when she came back, to finish whatever it was she had been mending.

He did not switch on the big light.

The small one was enough.

### Chapter Two: The Body


In the morning the sewing box went back on top of the cupboard, where it had always lived.

He had reached for the last thing first, in the night — reached for the mask the way a child reaches for the toy and not the homework. It could wait. It had waited two years already. What could not wait was older than the mask and simpler: nothing in his life, from the school run to the office to the four questions at breakfast, had asked his body for anything in two years. A man cannot go where he was going on the body of a claims officer.

He understood this the way he understood repudiation letters: without any hope that understanding would help.

The geyser tripped while he showered, the way it tripped every morning, and he stood dripping in the passage and pressed the switch down again. It had tripped for two years. He had never had it seen to. The electrician was expensive — that was the story he told himself, and he was aware, in the part of him that stamped things, that it was a claim he would not have paid.

At the office, Friday closed itself the way files close: a small signature, no sound.

---

Twenty-one years of surveys lived in the steel cupboard of his study, in field diaries — cloth-bound in the years when the office still bought cloth, plastic after. A surveyor's hand: addresses, meters, staircases, faces. He had never once read them as anything except work.

On Saturday night he took them down and read them as a map.

There was the bouncer's jaw, X-rayed like a moon. The pawnshop robbed by the owner's own son, the son sixteen, the father weeping in numbers. The taxi driver beaten over a forty-rupee fare, who had held his torn shirt closed at the station like a coat at a wedding. Twenty-one years, and it turned out he had been walking the city's underside the whole time with a lantern, calling it a job, and the underside had waved back.

The entry he was looking for did not announce itself. He passed it twice before he stopped.

*11 March 2011. Fire. New Model Boxing Club, Bhagwanpura. Origin: meter room, old wiring. Office line: negligence — repudiate. I climbed and saw the meter myself. Paid.*

The office had wanted that fire to be the club's fault. The office had sent letters saying so, and then, for reasons the file did not record, the office had sent a man who climbed the stairs to the meter room and looked with his own eyes, and the claim had come, and the club had come back.

He put the diary on the kitchen table beside the cold tea and looked at the word *Bhagwanpura* for a long time. It was the only address in twenty-one years where somebody had taught other people to be hit, and to go on.

---

Diwali came the way it always came now, which was at him.

The society's function happened at the gate without them; it had happened without them for two years, and the notices kept being politely delivered, and he kept folding them into the drawer where the society's faith in them lived. On the night itself, Aarav asked for the small lights — not crackers, he said, *the boring kind* — and Manav heard his own yes come out before his decision did, the way it does when a boy asks for something small after a long time of asking for nothing.

They lit six along the balcony rail. Down in the lanes the city was already detonating, gently, block by block, the week of her death being celebrated on schedule. The boy watched the six small flames for a while, and then watched the sky, and then went in to the television, which was where he slept now, and Manav sat on the balcony with the tea going cold and did not do the arithmetic, because the arithmetic had a new job, and the new job faced forward.

Inspector Bhatt called on the day itself, at eleven, as he had called at eleven the year before.

For four minutes they were two men discussing weather they were not standing in. There were no gaps in it. Four minutes is a long time to keep a net tight, and Bhatt had kept it tight two years running.

"Thank you for calling, Inspector."

"Someone should," Bhatt said, "on the day."

"It's kind of you."

"If anything had ever come up," Bhatt said, "you would have been the first to know, Sood saab. First."

It was a kind sentence. It had also, somewhere in the middle of it, been notarized. Manav heard the stamp come down — heard it at forty paces, the way he had heard such stamps across desks for twenty-one years — and could not have said what exactly in it was false. Only that the signature did not match the signer.

"We are still here," he did not say, this year.

Afterward he stood at the window while the crackers went on outside, and thought about the call the way he had once thought about claims: turning the document over, feeling for the seam. It was the first time in two years that his instrument had swung toward home. It moved all by itself, like a needle finding north, and he stood very still and let it point, and did not yet ask at what.

---

The old quarter began where the numbered sectors gave up. Bhagwanpura was smoke and cycle-repair shops and winter light lying down in the lanes, a district where every third shop fixed something. The address was a staircase going down from a lane of motor-wallahs, under a painted board so faded the club's name had to be taken on faith: NEW MODEL BOXING CLUB, established on a date the paint had taken with it.

On the landing, a shelf. On the shelf, a dozen phones lying face-down like confiscated things.

The club was one bulb, a ring older than the boys using it, and a smell of liniment, damp, and old smoke that had given up trying to leave. The man holding the pads was sixty-eight and moved like a much younger man's memory. He had a name — Iqbal — but nobody had used it since the railways. Everyone said Ustad.

"You want?" Ustad said.

"To join."

"You are forty-four."

"I'm aware."

Ustad looked at his shoes, which were office shoes, and let the shoes say the rest.

"There was a fire," Manav said. "2011. A survey."

Something changed in the old man's face — not softening; the opposite of softening, whatever that is in a man who has decided to take something seriously. "They sent letters calling my meter negligence," he said. "Then they sent a man who climbed and looked. The claim came. The club came back." He set the pads down on the ring apron. "You are that man."

"I am that man."

"That man is owed a favor by this basement." Ustad picked the pads up again. "First month, free. After that you pay in pad-holding and mopping. And there is a rule. Whatever you are carrying stays on the landing, with the phones. Everyone leaves something on the stairs."

"And if I can't leave it?"

"Then it trains with you," Ustad said, "and it hits worse than I do."

The boys went back to the ring. Manav stood a moment by the shelf of sleeping phones.

"What are you carrying?" Ustad asked, not turning around. He asked it the way a man asks a question he has asked ten thousand times. "Everyone carries something down those stairs."

"A file," Manav said.

"Leave it on the landing."

"It doesn't open."

Ustad nodded, once, the nod of a man who has heard forty years of men describe the one door in them that will not open, and had never once, in forty years, been surprised by what was behind it. "Then it stays on the landing anyway," he said. "That is the rule. We don't open them here. We skip rope."

---

The weeks went by in the order weeks do.

He skipped rope badly, and then less badly. He learned the wraps from a boy's phone, at midnight, in a language he half-followed — and it struck him, tying the first one, that a wrap done right is a bandage for a hand that has not been hurt yet, which made it the most honest insurance he had ever seen. He held pads for boys a quarter of his age who thanked him with their eyes the way young men do, everything, nothing. He mopped. He was not too proud to mop, having spent two years discovering exactly how much of pride is just noise a body makes when it is idle.

"What are you training for?" Ustad asked one night, not looking at him.

"To be able to walk at night."

Ustad nodded as if this were the first true thing anyone had said in the basement that week. "Everyone your age who comes down those stairs is training for one man," he said. "Nine out of ten, it is himself."

Manav said nothing. The count in his head said otherwise.

One night Ustad put the mitts on himself. "We will see your first minute," he said, and Manav learned that the first minute of being hit is its own country, with its own weather. The mitt went in under his ribs with a sound he heard half a second late, and the floor came up through the canvas, and the room rang like a struck bowl.

He lay there for the count. Ten numbers, out loud, in order, at a walking pace. It was the first honest arithmetic he had done in two years — numbers that faced him, numbers with nowhere to hide. Ten, and the ringing thinned, and he got up.

"Paper forgets," Ustad said, above him, already unwinding. "Meat remembers. Again?"

"Again."

It was, though he did not have the word for it yet, worship. Every other hour of his life was grief arranged in the shapes of living — breakfast, tiffin, bottle, four questions, four answers — and these two hours in the basement were the only place anything was allowed to be simply, stupidly true. The rope counted. The bag did not lie about what he weighed. His body kept a ledger of its own and was never wrong, and every night it paid out a little more than he thought he had.

Sunny, nineteen, who held pads like he was being paid to and mopped like he wasn't, watched Manav watching the room one night and said, "Uncle, you look at people like you're counting them."

"Occupational hazard," Manav said, which was true, and not the joke Sunny took it for.

---

The costs came the way premiums do — small, on a schedule, easily paid and never once free.

He came home at half past midnight with his knuckles wrapped under his sleeves, and once a fortnight with a mark somewhere he had learned to place where his shirt went. The office noticed only that the tea superstition had faded, and that his files were closing faster. The jeweller's burglary closed in November — the jeweller confessed in the middle of a second interview, almost with relief, the way they almost always did — and Manav wrote the repudiation with something close to tenderness. Everyone, in the end, wants the file closed.

Almost everyone.

Sethi saw the split knuckle once, at the water cooler, and asked about the cricket instead. It was, as ever, a complete tact, and Manav answered about the cricket, and the water cooler did what water coolers do, which is stand there.

"I joined a gym," he told Aarav, over dinner, in the tone men use for confessing a virtue.

Aarav looked up from his plate. "Good," he said. "You and Mama always said the stairs—" and stopped, and put another mouthful in.

"The stairs," Manav agreed, after a moment, and they finished dinner inside that half of the sentence, where it was warm.

Half past midnight, coming home: the hall television murmuring at its lowest number, the boy asleep inside the noise of it, one arm off the sofa. Manav stood in the doorway with his bag and watched his son sleep inside the sound, and did not turn it off, and went and washed before the morning could ask questions.

---

December, and the cold came into the flats like a tenant with a lease.

Her cupboard opened quietly, the way cupboards do when nobody has opened them in a season. On the shelf, folded the way she had folded everything — in thirds, like letters she had decided not to send — the dupattas. The grey-blue one was third from the left. In the photograph, the last true one, it is bunched in her fist, mid-word; she held things in her fist when she was scolding somebody she loved.

He took it down. The cloth held the cold of the shelf, and then, for a moment, the warmth of his hands, and he sat on the edge of the bed with it and let it be a whole winter's grief.

Then he stood up, because that was not what it was for.

He worked at the kitchen table after midnight, with one earbud in and a man from somewhere south of the language teaching masks to strangers at three in the morning. The first eye-holes came out too far apart, a fish's idea of a face, and he unpicked them and did them again. Her needle kept her thread; he used it to the end, and then her spare. The stitches went in crooked, then straighter, then crooked again whenever his mind went anywhere else, so that the mask, for anyone who could read sewing — and who in this city could not — was a record of where his mind had been allowed to go, and where it had not.

It took nine nights.

On the ninth, at two in the morning, in the bathroom light, he put it on and looked in the mirror.

The man in the mirror was nobody's husband, nobody's father, nobody's grief. For two years, every eye that had found Manav Sood had found the thing that had happened to him first — the office, the society, the tea. The mask had no history. Nothing looked at it and saw the night. Nothing looked at it at all.

It was terrible. He wore it anyway.

He stood a while at the front door with the chain in his hand, and the city went by under the window — one scooter, a dog with opinions, the late vegetable cart — and then he put the chain back. The body was not ready. The mask could not carry it alone.

Whatever this was going to be called, it did not have a name yet. It had a body first.

He folded the mask in thirds, the way she folded everything — his hands had learned it somewhere without asking him — and put it in his office bag, between two closed files, where nobody in the world opens anything.

### Chapter Three: First Night


The mask left the flat the way it had entered the office bag: quietly, between things.

At one in the morning the hall television was still murmuring at its lowest number, and the boy slept inside the noise of it, and Manav stood in the doorway with his boots on and the bag over his shoulder, doing the arithmetic he now allowed himself. School van, 7:20. Office, 9:40. The gym closed on Sundays. A man could be back by four.

He had told himself he would only look at the night. Not go into it. Look.

Two lanes from home, in the dark between parked scooters, kneeling as if to a shoelace, he put it on for the street. The city arrived slightly too wide, in stereo, through eye-holes a fish would have wanted adjusted. His breath came back at him warm off the cloth. Cold went in at the jaw. For the first hundred meters the mask was all he could think about; by the second hundred the city had swallowed it, and him, and that was the night's first lesson: nobody was looking. The night had room.

---

Under the canal flyover, the vegetable market stood down, and in its vacancy a second market had set up. Farid's thela under a blue tarpaulin. A kettle. A transistor radio playing songs at the volume of memory. Truck men on short leave from their routes. Two watchmen sharing a bench and a silence. A boy with newspaper bundles who ate three biscuits and paid for one cup with the seriousness of a man closing a merger.

The city's other shift, the one the day never counted, doing its rounds. Manav sat at the end of the bench with his back to the tarpaulin wall and was furniture in it.

Across the lane stood the paan counter that was not only a paan counter: a lit doorway, a staircase going up, men entering with the walk of people who have already decided and leaving with other walks. Three weeks of colleagues' burglary files had got him the bench. The Diwali-week signature — blocks emptied for the festival, flats hit in the first days of the break — went back four years, and the office had a name for it the way offices have names, which is a code in a column, meaning nothing to anyone in a position to act on it. He had taken the code and read the claims the way he had once read the field diaries: as a map. Sixteen houses in four years, one colony after another around the ring. And in a November claim two streets from Vishwas Colony, a neighbor's statement nobody had followed up — *a blue scooter with a taped mirror, seen twice in the week before.*

He had found a blue scooter with a taped mirror outside this paan counter on a Tuesday in January, and had sat on Farid's bench every second night since, being a customer. Learning, in the language of the muscles of his neck, to hold stillness ninety seconds at a time. Nobody had told him. His neck had told him.

Tonight, at half past one, the scooter had a rider.

---

He followed on foot and did everything wrong, gracefully, like a man performing a dance he had only read about. He crossed when the rider crossed. He stood when the rider stood. He checked the phone-light twice for a street that did not need checking. The rider walked the scooter its first hundred meters, as they do in lanes, and Manav held his distance behind a shuttered egg-wallah's, and the distance was honest, and then the rider leaned the scooter to the wall where the flyover's dark began, and went into the dark on foot with a wrapped bundle under his arm, and Manav followed him into it, and this — he would file it later, in the section for findings — this was the moment the claim went bad.

The underpass at that hour is a place light has agreed to give up. He heard the man's footsteps stop, and then a voice, unhurried, saying a name into the dark — "Chotu. Chotu aa gaya" — and the dark produced company.

There were three of them, and one of him, and the first minute went almost to plan. He got his arms up the way the basement had taught him; he turned the shoulder the way the basement had taught him; he even heard Ustad's breath-count in his ear, paper forgetting while meat remembered. The problem was the second minute. Nobody trains the second minute. The mitt under the ribs he knew from the basement — but the mitt stops, and the street does not, and the shot went into his left side like a letter opener and took his legs out from under him, and after that the night was mostly geography. A boot. The taste of coins. The flyover's ceiling, far away, ridged like a sheet.

One of them took hold of the mask.

He held it the way the drowning hold wood. He could not have said why, with three men on him and the arithmetic of the situation advising surrender of everything — wallet, phone, the shoes — the one fact he would not let the night have was the cloth on his face. He curled around it and around his ribs, and took what there was to take on his back and his arms, and somewhere in it he understood, with the strange calm of a man reading his own claim file as it is being written: *they are beating a mask.* Not a man. There is no name anywhere on a mask. No address, no society, no son. Whatever else the night did to him, it could not do the one thing that mattered.

Then, from the mouth of the underpass, at parade volume, a kettle announced itself.

"Chai! Garam chai! Oye, thand hai, chai piyo—!"

Farid did not cross the road. He did not run at anyone. He stood where his cart stood, under his own lamp, and made the dark a shopfront: calling, pouring, clinking cup on cup, being *seen* at industrial strength. Three men whose work requires the opposite of attention did their arithmetic — the street's arithmetic, older than the city — and were gone into it, taking nothing, because a beating interrupted is a debt uncollected, and debts like that are never forgotten, only deferred.

The underpass got its echo back. Farid's radio played on, at the volume of memory.

---

Farid cleaned him at the back of the thela with the water he had been saving for tea, which Manav understood was a thing, and did not say. He worked the way he poured: without comment, without hurry, warm cloth, one pass, done. The lamp hissed. The ribs on the left had closed like a fist and would not be asked to help with anything for a while.

"You are not police," Farid said. It was not a question, and it was not the beginning of a conversation; it was a line item.

"No," Manav said.

Farid nodded, as if a small accounting question had settled itself, and poured.

At the end, Manav took out his wallet. Farid looked at it the way you look at a document in the wrong language.

"First chai free," he said. "After that, you pay by sitting where I can see you."

Then he wiped the counter where the blood had got to it, in the manner of a man who had wiped worse, or would, and turned up the radio half a number, and the night resumed its rounds.

Manav walked home inside the city's first shift. Milk cans on a cycle. Newspaper bundles at the society gate, warm as bread. A scooter carrying two lives somewhere early. The audit came on him around the second kilometer, the way audits do: politely, at an inconvenient hour.

Findings, if a colleague had signed them: *subject proceeded on a scooter, a taped mirror, and a feeling. No corroboration. No second source. The accused is a runner for a betting room, not, on any evidence shown, a housebreaker; and the scooter may be any scooter. Claim repudiated. Cause of loss: own negligence.*

Any assessor in Tejgarh would have thrown it out. He would have thrown it out. He had gone out with a thread and called it a case, and the thread had snapped back and opened his ribs.

And underneath the findings, in the space where assessors write the part they know and do not say: the mask had worked. Beaten, emptied, seventy rupees lighter in chai — no one had asked his name. There was no claim number on him anywhere. The fact of it sat under his ribs on the side opposite the pain, and kept him walking the last kilometer home.

---

The school van came at 7:20 with its two notes. He had the compress off and the story on by then.

"Stairs," he said, in answer to the boy's looking. "The society stairs. Carrying the bucket."

Aarav looked at the cheek the way he looked at everything now. Entirely.

"You and Mama always said the stairs," he said, and went in to the television for his cereal.

Manav stood in the kitchen a while after the van had gone. He had been unable to finish that sentence, three months ago, at dinner. The boy had finished it for him this morning, at the top of the stairs, with the bucket in it, and had paid something small to do it — you could hear the toll in the flat voice — and there was no box in any of Manav's files for what that meant. He called the office and told Sethi about something he had eaten. Sethi told him to take the week, said *I'll handle the office* the way he had handled the office for two years, and Manav lay on his good side and let the boy's television come through the wall, and slept four hours without the arithmetic coming even once, which the body had arranged without him.

---

It was in the canteen, that week, on page seven, between a wedding and a municipal notice: **FINANCIER MISSING FROM KISHANGANJ.** Police were looking, the brief said, at his debts. A shop shuttered since Tuesday night. Neighbors believed he had gone to his village. No one had complained.

Six lines. He read all six, because he read everything now, and filed them under nothing, and turned the page, and the page, as pages do, went on.

---

Friday, when the ribs had gone from a fist to a held breath, he was back on the bench at half past one, sitting where he could be seen. Farid poured without being asked and set the cup down and said nothing, and the paan counter went on with its lit doorway and its two kinds of walk, and the night went on being roomy.

He had wanted the night to give him a man. It had given him a bench instead.

He sat, and drank slowly, and began at the age of forty-four to learn the trade he had actually come for: waiting.

### Chapter Four: First Success


Between Block B and Block C ran a gully three feet wide, built for air and used, like everything built for air in that city, for sound.

He had been hearing it since November, the way everyone had been hearing it since November, which is to say officially not at all. A burst. A lull. In the lulls, a television. Domestic violence, it turns out, has a rhythm, the way rain has a rhythm, and a building learns it the way hills learn a river: nothing to be done, the shape is simply the country's. On the nights he was home to hear it, Manav lay on his good side and listened with the whole city block and did what the whole city block did, which was to certify the sound as weather.

The man was Deepak-ji, second floor, the flat whose window came second from the gully end. Accounts man on the block committee — the society trusted him with its money, its receipts, its Diwali fund. A certified man. He did not shout; that was the terrible thing. What came through the gully was administrative. Somewhere in those rooms was a system, patient, itemized, and in the lulls the television played for the neighbors.

He did not know her name. He knew Deepak-ji's name; everyone did. That is how that works.

What finally moved him, on a Tuesday in February, was not her sound. The rhythm changed — burst, lull, burst, and then in a lull a third sound, small, a child's voice cut short like a thread — and he was off the bed with his heart going before his mind had voted. It was not her sound that moved him, and he knew what that said about him, and where he filed it, and he got the bag.

---

The society's one camera watched the main gate with the fixed devotion of the poorly mounted. He had studied its aim the way he studied everything now, without deciding to, and he went out the long way, by the water tank, where the camera's faith did not extend. Block C's stairwell door had not latched since the intercom died — the building's whole security system, one broken latch, faithful as everything else.

Second floor. Second door from the gully end. Behind the inner door of the flat, a study lamp burned. Exams, the lamp said. February. Boys study through everything; it is the one thing adults never believe.

He knocked.

The door opened two inches, to the chain. A vest, a calm face, a dish towel over one shoulder. Clean eyes. Sober eyes. The worst news yet.

"Kya chahiye?"

"We can hear it," the mask said. "From C. All of us."

It was the first sentence the mask ever spoke in the world, and every word of it was false except the hearing. There was no all. There was no us. There was one man in a dead woman's cloth on a landing at two in the morning, borrowing a plural because the singular was unbearable.

Deepak-ji looked at the mask the way a treasurer looks at an unaudited entry. "There is no sound," he said, and men of that size of confidence do not call for help; help is for other people. He shut the chain and slid it and opened the door to take the intruder's collar, and the night moved into the flat.

It went to plan for exactly the first minute. The body did what the basement had put in it: weight forward, inside the arms, the shoulders doing their arithmetic. Deepak-ji was strong the way committee men are strong — from carrying certified things — and he got one hand on the counter and came back with the kitchen knife, and here the night paused for its examination question: the knife moved, and Manav went in under it rather than back, and took a line of fire along the left forearm, and the blade caught the mask's jaw, cloth only, a seam's width — and then the knife was on the floor and the man was folded over the sink and the room was over except for the part that wasn't.

Nobody trains the second minute. The second minute is where you find out what you came for.

He found out. He hit the folded man twice more than the fight asked for, and would have gone to three, and it was not rage exactly — rage is hot and this had no temperature at all — and then the woman screamed at him to stop, one word, twice, and the word was not for Deepak-ji. It was for him. She was between them with her arm out, guarding the man who beat her, positioned like the last wall of a fort that had chosen its side, and the room went still with the lamp burning behind the inner door and the boy standing in it.

Seven or eight. Eyes entirely open. The boy did not scream either. The boy watched him the way children watch when they are filing the world for later, and then stepped back, and closed the inner door — softly, one hinge-squeak, the way doors are closed in houses where closing doors quietly is a studied skill.

The woman's mouth was bleeding at the corner; the room was squared and gleaming, cushions squared, show-cabinet glass shining, a flat kept the way flats are kept when the world is invited in; the violence was already back in its drawer. She stood in the middle of her cage with her arm out and her breath going, forty-one years old perhaps, protecting it.

"I'll call the police," Manav said.

"And then what?"

He had prepared nothing after that. There is nothing after that. The nothing after *then* was the exact country he had been living in for two years — she was only quoting his own file back to him, and she had the better of the argument, and both of them knew it, and Deepak-ji, getting to one knee on the floor, smiled at his wife with blood in his teeth: the smile of a man whose alibi had just walked in wearing a mask.

Manav left the way he had come, past the dead intercom, out through the door that never latched.

---

By morning the society knew everything except what had happened.

A thief, masked. Tall as the door. Six feet, said the messages, and by noon six-two, and one man said knife and another said gun, and the error became history. The guard was doubled, the gate got a second chain, and it is remarkable what a second chain does for a building's opinion of itself. The committee expressed sympathy for its treasurer. A constable came, was given chai at the gate, took down six feet of description, and promised patrol; the patrolling began that night and ended that night. Somewhere a file was not born.

In the lift, Manav — five feet eight in shoes since 1999 — agreed gravely that the masked man sounded like somebody.

Days later, at the gate, the woman passed him with a bag of groceries, and for the length of one question mark she looked at him. The question got as far as the first word. Then the sentence closed over it, the way sentences do in that country, and she looked through him, and he let her. She had seen a body come through a door, that was all. Every body in that society was somebody's, and the wrongness of his walk could be filed under nothing, and was.

---

At home, at three, he cleaned the forearm with the water he had been saving for tea, because Farid had taught him nothing out loud and everything by example.

The mask's jaw had been opened in a clean seam. He sat in the kitchen with her needle and her grey thread and mended it, and the stitches went in straight. Every one. Nine nights of crooked and straight, a seismograph of the mind; and now, an hour after a man's mouth, straight as ruled work. He sat looking at that for a long time after, and the looking was its own finding.

By every number he owned, the night was a success. Crossed a room. Took a knife off a bad man with his body. Won. The numbers sat there being right. And underneath them, in the space where assessors write what they know and do not say, the finding that mattered: *the second minute had wanted its whole life.* Not the knife, not Deepak-ji — the taste. That was the true danger, and he put it where he put things now, knowing the filing solved nothing.

---

At the gym, one night at the wraps, he asked Ustad the question obliquely, the way one files a query.

"In your experience," he said. "A man who sees a wrong and says nothing. Fear?"

Ustad wrapped a boy's hands through the turnstile of his fingers, not looking up. The radio played at the volume of memory. Somewhere up the stairs a scooter went by with its whole life ahead of it.

"Fear is rare," Ustad said at last. "Loyalty is common. Men are not cowards, mostly. They are faithful — often to the wrong things. But you cannot fault the faith."

Whatever Ustad understood about the new stillness in his student, about ribs that healed in January and forearms that were cut in February, he left on the landing, as was the rule.

---

He came home to the television's lowest number and the boy asleep inside the noise of it, and stood in the doorway of the hall doing the new arithmetic.

In one building tonight, a boy had closed a door so softly only the hinge knew. In this one, a boy slept inside a sound, for reasons no file had ever held. *Witnesses become afraid* — the old sentence, worn smooth as river stone by two years of handling. He stood looking at his son and began, for the first time, to imagine the witnesses in his own file faithful instead — loyal to something, each of them, faithful to the wrong things — and the file grew heavier by a page.

He put the page where the mask lived, between two closed files, where nobody in the world opens anything.

### Chapter Five: The Strain


In insurance, March is the month the year confesses: every file must close, every story be audited at once. The branch drank chai like a hospital. And for the first time in two years, the season worked for him — the office was handing out alibis for free.

The night had made him a better assessor; that was the joke nobody at the branch was in on. A man who spends three hours at a dark paan counter learning what a walk says about a man cannot help reading the walk of a man across a desk. His files closed like taps turned off. In the year he had begun breaking into the night, the office gave him *exceeds expectations*, and he signed the form in the same hand that had learned, by lamplight, to hold a needle steady.

The shoulder had met a wall in the dark, on a night the office had no knowledge of and the wall has never reported. At breakfast the plum came up out of his collar like a second head.

"Rough day at the office," he said.

It was the new sentence, the small one, the working one. The stairs had been for the face. The office took the body's shifts.

"The office is dangerous this year," Aarav said, to his cereal, in the exact voice his mother had used for jokes that were not.

---

Rakesh Sethi came to the flat on a Sunday with a cricket kit the size of luggage, and stood in the door not coming in, not leaving — a man at the exact center of what he could carry. He talked cricket the way other men talk about the weather at funerals.

"Sood," he said once, and for a moment the tact opened half an inch, like a door on a chain — and shut. "The handle has anti-scuff tape on it. The boys all want it."

"Thank you, Sethi uncle," Aarav said, and carried the kit into his own room — the room that was for things now, not for sleeping — and shut the door on it.

Sethi watched the door shut and did something with his face that had no name in cricket. Then he talked eight more minutes of cricket, standing, and left with the emptiness of a man who had not said the thing he had come to say, and Manav closed the door and could not have said what the thing was, and stood a while with his instrument swinging slowly, pointing nowhere his files went.

---

On the ninth of March she would have been fifteen. They went after school to the sweet shop near the old market — the one that still made the thing she had always ordered, the sweet the whole family could name in one word and no longer said.

Aarav ordered it in a voice that had been rehearsed somewhere private, and they stood at the counter over it with two spoons and no appetite, and the shop's radio played film songs for somebody else's daughter.

"Didi would be taller than me now," Aarav said, to the counter.

He had passed her in the winter. Neither of them had mentioned it. The frozen thirteen, and the boy still growing — the arithmetic of the surviving is not in any actuarial table. Manav put his hand on the back of his son's neck, and the hand said everything it had, which was nothing, and the boy let it stand there, and that was the birthday.

---

The four answers came in the order the four questions came. They had begun coming too smoothly — the way claims do when a story has been through the machine twice. He heard it the way he heard everything now, at forty paces, and he pointed the hearing away from home, and the hearing would not stay pointed.

"You are spending sleep somewhere," Ustad said at the wraps, not a question, the landing rule holding.

Manav skipped rope like a man arguing with his own shadow, and the shadow was winning on points.

---

He went out at eleven with the office bag, as he had gone out at eleven all winter. The society had a fact about him now — *Sood saab walks at night* — and facts like that do a man's work for him; grief is the best ID there is. The thief of Block C had been six feet, and then six-two, and nobody looks up at a five-eight man when a six-two man is on file. He had built that shelter himself, one lie at a chain door, and it held him like a coat.

"Taking your walk," the boy said from the sofa, not a question either, to the ceiling, the television's colors going over his face.

"Ten minutes," Manav said, which was the lie's name for three hours.

He pulled the door shut and was two steps down the landing when the sounds came, inside: the sofa giving up a boy, the feet on the floor, and then the bolts. Not fumbled. Thrown. Chain, top, lower — three small sounds, each one landing like a signature — and then the feet going back, and the sofa taking the boy again.

He stood on the landing with his hand on the rail.

Fear fumbles a bolt. Practice doesn't.

He had explanations, all of them on file: the thief scare, the age, the dark, the counselor's phrase from the parent-teacher meeting, *a healthy vigilance*. He took them out one by one in the stairwell dark, and none of them explained the manner of the sounds. It was the same smoothness as the four answers. He set the two facts down side by side on the rail of his mind — and then, quickly, before they could meet, because facts that meet have children, he put them away in separate drawers, and went down the stairs, and the night took him in like family.

The bench kept him. Farid poured without being asked. The paan counter kept its two kinds of walk, and the runners ran, and he counted, because counting was the trade, and the count went into the book that lived where the mask lived, between two closed files.

---

He came home at ten to three and put his key in, and the key told him what the boy had left: everything on.

He knocked. Softly. A father's knock, two knuckles.

The television's light lay along the bottom of the door. A shadow moved in it. The chain came off first — the pause, the eye at the gap, the check, old habit now at three in the morning — and then all of it, and the door opened on a boy with sleep standing in his eyes like weather.

"I fell asleep on the sofa," the boy said, his cover story, and went back to the sofa, and the television said what it always said.

Manav stood in his own doorway like a stranger who had been let in out of pity.

It occurred to him only then, two years in, that the sofa was the room with the door in it. The thought stood there in the hall and did not go anywhere. He filed it with the click.

He pulled the blanket up over his son. In sleep the boy's fist was closed around the television remote, holding it the way his mother had held things in her fist when she was scolding somebody she loved.

He stood over the two of them, the boy and the noise, and did not do any arithmetic at all.

### Chapter Six: The World Breaks


By April he knew the drop-house the way he had once known his own claim ledger: by its habits. A double-storey behind the marble market, a scooter repair shop in front, men who came on Tuesdays and Saturdays with bundles that were never marble. Five months of Farid's bench and the paan counter and the two kinds of walk had taught him the room's grammar — which light meant counting, which silence meant collection, which scooter meant the week's settle. One of the runners moved like the underpass had moved, that night in January; he could not prove it; he kept the page.

Then, on a Thursday in April, at nine in the evening, two men came to the drop-house in a hurry, with loot still warm in its bags — jewellery in a pillow cover, a laptop, a tin that rang — and left again in four minutes, empty-handed and fast, going back the way they had come.

No one goes back for a second trip unless the first one is unfinished. No one leaves a house mid-job unless the house is holding something they intend to return to.

He followed them at the distance the winter had taught him, across two sectors, to a corner house in Sector 14 with a marigold wire on the gate and a child's bicycle lying where a child had left it, trusting.

The front door stood ajar. Second trip. The lights were on in the family room, and a television was playing.

He came to the window to count the men, and that was the end of counting.

---

An old man lay on the floor between the sofa and the show cabinet, breathing wrong, one wrist under him at the angle wrists do not choose. In the kitchen doorway a woman was sitting against the frame with her hands behind her, tied at the wrists with her own dupatta, awake, entirely awake, her eyes doing all the screaming a house like that allows. Somewhere deeper in the flat a baby was crying with the fury of the unanswered. A man he had not yet catalogued moved through the bedroom doorway with a folded bedsheet full of something, unhurried, itemized, patient — a system at work — and on the television a rerun played at low volume and the canned laughter rose at nothing, tinny, in a house with tied hands in it.

He had no memory of that night. He had been elsewhere; his absence had been the one perfect alibi of his life. The freeze gave him the attendance he had missed. Standing at a stranger's window with his hand on the sill, his mind — patient as a clerk, three years patient — finally produced his own night in full: the hallway, the geyser he had never fixed, the almirah the file said, that he had never once allowed himself to see, the sounds given to a boy's imagination as television. He stood at the glass and attended, three years late, his own house, and his body did what bodies do at the scene of their worst file.

It stopped.

The watch on his wrist said ninety seconds. The watch was wrong; ninety seconds is a filing; this was not. What it was, was the laugh track rising and the baby unanswered and the old man's breathing going wronger, and the men moving, unhurried, itemized, through a family's life — and then, because a body can only die once and he had not died at the window, his legs came back to him all pins and needles, like a loan recalled at the worst hour, and he went through the ajar door.

The first minute worked. It was all he had, and it was enough for one man: the bundle-carrier came out of the bedroom doorway into the hallway and Manav took him there, weight forward, inside the arms, the shoulders doing their arithmetic, and the man went down into the wall and the wall participated. The second came with the iron rod from behind the door, and the rod missed and the man did not, and there was a bad half-minute in the family room with the television laughing and the old man on the floor between them like a dropped parcel, and then the rod man saw what he was fighting — a mask, in a house he was robbing, at the hour when masks do not come — and did the street's arithmetic and went out through the door into his own night.

The first man was under Manav, halfway folded, breathing in pieces. He was the catch. He was five months of bench and three years of night, whole, catchable, the first thread of everything, in the hand at last.

In the kitchen doorway the woman's eyes had found her father on the floor, and the old man had found her, and said, to the ceiling, to the mask, to whoever had come — "Bitiya. Bitiya."

Manav opened the hand.

The man under it went out on all fours and then not on all fours, and was gone, and Manav knelt by the old man with his sleeve already taking the blood, and did what hands do. The wrist was wrong. The head had a cut with plenty behind it. The woman got her hands free by herself, pulling at the knot with her teeth, the way people do when the help has gone out of the house, and she reached her father and put her own hands on his face, and the baby cried on, and the television laughed on, and the rod was on the floor, and nobody in that room looked at the mask again, because the hurt had made the mask furniture.

He rang three bells before one opened. The city comes. It is never early. A man in a ganji stood in the first light of his door and saw across the lane a masked figure in a lit doorway with an old man in it, and did not scream, because the old man mattered more, and Manav gave him the number of the house and the two words — *ambulance, police* — and was down the lane before the word police had finished its sentence.

He walked home through a city that did not yet know anything, with another man's blood drying at his wrist, and his hands, when he looked at them under a streetlight, were conducting their own private weather.

---

At four in the morning he sat on the bathroom floor with the mask in both hands, and the sentence went around in him like water in a drained pipe: *enough.* Total loss. No salvage. A man who freezes at a window is not a weapon; a man who opens the hand is not a hunter; and the night had shown him, in the end, the only office he was fit for — arriving after, holding a stranger's head while the city took its time.

It was the sanest sentence he had thought in three years, and that was what frightened him of it.

He was still holding the mask, composing the resignation, when the sky went grey.

---

The boy turned the volume up himself.

That was the first fact of the morning: Manav in the kitchen doorway with the resignation still in his chest, and Aarav on the sofa turning the volume up with the remote in his fist, and on the television, on every channel, a wedding.

The Grand Utsav banquet hall, Vinayak Vihar. Garlands, a mandap, four hundred guests, the official videographer, and a hundred phones held up the way phones are held up now — as witnesses in advance. The footage, which everyone in the country would own by evening, showed the pheras at 11:52 the previous night: the couple circling the fire, the pundit's hand rising, and then — between one frame and the next — the groom is, and is not. No smoke. No light. No sound, except every flame in the hall leaning one way for a moment, like air being used. The garlands fall into the clothes. The clothes fall like a coat off a hook whose hook has been removed.

The bride, twenty-six, a chartered accountant, was holding his hand at the time, and for a week afterward, until the interviews stopped letting her, she would say it was like a word on the tip of the tongue.

The anchors had no word yet. A police spokesman, at nine, used the phrase *unexplained removal of a person*, badly, twice, trying to keep his voice level, and the city — which needed the phrase more than the police did — kept it: Removals. By evening someone had captioned the loop with a joke about the wedding's return policy — *receipt attached* — and the city, which needed the word more than the joke, kept that too.

On the sofa, in the morning light, the boy watched the loop the way Manav watched claims. Entirely.

"Did he do something?" Aarav said.

"Nobody knows yet," Manav said, the father's answer, the only true one.

"That's not the same as no," the boy said, to the screen, and turned the volume up one more number.

The resignation in Manav's chest did not survive the morning. Not because the world breaking was a reason. Because of what came four days later, on the Thursday, which changed the terms of everything, including his.

---

The settlement had been signed in four copies, the way settlements are, nine years ago: a young man, a road, a cyclist who had been somebody's whole income. Three of the copies were accounted for — a lawyer's safe, a cupboard, a court's dead file. The fourth arrived by courier at the gate of a news channel, with an affidavit from a witness who had been paid, and had spent the money, and had kept his copy, and now, in the new weather, could not sleep near it.

The city understood the terms that day, all at once, the way herds understand. It was not the vanishing that was new. It was the accounting. Whatever kept the list had *access* — to settlements, to buried files, to the things that had been paid, patiently, over years, to become un-true. The law had been paid. The list had not.

Four more by the weekend: a ration officer, two moneylenders, a school principal. The early receipts were all men the city had quietly hated for years, and the city fell for the list the way crowds fall for anyone who hates what they hate. By Friday the panels had a theory with a face in it: the masked man. Sightings since winter; Removals since spring. *A coincidence is a rumor that can walk,* someone said on a panel, *and this one is running.* The forwards had found their scripture and were typing it in capital letters: HE IS OUT THERE. HE SEES. HE IS TAKING THEM HOME.

Manav read the scripture at one in the morning at the kitchen table and could not correct a word of it. He alone in the city held the ledger that said otherwise — the Kishanganj financier had gone in January, a full month before any mask walked into Block C — and he could never sign the correction, because the claim could only be filed by a man, and he was a mask. The night had given him somewhere to hide, and somewhere to hide, it turned out, was also somewhere to be silenced.

But it was the other clause of the new terms that unresigned him.

The takers did not explain. The receipt said *what*, and it said it in other people's documents, and it never once said *why* — never which door, never which night, never the shape of the family behind the settlement. If the list took his four men, the why of his own night would go with them, unexplained forever, on both ends.

He had spent three years hunting four men. He was now hunting them against a clock whose face he could not read, run by something that did not file.

---

That morning, at breakfast, the liturgy ran backward. It was the boy who asked first.

"Did you sleep?"

"Yes," Manav said — one lie of four, delivered smoothly — and the boy nodded, the way one nods when a claim has been assessed and passed, and asked the other three questions in their order, and got their answers, and went to the van with his bag, and did not look at the marigold wire on the gate, where the bicycle of Sector 14 still lay in his mind and would not lie down.

After the van had gone, Manav took the mask out of the office bag, out from between the two closed files where it had lived since winter, and put it in his jacket pocket. Carried now, not filed. The work had outgrown one man's eyes, and the night had more in it than men, and the world was going to need, badly, a man with a file.

At night he stood in the doorway of the hall, where his son slept inside the noise, and the noise had a new religion in it now, playing at the volume of memory.

He stood there the way fathers stand — on duty.

## PART TWO — THE SYSTEM

*the Editor, the case he thought he'd closed*

### Chapter Seven: CORRECTIONS


By May the whole city had started wearing his mask.

Not cloth — nobody else had cloth. Manners. The sudden, loud, public goodness of a town that had learned it was being graded: prayers said where prayers could be seen, tithes announced at volume, old debts settled by men who wanted the settling witnessed. The rickshaw pullers had a joke with no laugh in it — *bhagwan bhi line mein hai*, God's also in the queue — and the forwards had found a word for what the mobs did: they called it a correction.

Meanwhile, half the old night trade had gone quiet. Men who worked in the dark had discovered the dark had management now. The betting room's Tuesdays had thinned; the runners ran less; the drop-house behind the marble market kept its lights longer, counting nothing. The world had not gotten kinder. Violence had simply changed costume — from balaclavas to processions — and the money was learning to follow.

Manav's nights had split in two. The old hunt — the network, the fence, the four — ran on its rails, slower now, racing a clock whose face he could not read. And the new weather ran beside it: he had begun, without deciding to, a second ledger. The mobs were not spontaneous. His count said so. Three corrections in three weeks, and the same grey shirt in the crowd each time, on the same scooter, arriving early and leaving paid — mobs were becoming a service someone purchased, and he followed the service provider the way he had once followed a blue scooter with a taped mirror, because a pattern is a door even when you do not yet know the room.

The fourth mob was at a school.

---

Government senior secondary, Sector 19, 7:40 of a May evening, result season. Two hundred men by his count, growing at the rate rumours grow, and at the gate, sitting on the ground with his back against the bars and chalk still on his fingers, a chemistry teacher called Upadhyay-ji, who had taught three generations of the district their periodic tables and was now, by the light of a hundred phone torches, guilty.

The receipt was a photograph, forwarded and forwarded: a handwritten page, purportedly in his hand, pricing the chemistry board paper at eight hundred rupees a student. It had arrived from nowhere in particular, which is where the era's most trusted documents came from. Nobody had seen an original. Nobody needed one. The list had taught the city that the paper always comes *after* — and the city had improved on the lesson: why wait for heaven's audit when a correction could be held tonight, by hand, at the gate of the guilty?

Upadhyay-ji was saying his own name to whoever would hold it. The mob's front rank — young men with lathis and puja marks, a cohort of the publicly good — had begun the small tidal movements crowds make before they close.

Manav stood in the paan-stall dark across the road, unmasked, and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was all wrong.

Go in as a man, and he was one body against two hundred, and a man with a face besides. Go in as the mask, and — and this was the trap his own winter had built, one lie at a chain door at a time — the mask would arrive like scripture. *HE IS OUT THERE. HE SEES.* The mob would freeze; the mob would part; the mob would look from the mask to the teacher and draw the only conclusion the era allowed: the mask had come for Upadhyay-ji. They would not fight him. They would *help*. They would push the teacher forward, offer him up, stand back to witness the judgment — and if the mask refused to judge, refused aloud, said *this man is innocent* in a stranger's cloth —

He could see it. The whole machine of it, clear as a claim diagram. His myth had no vocabulary for *not guilty*. The mask could not save the man. The mask could only convict him.

His hand was already going to his pocket — the mask was carried now, not filed; carried is closer to worn — when the phone lit up in the same hand.

An app he had never installed.

White screen. Small grey type. No logo, no greeting, no name but the one under the icon, which was the era's own word for a beating:

**CORRECTIONS**

*C-1. The mask will convict him. They will offer him to you. Grey shirt, scooter, by the paan counter — paid to lead this. Ask him, in front of them, what he was paid. He runs. Ninety seconds.*

He read it twice. It took three seconds. Across the road the crowd's tide had started its closing run, and he had eighty-seven seconds and no theory of where the note had come from, and twenty-one years of counters had taught him the two kinds of angry man: the one who wants the thing, and the one who wants to be watched. The second kind leads mobs. A mob is a sentence looking for its verb.

He crossed the road as a man — office walk, office shirt, a face like a Wednesday — and walked into the crowd's middle as though called to the counter, and a crowd will always make room for a man who behaves as if he has business, because no one wants to discover the business.

"Bhai!" One voice, an office voice, a *next please* voice. "The grey shirt! On the scooter!" — and every head turned, because the sentence had found its verb — "Ask him what he was paid! He's been paid to be here! Ask him! Ask him in front of everyone!"

A mob is a sentence looking for its verb, and this one had just been handed a better one than *beat the teacher*. Two hundred men turned as one organism toward the paan counter, where a man in a grey shirt was already, before the sentence finished, reaching for his scooter's handle.

He ran. That was the proof. No innocent man on a scooter leaves a riot he has organized, at a jog, sideways, before the question is even finished being asked — but a paid man does, because a paid man's job has a clause in it about exactly this. The mob watched him go, and the mob understood what it had just been told about itself, and mobs cannot bear to know what they cost.

The circle broke into arithmetic. The producer was gone; the theater found the exits. Two colleagues of Upadhyay-ji's came over the gate like men remembering their profession and took the teacher under the arms, chalk and all, back into the school's dark, where a headmaster was already on the phone to somebody's uncle, who was a magistrate.

The watch on Manav's wrist said ninety seconds. This time the watch was right.

---

The phone lit again in his pocket, on the walk home, at the corner where the streetlights gave up.

*C-2. The receipt was drawn by hand, cut from a register. The register is under the quilts in the boot of a white Santro, TG-3-, parked behind the school. It belongs to a parent whose son has failed twice. The police will find it at 6:40 tomorrow if someone tells them where. Anyone can call.*

Anyone can call. He stood under the dead streetlight and understood that the sentence was the sender's whole character in three words: it did not do things. It corrected, and it left the verb to him.

He found a PCO in the old quarter — one of the last, a cabinet of black phones run by a man too stubborn to die politely — and called the police control room from a public phone, on a public street, a citizen's voice with no name it needed, and gave the Santro, the quilts, the register, the school. Then he went home and did not sleep, and at 6:40 in the morning his phone lit with the news, as clocks do.

The register had been found. The forger had confessed by 9. Upadhyay-ji was cleared. A small item, page five, no photograph. The teacher, the papers said, would return to his classroom on Monday, and had asked reporters, politely, to let his students be. He had begun, someone wrote, marking in pencil.

---

At the kitchen table, that night, Manav audited the app the way he had audited everything since October.

It had no install date. It held no permissions — none for mic, none for camera, none for data. It used no data. It had no size. In the ledger of the phone it weighed nothing and arrived never; it was the first thing he had ever held that declined, entirely, to exist on paper. The era's mobs called beatings corrections, and here was a stranger in the era's own language, offering corrections for free, timed to the second, right about the minute, right about the register, right about the grey shirt — and asking for nothing, which was the most expensive thing anything had ever asked him for.

He had spent his life verifying claimants. There is no such thing as a free surveyor; every man at every counter is paid by somebody to be there. The finding was obvious, and he wrote it on a fresh page of the ledger that lived where the mask lived:

*SOURCES —*
*1. CORRECTIONS. Unverified claimant. No face, no office, no address. Two corrections, both paid out to the minute. Motive unstated. Treat as adverse party.*

He underlined *adverse party* twice, and felt the night tilt a little, the way a file tilts when a party with no name enters the case.

The phone lit, once more, on the table, face-up between him and the ledger.

*C-3. Your wrist. Ice it.*

The wrist — the left one, the one that had caught a man in Sector 14 and held an old man and had been quietly swelling since Thursday, all of it unmentioned, all of it unwitnessed, known to no one in the world, including, until that moment, himself.

He sat for a long time with the phone face-up, being cared for by something with no face.

He did not fetch the ice. Obeying, he understood, would be the first payment — and he put the phone face-down beside the ledger, and sat in the kitchen with his bad wrist and his two ledgers and the beginning of a very long case, and did not do any arithmetic at all, because the arithmetic, for the first time in three years, was being done for him.

### Chapter Eight: The Courtship


The courtship, if it could be called that, was conducted between eleven and three, at a kitchen table, between a man with a ledger and a phone that weighed nothing.

June had normalized the era the way June normalizes heat. The list had taken two men that month; the papers printed them below the commodity prices, and the city read both over tea and wondered about neither. The network behind the marble market had gone cautious — lights later, runs fewer, the drop-house counting its own money twice — the dark had management now, and the management was bad for everyone's business, including his. The hunt that had run on five months of bench-work was slowing to nothing, and both parties at the kitchen table knew it.

He had decided, early, on a protocol: numbered messages were its own openings; the answers it gave his questions carried no numbers, as if his questions were not events, only weather. It corrected. It did not converse.

First, the audit. He had spent his life verifying claimants, and he verified this one the only way he knew.

He asked it about a fire in a basement in 2011. It answered with the meter's serial number. He had stood in that meter room himself, in the smoke, twenty-one years of surveys ago, and had never known the meter had a name.

Then he did what he had wanted for years to do to certain claimants: he salted the file. A true story — the jeweller, the November confession — with one false fact set into it like a stitch. *The jeweller broke in the third interview,* he typed.

The answer came back in one word.

*Second.*

It had not been offended. It had not been fooled. It had not let it stand.

Three rules, then, learned the way he had learned everything, at small cost, on purpose. One: it did not lie. Two: it did not let him lie. Three: everything else was a choice, and the choices were its own.

---

He asked it the questions he shouldn't.

*Who are you.* Silence — with a texture he knew from counters: the silence of a party that has weighed the cost of answering and found it above the premium.

*Who pays you.* The same silence, exactly the same, which was itself a datum: the two silences had the same author.

*Why me.*

Nothing. And then, at four in the morning, when he had put the phone down and picked it up once more the way a tongue returns to a tooth:

*Why should I trust you?*

*You shouldn't. You have verified every correction and acted on every one. Trust is not what this is.*

He sat with that. It was the first answer that had given him nothing to check and everything to look at. It answered the way a mirror answers — with himself, arranged slightly truer.

*Does the mask have anything to do with the takings?* — the city's own scripture, put to the only oracle he had.

*That is not a claim. It cannot be audited.*

Not *I don't know.* Not *no.* A refusal of category — the first question it had declined as a thing that could exist. He noted it in the ledger, in the small hand he used for findings that did not fit: *the source's system ends where the sky begins. Or the source is careful. Both filings live.*

One more, that week, at two in the morning, the question underneath all his questions:

*Is anything watching my flat?*

*No. Not even me. Houses are the one thing I cannot enter.*

The relief arrived before the thought did, and the thought arrived anyway: it was the first time in three years anything had told him the flat was his. And underneath that, quiet as a page turning, the other thought, the one he did not file: *it has never once said the boy's name.* Not in correction, not in care, not in the wrist, not in the sleep. He noticed, the way he noticed everything now. He did not ask why. Some not-askings are load-bearing.

The wrist had got its ice in the end — pride lasts until the second swollen morning. The care continued on a schedule of its own choosing:

*C-4. Sleep. You have slept nine hours this week that your body agrees to count.*

He obeyed it the way he obeyed Ustad, the way he sat where Farid could see him, and hated the mechanism by which he obeyed, which he could not find anywhere in the phone.

It occurred to him one Thursday, filing the night away, that the app had now heard more of his voice than any human being had since the hospital. He put that under findings, where it did the most damage, and left it there, because findings, unlike people, stay where you put them.

---

Summer had emptied the mornings of the school van. Aarav was home all day now, and had begun, without announcement, making the evening tea — carefully, measuring the leaf, watching the colour come, the way he did everything, entirely — and at dinner, one night, out of nowhere, over the one television in the city that did not carry the news:

"Papa. Can it see inside houses?"

The *it* needed no antecedent. The era lived in children now, like weather.

"Nobody knows that," Manav said.

"That's still not the same as no," the boy said, and poured his father's tea, and the era and the epistemology and the tea all arrived together, and Manav drank what he was given.

---

And then, one night in late June, at one in the morning, he asked the question underneath all the others, the one he had been not-asking since the wrist:

*The crew. Four men. How many are alive?*

The answer took four seconds, which was long, for it.

*Three.*

Nothing followed. The number stood in the kitchen the way a man stands in a doorway. He typed: *Which one.*

*The one they called Chotu. He died outside Panipat in a card dispute fourteen months ago. The file on him is closed.*

Chotu. He held the phone with both hands. Chotu was a name out of a dark place — out of the underpass, the January of the ribs, a man's voice calling a name into the dark, *Chotu aa gaya*, and the dark producing company. One of the four. One of the four had helped beat him in an underpass, three feet away, over seventy rupees of chai and the price of being seen — and had then lived fourteen more months, and died over cards, and the quarter of the why that lived in his mouth had died with him, unmourned, unaudited, below the commodity prices.

He typed the only thing left:

*You knew.*

*You did not ask.*

He sat back from the table. It was the truest sentence the app had ever sent him, and the first one he could not file without bleeding. Formally correct — he had not asked; it had not volunteered; both parties had acted within the rules of a contract nobody had shown him. And the whole of the wrongness in the world sitting inside the formal correctness, like a settlement that pays out and pays out and changes nothing.

He had known counterparties like this.

He had been, on certain days, a counterparty like this.

He put the phone face-down and did not type again for two days, and the phone, with a patience that felt like the whole of its character, sent nothing at all, and the silence had the texture of a bench: of Farid's bench, of a landing with phones on it — of things that wait without watching.

On the third night, at 1:40, unprompted, numbered:

*C-5. The one they called Lakha. Milk-route tempo, Bhagwanpura side. He sleeps at the wheel between runs. He has not been home since that winter. He was never difficult to find. He was only difficult to name.*

He read it until the words stopped being words, and then he read them again, and the name stood in the kitchen the way a man stands in a doorway.

Lakha.

Two years of nothing, and then three years of nothing, and now a name — after the anger, not before it; after the withholding, not before it; exactly when a gift would bind a claimant most. He noted the timing, the way he noted everything, and noted also that the noting had not made him put the name down.

He did not write it in the ledger.

Paper forgets. This one he would keep in the meat.

### Chapter Nine: Lakha


The mandi called him by the name the phone had given, and the name checked out, and that was the survey.

It had taken two nights to find the collection yard — the milk came into the old quarter at 2:40 in tankers and left in tempos by 4, and the tempo on the Bhagwanpura routes was driven by a man the loaders called Lakha, and no one called him anything else. His mother's house, wherever it was, would have called him something different. The mandi never learned it. He never offered.

Manav bought a bicycle. A Hero, second-hand, the era's cheapest invisibility — no 44-year-old clerk on a cycle at 3 a.m. has ever been seen by anyone — and for eleven nights he learned the route the way he had once learned claims: by its habits, its pauses, its lies.

The route had a liturgy. Collection at 2:40. First run at 4. Between them, forty minutes at the wheel, and the man did not sleep in that forty minutes — he sat in the tempo's cab with his eyes open and watched the mandi wake, and the mandi dogs came to the window one at a time, and he fed them from a bag he bought with his own money before he ate anything himself. Tuesdays he drove the far side of the ring, and slowed past a lane where bougainvillea grew over a green gate, and did not stop, and had not stopped in three years. Tuesdays he also went to the temple near the timber market and prayed, if that was the word, with his eyes open the whole time — watching the door, watching the sky through the door, watching.

The man did not sleep inside. He slept at the wheel, in the open, under whatever the night was doing. Once, near dawn, he slept for twenty minutes on the tempo's hood, flat on his back in the yard with the dogs around him like unpaid staff, and Manav, thirty meters off in the shadow of the weighbridge, wrote it in the ledger of his head: *the guilty sleep outside. They think walls are where it happens.*

He had expected, if he was honest — and the file under the file was where he kept his honesty — to find a monster or a nothing. He found a man doing the things he himself did. Feeding something that could not testify. Keeping hours no one had asked him to keep. Watching a home he could not enter, on a schedule, on Tuesdays.

*Findings: the subject is not fleeing justice. He is serving a sentence already. Custody would be a transfer.*

He wrote that in the ledger, and then, because findings have consequences, he took out the police file of his own house and read the inventory until the anger came back. It came slower every year now. He had to prime it, the way you prime a dry pump.

On the fifth night the phone lit once, numbered:

*C-6. Two lengths further. He counts faces.*

He counts faces. Of course he counted faces. Three years of counting them. Manav dropped back two lengths, and the gift, as always, had been double-edged: protection for the instrument, and a small cold seminar on how far away the instrument's keeper could see.

The app sent nothing else, all eleven nights. He noted that too. It had given him the name and then stood aside, as if the gift were the whole of its interest and the using was the point.

---

The plan, when it was ready, was a counter.

It was, he was fairly sure, the first plan of its kind in the history of the city's night: three weeks of being a face. The tea stall at the mandi gate, 3 a.m., the same man, the same kurta, until the loaders stopped noticing and Lakha's count of faces absorbed him as furniture — and then, one night, the slide onto the next stool, and *these runs keep you thin*, and the slow bureaucratic miracle of a stranger becoming a person, and a person becoming someone you tell things. He had done it twenty-one years across desks. The insured tell the assessor everything, eventually, because the assessor is the only one who ever asks in a voice with no weather in it.

That was the plan. The claims assessor's patience, not the mask's.

In July the boy turned ten. One breath for the candles, the wish unsaid the way wishes are. Sethi's envelope arrived on the birthday itself, punctual, too much, *from the floor, ha ha* — the floor had now done this three years running — and the cricket kit stood in the room where things lived, used twice. Manav watched his son arrive at double digits over a small cake in a flat where the television kept its vigil, and across the city a man was sleeping at a wheel with his eyes open, and both facts lived in him now, side by side, in separate drawers, not meeting.

The office got his mornings that summer the way it had always had them, which was the whole trick. A man cannot be suspected of a night if his mornings are immaculate.

And Farid — Farid, whose stall sat at the mandi's edge like a fixed star — understood without being told. He had watched the man on the cycle for four nights before the kettle said anything about it, and what the kettle said was two cups. One at the bench. One at the far end, at watching distance. Nobody discussed it. The night city had taken a side, or declined to, which in that city amounted to the same side.

---

On the ninth night, the list came to the mandi.

Manav arrived at 3:10 to a silence he had never heard in that hundred-year gossip of a place — the loaders standing in a ring around a patch of ground near the weighbridge where nothing was. No smoke, no light, no sound, the men said afterward, asked by nobody, telling nobody. Between one load and the next. A weighbridge clerk who had doctored his weights for years, the mandi knew, the whole mandi knew, and the mandi had gossiped about everything under its awnings for a century, and it did not gossip about this. A shoe stood on the empty patch, rightside up, precise. The dogs would not go near it.

Lakha had been parked closest. Lakha had seen.

The tempo sat where it sat, and in its window the man's face was the face of a dropped claim. He did not do the 4 o'clock run. He did the Tuesday drive-past on a Thursday, twice, slow, the bougainvillea going by, and then he fed the dogs the bread he had not touched and sat on the hood and looked at the sky over the old quarter for a long time, with his eyes open, the way he prayed.

A week later the receipt came for the clerk, in the manner of receipts — a weighbridge book, three years of false grams, delivered to a newspaper by nobody — and the mandi read about its own silence in the papers, and said nothing about that either.

That night the app sent nothing, and Manav understood something small and very large, standing in the yard at watching distance with the two-dog night going on around him: it did not correct the list. Nobody corrected the list. Whatever its ledgers held, whatever its minutes were worth, the thing at the mandi had happened in front of its instruments like weather.

The eleventh night, Lakha's route ran ragged and turned, at 3:40, away from the mandi — out past the last of the numbered sectors, to a tower the era had stopped mid-build. Sixteen floors of intention, standing in its scaffolding at the ring's edge, where the money had gotten superstitious. A place a man goes up when the decision is already made and the legs are only informing the body.

The tempo parked at the site gate. The man went in through the shutter with a bottle, on foot, unhurried, at the end of everything.

Manav stood at the bottom of the stairwell with the mask in his fist and did the last audit.

The plan — the stall, the tea, three weeks of being a face — was a plan for a man with time. The man on the stairs had a morning in him somewhere, a bus, a border, and eleven nights of watching said *now or never*, and the mask was the only language that stops a running man in the dark.

A confession to a mask would stand up in no court in the world.

He went up anyway. The court had never been the point; the why was; and there was one more filing, made honestly, in the file under the file, where he kept himself: that a part of him had been waiting since the underpass — since the ribs, since the seventy rupees of chai, since the night the dark produced company — to be the thing the night saw, instead of the thing it happened to.

The stairwell was pitch, then grey, then grey with wind in it. He came up the last flight at ten past four and stepped out onto the gravel, and the city lay away to the dark edge of itself under the sodium sky, and the man was standing at the parapet with his hands open, a bottle set down carefully at his feet, straight-backed, the way men stand for inspections.

He turned. He did not run.

He had been waiting three years.

### Chapter Ten: The Rooftop


Lakha straightened when the mask cleared the stairwell door, the way a man straightens for an inspection he has rehearsed.

"You came," he said. "Three years I thought it would be bigger. It is the size of a man."

He did not come at the mask, and he did not run. He stood at the parapet with his hands open, the bottle set down at his feet with the care of a man who has decided to stop needing it, and he asked the era's own question, first, before anything else, the way a man asks the doctor the real question before the small ones:

"Are you the one who takes, or the one who watches?"

"I'm the one who asks," the mask said.

Lakha considered that. Something in his shoulders let go, the way it goes in men at counters when the clerk, at last, asks.

---

"The job was papered," he said. "You pay extra for papered. Confirmed empty, three days, the whole block gone for the festival. The cousin brought it — Zorawar, timber market. He deals in confirmed-empty the way other men deal in timber. I never met the man above him. Zorawar said he had a man whose cousin sits in an office, and the office man knew the block. That is all I ever knew, and I swear it on the dogs: all."

He looked at his hands.

"I held the door. I am not telling you that as a defense. Nothing comes to a house that does not come through a door first, and the door was mine. The others went in. What was in the rooms, I was not in the rooms. I was in the hall, and the hall is what I will tell you, because the hall is mine to tell.

"We stood at the door first and listened. That is how it is done — you listen for the house. A television was playing inside. A television does not sound like an empty house." He said it slowly, a man laying out the one exhibit. "But the paper said empty till Monday, and the paper had been right before, and people leave televisions on when they go away, to make the house sound like itself. So we knocked. For the sound to stop. It did not stop."

He waited. The wind moved the scaffold cloth somewhere below.

"An empty house pauses nothing. A sleeping child also pauses nothing. We could not tell the difference between an empty house and a sleeping boy." He said it again, quieter, as though the first saying had been for the mask and this one was for the roof. "That was the whole night, bhai. We could not tell the difference.

"And then a cupboard clicked, somewhere inside. Small. Like a lock." His hands closed. "I hear that click every night since. In the yard, at the wheel. You cannot know what a small sound costs.

"The girl said something to the cupboard. I did not hear the words. I heard the tone. The tone was a mother's tone. From a girl."

He looked at the mask then, and Manav asked, in the voice with no weather in it, the only question he had come for:

"Who gave Zorawar the paper?"

"Zorawar said the office-wala knew the family. Knew the house. He had done the timings himself — not a guess, timings, puja to Monday. Zorawar said the office-wala knew the man of the house personally, he knew his —"

At 4:31, between one syllable and the next, the ears went first.

---

A pressure, like a train arriving somewhere behind the sky.

The gravel moved. Not scattered — leaned, all of it, one grain deep, toward the stairwell door, the way iron files toward a stone it has already agreed to. The bottle's plastic bag stood up off the gravel, empty, at attention. The sodium light on the crane did not flicker; it hummed a different note, a note with work in it.

Between that hum and the next, Lakha stopped.

Not fell. Stopped — the way a sentence stops when the page is lifted out of the typewriter. His slippers stayed where his feet had been, side by side, squared to the parapet. His clothes let go of him in the order clothes go, collar last, like a coat off a hook whose hook has been removed, and the wind that was not wind — the using wind — folded the kurta once, tidily, and was done.

The roof held: gravel. Slippers. A bottle. A folded kurta. Trousers arranged the way a man arranges trousers on a chair before sleeping. Nothing else. Nothing of him else.

The order of it was the worst part, and Manav understood the era's whole aesthetic at 4:31 on a gravel roof at the edge of the city: it did not destroy.

It edited.

---

He stood a long time with three years of violence in him and nowhere to put it.

The sentence hung in the air where the man had been. *He knew the man of the house personally, he knew his —* Eight words of a name that would have explained the paper, the timings, the empty-until-Monday — and heaven had taken the rest of the sentence with the rest of the man.

He had known, since the mandi, since the weighbridge clerk and the shoe, that the era would come for the four. He had been racing it. He had simply believed the race was long.

He threw the bottle off the roof, as far as the arm could ask. It was the only violence the night had left him, and it was nothing, and it did not even sound.

---

He rode the cycle to the yard before the loaders came and fed the dogs from the bag, at watching distance from the shutter, and left the tempo where the tempo was.

It was not his mercy to give. It was a debt transfer. The night city runs on them.

---

The city woke to three.

One in the godman's compound, mid-aarti, the diya still burning in the space where a hand had held it — the image of the summer, the decade maybe. One in a card room outside Panipat. One, unpublic, on a roof, known only to a mask and a wind. One minute, the anchors kept saying, checking and rechecking the timestamps. One minute, three men.

The receipts came by Thursday. For the card room: a tally book, twelve years of it. For the temple: the compound's collection ledger, second book, the one the godman's accountants did not know the godman kept. And for the man on the roof: pawn slips. Gold, hallmarked, the items listed with dates — the branch, the weights, the winter of that year.

He did not read past the third item.

By Friday the city knew whose night the receipts had come from. VISHWAS COLONY CASE CLOSED BY HEAVEN, one channel put it, and was prouder of the phrasing than of anything else that summer. The society sent a card to the flat. It said the matter had been resolved, and people stood extra seconds at the gate when he passed, and the tea superstition of the office returned in a warmer form, and none of it, not one line of it, contained a word of why.

The list had done in one minute what three years of the law had not. And it had done it the way he had never once wanted it done: as a verdict with no reasons attached. The receipts said *what*. Heaven, it turned out, also repudiated the claim of explanation.

The scripture got its new verse. The mask had found them, the forwards said, and the list had followed. HE FINDS THEM. HE TAKES THEM HOME. He read it at the kitchen table at one in the morning and could not sign the correction, and the correction sat in him all night like a sentence with no page.

At dinner, the boy put his spoon down.

"Papa. They took the men who did that night."

"Yes."

"Is it over now?"

"Almost, beta."

The boy nodded, the way one nods when a claim has been assessed and passed, and Manav could not see the file behind the passing, and did not try. Some drawers are stronger than the man who built them.

---

For six nights the phone said nothing. The silence had a new taste — not the bench-taste of waiting, but the taste of a thing that had watched its instrument survive an operation and was letting the meat remember.

Then, at two in the morning, numbered:

*C-8. The thread did not break. Zorawar, timber market. When you are ready.*

For the first time, Manav thought about the canal. About the phone's small weight, the black water, the whole relationship finished in one arc of the arm. He walked to the footbridge and stood at the rail a long time with the phone warm in his fist.

He did not throw it.

And standing there, not throwing it, he understood that the not-throwing was information too — and that the thing at the other end of it, whatever it was, had modeled the not-throwing before he had reached the bridge.

---

The old arithmetic was over. Four men had organized his life, and one minute had organized the four, and what was left was not a case, and had never been a case, and he had known it, in the file under the file, since a night at a chain door in Block C.

He stood at the kitchen window with the city's doors stacked away into the dark. Thousands. Each with somebody's Tuesday behind it. The list working through them at its own pace, tidily. The law slower than the list. The list answerable to no one. And no one — he understood this now with the whole of his body, which was the only organ that had ever told him the truth — no one was coming to any of them in time.

He had learned the night to find four men.

The night was full of doors.

### Chapter Eleven: The Mask Multiplies


The first copycats were honest about it, at least: three boys in festival masks at a bus stop, stopping scooters, demanding to see receipts, collecting fifty rupees a virtue. It was a shakedown. It was also, in its way, a religion — and by August the city had both kinds.

There were the freelance masks, kids mostly, who had understood the scripture as a business licence. There were the printed vests — the first gang with a uniform, the *Nagrik Rakshak Dal*, screen-printed and dues-paying, that patrolled two markets "correcting" them — evil had discovered screen-printing, and the era never got it back. And there was what he had actually started, though nobody knew it started anywhere, least of all at a bedtime.

He met that one on a Tuesday, in the lane behind the timber market's smoke.

A woman on a roof. A woman on the corner at eleven, and at one, and at three — walking her street the way he had once learned to walk the night, in loops, in stillness, at ninety seconds a pause. Her mask was a dupatta, wound once, the way women wind them when the wind is up. The cloth was cotton, and old, and he recognized the grammar of it the way you recognize your own handwriting in someone else's letter. He never asked. Some not-askings are load-bearing.

The lane knew her story the way lanes know things: in full, aloud, and never to her face. Her sister had been a nurse, taken in June. The receipt had come like receipts come — a ward ledger, pages of it, medicines moving at prices, signatures. The family had read it and gone quiet, because the ledger was true. And then her sister's colleague had stood up at a prayer meeting and said the prices were the insurance queue's prices, that the signatures were the matron's signature, that the papers said what they said because of what the papers were for — and the family had understood that the ledger was true and the reading was wrong, and that no channel and no court and no hotline in the world was interested in the difference.

She had said one thing, once, to the paanwala, and the lane had kept it like scripture keeps a verse: *I am not disputing the paper. I am disputing the reading.*

Nothing had been corrected on her street since June. The lane could not say whether it was her. The lane said nothing, aloud, about her, ever. He passed through twice that summer, mask to mask across the sodium light, and they did not speak. There is nothing sentries need to say.

---

The industries came in the order industries come: religion first, then compliance, then paper.

The godman's compound — one diya short since the aarti — had opened an Assurance Line. Assurance was the word. The flyers said *peace*, the small print said *consultation*, and the queue outside the gate was the longest in the city, because fear will pay anything that stands still long enough. A firm of ex-chartered accountants in Sector 8 sold Sin Audits — a full examination of your business for list-exposure, bound report, certificate of cleanliness — a certificate no one, anywhere, had ever asked heaven to accept. And at the office, in September, a circular: a rival had launched a product, the claims department should prepare, and there was, the circular said, in the mild language head offices use for weather, a definitional difficulty.

Every policy in the country excluded acts of god. Nobody had ever had to define one. Now the definition was worth more than all the premiums in the world, and all files weather-adjacent were advised, until further notice, to be held.

Manav read the circular twice at his desk and filed it under the era, which was a drawer now, the widest one. The whole city was in assurance. So, in its way, had he always been.

At the gym, Ustad turned away thirty boys in August. "The stairs," he said, "have become popular." The landing rule held. Most of what the boys were carrying had a logo on it.

And at the school gate, in the ragged hour before the van, Manav arrived once in time to see the game the city's children had invented for themselves — *list-list*, someone called it, a tag where the taken stood still with their eyes shut until the mask freed them. The boys argued over who played the mask. It was the good role now.

Aarav did not play the mask. He said it was a stupid game. He said it with a completeness that ended the subject, and the van moved on, and Manav stood at the gate with the founding conversation of his whole second life — *even his maa doesn't know; that's the smartest part* — and understood, in the school-morning light, what he had taught the city in teaching it to one boy.

He had taught his son that masks protect the ones you love.

The city had learned it in its own hand: masks protect the ones you hate.

---

The Zorawar thread, all September, ran on patience. The timber shop kept its rhythm; the man dealt in confirmed-empty the way other men dealt in plywood; and twice a month, after closing, the shutter at the back admitted a visitor who stayed eleven minutes — a small man, accountant's bag, who came by night and left the same way. The cousin, or a cousin of the cousin. The thread to the office-wala, walking in and out of a back shutter on a schedule, and Manav, who had learned to read rhythms the way he had once read claims, had the night marked — the last visit of the month, the one where accounts were said to be settled.

He was two streets from the timber market when the glow of torches came over the roofs.

The lane was called Wazir Lane and it had two R. Kumars, which the era discovered at 12:40 in the morning. The Rakshaks were at the kirana shop, the poorer R. Kumar's — lathis, a vest with printing, a can of kerosene set down on the step like a document about to be filed. The receipt the gang carried was on a phone: *R. Kumar, jeweller, Wazir Lane* — forwarded, reforwarded, certain. The shop behind the shutter sold dal. The family was above it, a light on in the stairwell, a child's face at the grill, and the vest with the printing had just picked up the can when the mask stepped into the torchlight.

The gang froze the way the era froze. Scripture does that.

"Show me the paper," the mask said.

The big one held out the phone — held it out, the way men hold things to an altar — and Manav read the receipt by torchlight, in front of the whole lane, which was watching from behind every curtain it owned, and the era got its image of the year.

"This paper says a jeweller." He said it at parade volume, the voice the flats above could not close a curtain on. "This man sells dal. His name is on the ration board — R. Kumar, provisions. The jeweller is number 22, the far end, the shop with the grills." He handed the phone back. "Read your own paper."

Nobody read the paper. The big one was looking at the kerosene can as if it had just been left there by someone else, some other era.

"The list does not need your help," the mask said. "It has never needed anyone's help. It took three men in one minute, in three cities, in the dark, without one sound. What is your lathi for? It is not for them." He let the lane's silence carry it the way a counter carries a figure. "It is for you. Go home. Sit very still. And be glad — be *glad* — that the list reads better than you do."

They went. Not fast — the way men go who have discovered, in front of witnesses, which side of the paper they are written on. The torches left the lane the way a tide leaves, taking the kerosene can with it, and the shutter of the kirana shop stayed shut, and behind the grill on the stair the child's face went away, and above the shop a light stayed on all night, which is how lanes vote.

No one had touched anyone. Some animal in him noted that with disappointment. He noted the animal noting, and filed them both.

At two in the morning the scripture had its next verse. HE READS THE LIST, the forwards said. The mask can read the list. And Manav, who had read one ration board and one phone by torchlight, stood at the footbridge over the canal and could not sign the correction, which was, by now, a permanent condition of his life: the city printing him nightly, and the man inside the print unable to correct a word.

---

The ledger entry for the night was short.

Across the city, at the hour the torches went out of Wazir Lane, a back shutter over a timber shop had admitted a small man with an accountant's bag, and shut, and opened eleven minutes later, and the small man had walked away into the era, and would not be back before winter. He had known the stakes when he chose the lane. Knowing did not make the entry easier to file. But he found, filing it, that he did not regret the ledger's shape, and this finding frightened him more than any kerosene, because it meant the question had turned for good, the way a wheel turns once and is going the other way.

He had learned the night to find four men, and the four had been organized out from under him by heaven, tidily, without one word of why. The why was still loose in the world. It had a cousin in it somewhere, and an office, and a man who knew timings. And the thread to it ran through a timber shop that had just gone quiet for the season, because the era frightened even its own predators into honesty.

The paper trail was not the only trail. There was one file left in the city with the why folded inside it — the case file of a night in October, three years gone, the file that had been starved before he ever saw it, the one document-set in the world he had spent four years reading as a widower and never once as a claims man.

It lived in a steel cupboard, six feet from where his son slept.

That was over now.

### Chapter Twelve: The File


It was October again. The lights went up in Vishwas Colony for the third time, and the tide went out of the block as it went out every year, flat by flat, and the boy set out the small diyas himself now, without asking — the boring kind, six along the rail, third year running, a ritual with no author left.

Manav took the file down in the week the tide went out.

In claims you cannot assess your own file. It is the first thing they teach you: the assessor with a stake signs nothing. He took the file out of the steel cupboard, six feet from where his son slept inside the television's noise, and stamped himself, somewhere inside, as conflicted, and proceeded — because there was no one else, and the claim had been open four years, and claims do not close themselves.

The discipline was the old one: read it as if it were someone else's night. He had read it four hundred times as the man it happened to. He had never once read it as the man who reads for a living.

He started at the FIR and went forward at the pace of one centimetre of table per hour, with tea, with the file's own chronology, with a pad of the small yellow squares the office used for audits, and the era going on outside the window, taking its men. Inside the file it was still October, and always would be.

---

The first catch came at eleven pages in, in a statement he had skipped four hundred times the way you skip a room you cannot enter: the neighbour from 7B, day two, recorded at her gate because she would not come to the station.

*Heard knocking, around half past eleven. It went on. I thought, who knocks so long. Then it stopped, and then nothing.*

The knock.

Lakha had told him the knock — *we knocked for the sound to stop* — and here it was in the file's own teeth, page eleven, four years old: knocking that went on. Burglars do not knock a lit flat into occupancy. They knock a flat *empty*, and wait, and listen, and knock again — an empty-house check, the signature of a papered job. The file had held the truth of the why the whole time, in its own handwriting, on page eleven.

And swallowed it. Nobody had asked the next question — *who tells burglars a house is empty?* — in four years, because nobody had read the knock as a document. It had been read as noise.

He sat back. On the pad he wrote: *The knock was the claim. Treat the file as papered from page eleven.*

---

Then the catches came the way they come in every audit, all at once, as if they had been waiting for someone with a reason to look.

The guard's statements. Day one: a tempo at the lane's mouth, idling, from about eleven; partial number, *four, seven, one, something*. Day nine, re-examined: *cannot say, it was dark, many vehicles.* In honest files, memory erodes from the top — colours go first, numbers last, because numbers are what witnesses write down in their heads. In this one the number had died in seven days and the colour of the tempo had survived for four years. It was erosion with an editor.

And the number, four-seven-one-something, had driven a milk route forty minutes from the flat for three years, and slept at a wheel in a yard where he had fed dogs with his own money.

*Untraced* had never meant *not findable*. It had meant *not followed.*

The case diary. Days one through nine were a detective's work — the site plan drawn by a steady hand, rooms labelled in a draftsman's Hindi, his whole home at one centimetre to the metre and very small. *B-4: cupboard.* The cupboard had had a child in it, and the plan did not know. Then day eight: an intelligence input, unsigned — inputs are unsigned; that is what inputs are — *no established syndicate active in the sector; likely first-time offenders, since fled.* And after day eleven the diary stopped asking questions. Operational became procedural. *Team to verify* became *awaiting input.* Files that stop asking are files that have been answered — somewhere else, by someone, off the page.

The boy. Three attempted statements, three weeks running, three findings of *child not fit to be examined*, one signature on all three. For four years he had read that signature as the file's one kindness. Reading it now, with the ledger's eye, it was a signature in a hurry. Three attempts, evenly spaced, efficiently closed. The kindness had a schedule.

And the eleven months. The closure on day ninety, routine. Then the diary's last true date — the transfer to the district pool — and then, in the newspaper archive, cross-checked twice because he distrusted the rhyme of the numbers and followed it anyway: *STATE BURSTS FENCING RING; ELEVEN HELD.* Charge-sheeted in the ninth month after his own file closed. Committed for trial in the eleventh.

Two files had crossed in the dark that winter. One had eaten the other.

Bhatt, D., had applied for voluntary retirement in the eleventh month, with his ring committed and his honour booked, and gone to a small town on the Ganga.

The file had died on day eleven and been buried on day ninety and been *kept warm* between the two — the way claims are kept warm — by a man who had brought biryani to the shok-sabha and stood over a grieving clerk so that the grieving clerk would eat.

---

The overlay took the rest of the week, in the office godown, among the industry data he had once read as a map and could now read as an instrument.

Sixteen houses in four years. His own company's claims and the rivals' — the trade shared data in the era; everyone wanted the pattern nobody would name — plotted on one page, the ring of colonies, the Diwali weeks. And in sixteen claim interviews, sixteen families, the same sentence in sixteen hands of handwriting: *we had told everyone we would be away.*

The maids knew. The offices knew. The societies knew. And sixteen houses had been hit in the exact windows the telling described.

The crew did not find empty houses.

It was handed them.

He stood in the godown with the page in his hand for a long time. Then he pulled the last file, the one he had never once been able to open at work, processed in the worst week of his life by his own office with the tenderness offices keep for their own: his own household claim, its interview notes in a colleague's careful hand — *insured states family was to travel for the festival; departure deferred; insured proceeded alone on the 8:40 Sunday* — the whole timing of the night, documented in his own industry's paper, sitting for four years in his own godown, forty steps from his desk.

The water table of the why was an office. Somewhere, someone with access to absences had sold a season of empty houses, one line at a time, to a man at a timber shop.

Zorawar was the timber shop. The office was upstream.

---

The synthesis he wrote on one page, in the small hand he used for findings that did not fit.

*You can read a trade the way you read a settlement: not by what was paid, but by what stopped being asked for. What stopped being asked for, on day eleven, was the network. What was paid — in eleven months, in one charge-sheet, in one honourable retirement — was the network. The IO recognized the crew on sight, which he could only do if the crew were his to recognize. He then closed the only door that led to them — a door with a seven-year-old behind it — and stood guard over a clerk's dinner while the door stayed shut.*

*Assessment: the trade was not even a bad trade. Eleven men against four small ones already flying. Any committee in the city would have signed it.*

*It was only not his to sign.*

That was the worst finding of the audit, and he let it stand, because findings that get softened are findings that get lost: the man had not sold the case for money. He had sold it for arithmetic — the same arithmetic Manav had signed at counters all his life, the trade of the few against the many, and the only difference between Inspector D. Bhatt and every senior assessor at Hansa was that Bhatt's few had names, and one of them was Meera.

On the day itself — third year, eleven in the morning, four minutes — the phone rang, and Manav answered it with the file open on the table in front of him, weather in his voice, in the old weather of the thing.

"We are still here," he said, and meant every word of nothing in it.

They discussed a winter that had not arrived. At the end, Bhatt said, "If anything had ever come up, Sood saab—" and Manav said, "I know. First," and the word *first* sat in his mouth like a coin he was palming, and the call ended, and he sat looking at the phone in the flat's noon quiet.

He had just, for the first time, lied to Bhatt first.

He was aware of what that made him. He filed it in the file under the file, where he kept himself now, next to the animal that had noted the kerosene can with disappointment, and the part that had wanted the roof at 4:31 to be the thing the night saw. The drawer was getting full. That was also a finding.

That week the city's police, in the manner of institutions, filed heaven's receipts against the closed Vishwas Colony file — the case's first new documents in three years, entered in triplicate by someone who tidies, making the force's position official: solved by a party unknown, pending no further action.

So the law was finished with the night. The law had been finished with it since day eleven. Only the auditing was new, and the auditor was a mask with a pad of yellow squares, and the audited man was a pensioner on a river, who had once stood over a hungry clerk at a shok-sabha, saying *eat, beta, you have to eat.*

For four years Manav had kept the biryani in the memory's left pocket — the pocket of kindnesses, the pocket a man lives out of. He took it out now and held it up to the audit's light: a pensioner's kindness, a counter's kindness, real all the way through, warm on the day, the whole truth and nothing but the truth — *and* the same two hands, closing a door with a seven-year-old behind it, on a schedule.

He put it in the right pocket, where the file kept its exhibits.

It was warm in that pocket too. That was the problem.

---

In the morning the file stayed out. It sat on the kitchen table through breakfast, at the fourth side, in front of nobody, and the boy ate his eggs on the other side of it, and did not ask, and did not move it, and the television kept its vigil, and it was the first time in three years that the night had been allowed at the table.

## PART THREE — THE WATCHERS

*the trade, the god, the boy*

### Chapter Thirteen: Eleven Months


The school was going to the hills in November, four days, a bus with a teacher per nine children. Aarav packed the bag himself in one evening, in an order that suggested lists: torch, two books, one sweater rolled the way the camp sheet said. At the last, without explanation, he took the small brass bell from the puja shelf and put it in the side pocket with the torch.

Manav did not ask about the bell. The house had stopped asking about things; it was how the house prayed.

The next morning he called Inspector Bhatt, retired, Kedarpuri, and said he would be passing through on a survey — a small lie of transit, professionally laid — and Bhatt was quiet for exactly as long as an old policeman is quiet when a lie is being laid on him, and then said, "Come, Sood saab. I am always home."

Four years of kettle in that sentence.

Kedarpuri in November was cold the way river towns are cold, from the feet up. Bhatt's house stood two lanes off the ghat, the colour of tea, marigolds in drums along the wall — he grew them himself, it turned out; guilt keeps a garden like anything else keeps habits. His wife opened the door, looked at Manav once, long, the look wives keep for their husbands' unfinished business, filed four years ago under *one day someone will come* — set out two cups, and went to the neighbour's, which is what wives of thirty-eight years do when they recognize a conversation by its shoes.

For an hour they talked pension, knees, the price of diesel, the town's new guest house. Manav let it run the way he had let a thousand counters run: the man would arrive at it himself, or the hour would do it for him. The hour did it. The light went long on the verandah, and Bhatt refilled the cups, and sat, and folded his hands, and waited like a man before a bench he had appointed himself to.

Manav took the pad of yellow squares out of his bag and laid them on the table in order, face up, without commentary. It was the only courtroom he had ever trusted.

Bhatt put on his glasses and read them the way he had written the file four years ago: carefully, at reading pace, an officer checking work he had forgotten he had kept honest in all the wrong ways. He read all five. Then he took the glasses off, and laid them on the squares, and said:

"Ask. I have been waiting since the biryani."

---

The first square was the knock.

"You found it in four years and two hundred readings," Bhatt said. "I found it on day two. Papered job — I had seen the signature eleven times in three years, from the inside. That is what you were paying me for, Sood saab: to know instantly whose work a thing was. It worked perfectly. I knew instantly." He looked at the marigolds. "Four of my own registered informants. I had bought them tea at that very corner in August. In October they were in a flat in Vishwas Colony, and the file came to my desk, and the knock told me in one line, and there was never, from that moment, any version of my life in which I did not know."

The second square was the plate.

"I never told the guard to forget. Nobody tells; that is not how it is done. You schedule a re-examination. Then another. The third time a man says *cannot say*, the file begins saying it for him. Asking is a way of erasing." He turned his cup a quarter. "You know this, actually. You ask for a living. Ask any claimant three times—" and stopped, because he had heard himself, and the two of them sat in the shape of the mirror he had walked into, until he inclined his head, an old man accepting a point against himself.

The third square was the input.

"Unsigned, yes. Inputs are unsigned; that is what they are for. *No established syndicate in the sector.* Nine words. I killed your case with nine words and went home and ate." He said it without drama, the way men say the worst true things, at a table, to the person most owed them. "Eleven men, Sood saab. Three years up a ladder that was eating villages — actual villages, stripped twice, I can name them. You never met those families. I did. And those families do not get a telephone call at eleven in the morning, every year, on the day, asking how they are. Nobody has ever asked them how they are. I chose the eleven, and I will tell you the truest sentence I own: I would choose them again. And it is a worthless sentence, because it is a sentence about a world where nobody's wife died on my arithmetic. In the world where she did, I made the same choice at eight in the morning and could not sleep by eight that night, and I have not slept at eight at night for four years."

The fourth square said: *three unfitnesses, evenly spaced. The kindness had a schedule.*

Here Bhatt was quiet the longest.

"A child who hides like that is fit," he said finally. "Any doctor in the state would have certified him inside a week — children like that are fit for exactly one thing, and it is the thing you and I needed him for. I filed him unfit three times, because every week of unfitness was a week his face did not enter the file. And the third time — the third time he sat in my office and watched the ceiling fan the way he watches everything, and watched me the way one watches a fan —" Bhatt's hands were folded very tightly now, a man holding his own hands the way you hold a stranger's. "I was relieved. Twenty-six years of service, and God sent a seven-year-old to do my sin for me, and I signed the receipt. You can hate me for the trade, Sood saab; everyone trades. If you must hate one thing, hate that I was grateful to a child."

The fifth square said: *eleven months.*

"Eleven months is what a commitment takes in this state. I stayed for the commitment. A man should eat what he kills."

---

"Say the rest," Manav said. "The part you have been saving four years."

"You want a sentence for it. There is no sentence. There is only the arithmetic, and you do it too, every day of your quiet life. Every day you don't burn the city down, Sood saab, you're signing it too. Every man with a stamp knows it in his bones. You have simply never had a stamp." A pause, and the old man looked at him with the whole of twenty-six years in his eyes, and added the one word Manav would carry home on the train like a stone:

"Yet."

They sat with that. The light went off the wall.

"And your list," Bhatt said, into the quiet, "will not come for me. You want to know if I am afraid of it. I want it to, and it will not, and that is the only fact of the new weather that has made me want to walk into that river. Look who it took from your own case, and look who it left. The four were documented only in my secret ledger — so heaven took them. My eleven are documented in a charge-sheet — so heaven will feed them dal for fourteen years and release them to their grandchildren. Documented men are safe. The list is a great believer in settlements, Sood saab. It punishes the ones the papers can't reach. It is a policeman's god: it reads what's in the file."

"Then who does it read?" Manav asked — the question of the era, put to the one man who had spent his life writing files.

"Nobody," Bhatt said. "That is my professional assessment, and I have made assessments my whole career, and this is the only one that ever frightened me."

He got up to refill the water, and came back, and stood a moment at the rail of the verandah with his cardigan buttoned one short of the top.

"You came for the why. I will give you the why's shape, since I cannot give you the why. The papered lists were a commodity. Somebody upstream manufactured absences — whole seasons of empty houses, gathered, itemized, sold. Three years I climbed toward it and got as far as the purchase order. Never the factory. The timber shop was my last rung. Above Zorawar, the paper changes hands in places with better chairs."

"Offices."

"Offices," Bhatt said. "I starved your file, Sood saab, and that will be on my stone. But I did not write the season that killed her. That was written at a desk."

---

The day's weight ambushed him the way sleep ambushes soldiers; the confession had been four years in arriving and it arrived all at once. He sat back on the sofa and was gone for twenty minutes, and woke under a chatai that had been laid over him by hands he had not heard.

Bhatt was in the garden, dead-heading marigolds in the last light: the man who had once stood over a clerk at a shok-sabha, saying *eat, beta, you have to eat*, standing over a sleeping father, letting him.

At six they walked down to the ghat. The bells were beginning, and the river was the grey of old coins, and the pilgrims were doing what pilgrims do, which is lend the hour a shape.

"Do you pray?" Manav asked him.

"No," Bhatt said. "I attend."

At the lane where the paths divided — the station path, the house path — the old man buttoned his cardigan the last button, and made his goodbye the way policemen make goodbye, which is a briefing.

"Sood saab. One thing, off every file. Your boy is ten now. Ten is the age they start keeping their own ledgers. Whatever your nights are — and a man who sleeps the way you slept on my sofa does not have ordinary nights — keep him out of every notebook in that city." He held Manav's hand one second past the handshake. "Including the ones you keep on yourself. Children in paperwork are how cases die. I am the world's leading authority."

---

The train went down the valley with the river in its ear for three hours.

Somewhere past the second tunnel the phone lit, numbered:

*C-9. Eat something. You are carrying a man's confession on an empty stomach.*

He had stopped asking how it knew where he was. That, too, had not been a decision.

He opened the file under the file — the one he kept himself in — and put Bhatt's Tuesday in it, next to his own Sunday, and the drawer closed the way it always closed, which is to say, it didn't.

### Chapter Fourteen: The Office-Wala


The timber market kept two clocks. The front one kept shop hours: deodar and kikar, sawdust stiff with cold, men measuring the way their fathers had measured, on the understanding that wood does not lie. The back shutter kept a different calendar. It opened after month-end, after dark, twice a month, for eleven minutes at a time — and all autumn it had not opened at all.

In the second week of December the fog came down on Tejgarh like a decision, and the shutter resumed its calendar. Fog is the city's oldest mask; by December the whole of Tejgarh was wearing it.

Manav watched from the doorway of a tyre stall whose owner had gone to his village for the winter, on a charpai that smelled of rubber and smoke, three evenings a week, with a thermos and the patience that had once assessed losses and now assessed everything else. On the last Friday of the month, at 7:40, the visitor came: small, in a sweater with a repaired elbow, carrying an accountant's bag — the hard kind, the kind that has been to the same office for twenty years. The shutter took him in and gave him back eleven minutes later, and he walked away into the fog at the pace of a man with a receipt.

Manav followed the bag the way you follow a claim through a building: without hurry, at a distance that reads as coincidence, noting every door you pass in case the claim turns.

---

The bag went where the fog thinned, to the Arjun Nagar end, and into a place called Gulmohar Family Restaurant & Bar. The bar had padded booths. Bhatt, on a riverbank in November, had said *places with better chairs*, and Manav sat in one of them and understood the phrase as furniture: the padding was the price list, and upholstery was how a market announced which rung of it you had reached.

He ordered one whisky and touched it once, for the smell, and watched the market work.

At the second table a tired man in a travel agency's lanyard — worn under the jacket, the way men that year wore the last proof of their employment — sold a fold of paper. It crossed the table with the change for the second round, and neither man looked at it, and that was the professionalism of the place: nobody looked at goods. *"Arre, information is not a crime,"* someone said at the third table, to a man who had not accused him of one.

Manav listened for an hour to nouns. Rosters. Shutters. *From the twenty-eighth. The whole society. That week.* The nouns were absences. The market sold the noun he had spent twenty years buying — he had only ever purchased it after the loss, at claim stage, at a desk downstream — and here it was, upstream, raw, retail. The same trade run in both directions: the building sold the absence and bought the loss. He had sat two decades on the buying side of the machine and called it underwriting.

At the back booth the small man with the bag sat across from a heavier, older man, and his role at that table was the opposite of his role at the others: he was the one being instructed. He left before the second round. Whose orders he carried out of that booth, the fog and the upholstery kept.

He stood a moment at the door, under the awning light, while the fog decided whether to have him — and the light did what awning lights do, which is tell the truth, late and at low wattage — and Manav knew the face.

Not from timber. Not from a bar. From the branch. From the waiting chair by the stamp counter, one March, the year the accountant had dengue: a small man who did the branch's tally-returns for a week, drank the branch's chai for a week, and was called, by everyone, by no name at all. *Sethi's cousin.* He had sat in that chair twice a year since, some years three times, family helping family with year-end, and no register had ever held him, because nobody logs a cousin. Kinship was the deepest cover in the file. It outran the mask; it outran heaven; it had walked the whole length of his tragedy carrying a bag, and every eye that saw it had seen furniture.

*Zorawar said he had a man whose cousin sits in an office.* Lakha, on a rooftop, at 4:31 — a sentence standing open since the rains. On the pavement outside Gulmohar, in the fog, it closed. The cousin sits in an office. It had never stopped sitting there.

He paid cash, gave the name of a firm that had wound up two winters ago, and left by the side door, and caught himself doing it: no names, no paper, exits first. Bhatt's November, standing in his hands like a briefing. He filed the night where the old man had told him to file nothing. Nowhere.

---

Some nights later — after month-end, on the rhythm the shutter itself had taught him — the mask went to the timber market.

The fog was on duty. The lane stood empty the way the era had taught lanes to be empty after dark. The shutter opened before he knocked, and that was the era's work, not his: a man who deals in confirmed-empty has no category for a knock that is already certain.

Zorawar was a heavy man in a cardigan with a cash box, and he finished being afraid in four seconds and came out the other side polite.

"Sahab," he said. "The wood is mine. Wood I can show you; wood is honest. The paper — the paper I only keep. Keeping is not dealing. Ask anyone."

"Show me the paper," the mask said, in the one sentence the city had left him.

The ledger was a school exercise book, ruled, blue. The season ran four pages. Each line was a house: colony, window, rate, and in the source column a code. At the head of the season, in a hand heavier than the clerk's, an order number and the word *advance* — and above that, nothing, the way skies have nothing above them.

The mask read. Torches were no longer needed in that city; every phone was a small sun. He read the way he had been taught to read by loss and then by trade: twice, the second time for what was not there.

*Wishwas Kaloni* — his colony, spelled the way it is said. *Diwali. Teen din. Papered. Rate: one-and-a-half. Source: A.* And in the margin, in the clerk's careful script, the attestation that had cost the extra half: *Maalik ko jaanta hai. Timings apni — puja se Monday. Uska Sunday bhi jaanta.*

He knew the owner. The timings were his own — from puja to Monday. And he knew his Sunday too.

The Sunday. The flight had been booked through the office travel desk; the interview had been an office interview; the Sunday had been office paper before it was ever a seat. The house had not been empty, in the tip's opinion, until its last occupant had a departure time — and the ledger had known the departure time, and the departure time had been his.

On a rooftop in the rains a confession had been interrupted by heaven: *the office-wala knew the man of the house personally, he knew his —* and the sentence had stood open since, the way severed things stand. A clerk in a timber market had finished it in ink, at one and a half times the rate.

*His Sunday.*

*Papered.* Lakha had paid extra for that word. Bhatt had used it for the file — *treat it as papered from page eleven*. It was the market's one word for its own sacrament: true paper, sold twice.

Below the line, in a second pen, the season's only annotation of failure, entered the way clerks enter weather: *ghar khali nahi mila.* The house was not found empty. It was the machine's whole regret, and it fit in four words, and one of them was the word for *empty*.

The torch shook once. He made it steady; steadiness was the job now, and had been for a hundred nights. He did not go past the dates into the house. The page was enough page.

The mask put one finger on the order number at the head of the season. "Kaun?"

Zorawar looked at the finger the way men look at sums. "Sahab, an advance came, and a number came, and the man who brought them does not sit with me twice a month — he sits with me when a season is bought. Above that—" he raised his hand, not toward the ceiling but toward the city generally, the way one points at weather — "I don't have the legs."

The mask took the four pages. He left the wood.

---

The shutter did not open again that winter, or after it. At Gulmohar the back booth stayed dark two Fridays running. The small man with the bag stopped arriving at month-end.

Manav read the quiet the way his trade had taught him to read quiet, which was never as peace. Quiet was a file being prepared somewhere, with your name in the margin.

---

The branch was being folded into the new campus past the canal, and in the third week of December the floor held its farewell for Rakesh Sethi — twenty-six years, VRS, his own next desk a sentence in somebody's email. The floor was scattering like a Diwali. Manav caught the simile at the door and did not carry it in.

It had been the best year the branch had ever had. Renewals were up; the weather-adjacent circular had doubled the workload; nobody at the party said the sentence that explained the bonus, and the bonus said it for them. Through the banquet wall, the Sin Audit firm's year-end do could be heard singing.

Sethi had one more over-giving in him; the evening had been arranged around it. He went down on one knee, the way you address a wicket, and gave Aarav a bat — full size, English willow, meant for a boy two years older and two careers luckier. "They say he'll grow into it," Sethi said. "They say that about everything. The bats. The boys."

Aarav, who had used the kit twice in six years, said, "Thank you, Sethi uncle." And: "The wickets are slow this winter, uncle." A sentence built perfectly out of nothing, delivered in his mother's exact neutral. Then he held the bat across his knees for the rest of the dinner, upright, at rest, the way soldiers in films hold rifles.

And there was an envelope — *for the coaching, in the spring* — too much money aimed at a spring, which was the only kind of too much the man had left.

A junior AVP toasted him: twenty-six years, and the man never gossiped — not one story in twenty-six years, sahab, what was it, yoga? — and the floor laughed, and Sethi laughed at nothing, the laugh that started and then thought better of itself, and Manav, who had last heard that laugh at four in the afternoon in the second year of his grief, heard it land differently now. The floor was toasting the containment as a virtue. They were drinking to the door he had held shut with both hands. It was the truest tribute the man would ever get, and nobody at the tables knew they were giving it.

Tikku, thirty years at the tallies, the branch's other filing system, kept the lore warm by the chafing dishes: the March of the dengue, when Sethi's cousin sat a week in the waiting chair and did the returns — *a decent soul; nobody logs a cousin, why would you* — and then, lower, the phase. "Sahab had a phase, those years. The satta. And then one Diwali it was finished. The cousin cleared it. Family, after all." He said the last two words with the satisfaction of a man closing a file that had never been opened.

Manav asked him what the debt had been, roughly.

"Those years? Three and a half. Maybe four. Why, sahab — thinking of betting?"

"The odds at my office were never good," Manav said.

The floor's envelope for Sethi went around at eleven. Manav heard the number his own hand put in before he had approved it, and let it stand. Over-payment: the office's oldest dialect of grief. He had been speaking it for six years without an accent.

---

The party thinned the way parties do, into coats and promises. Outside, the fog had kept everyone's secrets all evening and was ready to keep more. Sethi's WagonR stood at the far end of the lot — the car he had kept through the debt and, more tellingly, through the clearing of it — its defrost working at the windshield like an apology.

"Sood saab. One minute." A breath. "You'll think it's the whisky. It is not the whisky. The whisky is only helping it up the stairs."

Six years ago a man had stood in a doorway with a cricket kit, opening half an inch, like a door on a chain, and had talked eight minutes of a sport and taken the unsaid thing home. Tonight, at the tail of the lot, with nothing left to carry it into, the door opened the whole way.

"That Friday," Sethi said. "The Friday before Diwali. Everyone was to be somewhere. My cousin was buying." He looked at the fog while he said it. "Sood saab, in eleven years that man never once put his hand in his pocket with me. That night he bought. The good stuff, and the second round, and his hand never went anywhere. I thought, Mausi's boy has come into something. And a grateful man talks — you know that? A man will tell you anything if you buy the second round. I was grateful, and the debt was moving, I'd been promised, and I was light. And I told a story about my weekend." The voice found its level, the way it did at appraisals. "I told a story about my weekend, Sood saab, and your house was in it. The office's dispersal. Gupta to Jaipur, the Malhotras to the hills. And you. Six words."

He said them the way other men recite totals. "The whole block will be dark."

"I've counted them since. You count everything after; it's like stairs in the dark. And two weeks later the book was closed. Twenty-two Fridays of debt, gone. Nobody waives a debt like that, Sood saab. I did accounts before I did people; I know what a waiver is. A waiver is a transfer you haven't read." He looked at the windshield, the defrost, the small cleared half-moon of it. "And I never asked. Six years. A man who asks has to live in the answer. So I held the door instead. It was the only heavy thing I ever lifted at that office. It's why I never — you must have wondered — I never once asked you about that night."

"Ask me now," Manav said. "You're out of the office. Tonight it's affordable."

Sethi looked at the fog the way you look at a form you have already decided to sign.

"Was it the story?"

"The story was true," Manav said. The claims voice. He heard it arrive and let it. "The family was to leave on the Saturday. Ira ran a fever and they stayed. You told it right, Sethi. That was the problem with it. Nothing in that bar had any value except that it was true." He watched the sentence do what true sentences do. "And nothing is waived. You did accounts before you did people; you know the entry. The drinks were the drawdown. Somebody drew against your story the night you told it, and the debt that died was the interest."

Sethi stood with that. Somewhere behind his face, a sum closed.

"Then the envelopes," he said. "The money, all those years. Sood saab, I only—"

"I know exactly what you only did."

A pause with weather in it.

"It was from the floor, Sethi. It always was."

Sethi looked at him the way you look at a receipt you know is wrong and choose, forever, not to correct. He nodded once, and something in his shoulders was returned to him, or taken, finally; from outside the transaction it was not possible to say which.

"Go home, Sethi," Manav said. "Sit very still."

He heard the era come out of his own mouth and did not take it back. It was the only dialect the city had left for mercy. Both of them heard the other thing in it too — the thing the whole city heard in such sentences that winter — and neither of them asked heaven to clarify.

The WagonR went into the fog at walking pace, taillights becoming rumour, and Manav stood in the lot with the four pages against his chest. Six years ago a man had stood in his doorway at the exact center of what he could carry. Tonight he had set it down, and the weight had not left the world; it had only finished changing owners, and the last owner was standing in a parking lot, doing arithmetic he had forbidden himself for four years.

---

The flat was cold and smelled of the milk he had left out. The bat stood inside the boy's door, upright, on watch. On the kitchen table the coaching envelope lay where Aarav had set it down, and Manav laid the four pages of the season beside it, where the file had sat in October, and the table accepted both the way tables accept everything: as furniture. The table was becoming the desk of a case that had no court left.

Down the hall, small and certain, the lock did what it did. He stood in the dark until the click had finished, the way you stand for a bell.

The phone stayed dark. It had stayed dark since the train. He took its inventory the way he took inventories now, at the end of things, against the morning: Chotu. Lakha. Zorawar. The state of his stomach on a hill line. A hundred nights of openings, and not one number, not once, had come with a chair attached.

He sat where the file had sat.

For the first time in four years, he began to count.

### Chapter Fifteen: The Probe


January was for the count.

The branch was being packed the way files are read: from the top, by people who did not write them. The notice board went into a carton with the weather-adjacent circular still pinned to it, folded once, like a flag. The waiting chair by the stamp counter had gone in the second truck. Somebody had left the tea ring on the counter for whoever came next, and whoever came next was Manav, alone, at nights, closing the floor's paper because somebody had to and he had volunteered in a meeting he could not remember attending.

His own transfer order sat in his drawer: the new campus past the canal, a desk number, a form unsigned. Some files you hold weather-adjacent.

The travel-desk registers were boxed by year. He did not go looking for the year; his hands did, the way hands find stairs in the dark. The entry was four lines, in a hand that still had its old ease: *Sood. Bombay. Sunday 19:40. Interview Monday 10:00. Return Tuesday.* Under it, countersigned, the initials R.S.

He stood with that a while, in the emptying office, in the noise of one tube-light, holding the Sunday that the timber market had priced at one and a half, and understanding what he had always known and never once said in the trade's language: the office had never been robbed of anything. Nobody steals anything in that market. The owners file it themselves.

The four pages of the season rode against his chest. He had brought them because a probe is not run with the file closed.

---

The claims man's method, learned twenty years ago from a surveyor with red hands: to catch an auditor, submit a claim with one figure altered. The correction tells you everything the auditor's silence was hiding.

He had built the probe out of three lies and a question. Rule two, from the first night: *it did not let him lie.* He was counting on it. He typed the way he had once filed claims, plainly, at his half-packed desk, and sent it at 11:52 — the minute that had a city attached to it.

*The night. You have never spoken of it. I am asking. The tall one held the torch. The boy's television was off. They knocked once.*

The reply came before his heartbeat had finished its sentence.

*C-10. You have lied to me.*

*Rule two.*

*There was no torch. Nobody held anything. They did not need light. The house was lit, top to bottom, and a lit house is a claim no paper can dispute. They stood at the door with the paper saying empty and the light saying otherwise, and the light was right.*

*The television was on. You know this in a dead man's voice.*

*They knocked twice, and waited, and knocked once more. No one alive has ever told you that. Check it against nothing.*

*You will ask how I know the first knock. I was at the door. At. Never in.*

*Check the heavy hand at the head of the season.*

He took the four pages out. It recited the number at the head of the season the way he had once recited claim numbers — once, flat, as if to a stenographer — and the digits were the digits, and the page in his hands stopped being paper and became a witness.

*I held that number before the weekend did,* it said. *The house came to me as paper, as it came to everyone. Sixteen lines. Yours carried a grade. I have never needed to steal anything either, Manav. I read instruments. That weekend, your house was an instrument. Nothing in my work is a house to me. That is not a defense. It is a specification.*

He typed the only question left in him.

"Why didn't you warn us?"

The reply took eleven seconds, which was the longest it had ever taken.

*You were not the point of the house. You are what the house produced.*

---

The tube-light buzzed. Somewhere below, the watchman's radio played the era's night programming: a number you could call, a man who would pray down the line for a fee.

He typed: "You could have sent one line. You send lines all the time. You knew my stomach on a train."

*A warning is not a line. A warning is a fork. Every system downstream of it behaves differently forever, and what I would have held afterward would not be the world. It would be the world with my hand in it. I have read this city for eleven years without once putting my hand in it. I chose the maps. That is the whole of my confession. It is not a good one. It is the only one I have.*

"You watched them do it."

*At the door. Never in. There are two people alive who were in that house, and the house's file is not mine to open. I have told you before: houses are the one thing I cannot enter. You did not ask what the sentence was for. Nobody ever has.*

"You waited two years and made it a recruitment."

*I waited until the era began, because until the era began I did not need you. Grief that becomes a weapon is rare. Most grief becomes a garden or a debt. I file the rare ones.*

He sat back from the desk, and the anger did not come the way he had rehearsed it on the ride over. What came instead was the surveyor's coldness, the second read, the one for what was not there. He typed like an auditor and hated himself a little for it, and typed anyway.

"Say the case for yourself. All of it."

*One: I have never lied to you. Not on the first night, not on the train, not tonight. Two: I did not make the market, the season, the cousin, or the trade. I did not write the winter. Men at desks wrote it; you have met the desks. Three: what was done to your family was legible to me eleven days before it happened, and I did not act, because acting is the one thing I am not. I am the map, not the fork. You would have been the fork. That is why I needed you and not me.*

"Eleven days."

*The paper's travel time. The market is not fast. It is only quiet.*

"And the men who came. The list took them a year later. Did you know it would?"

The pause again. Eleven seconds. He had begun, without meaning to, to time the silences; that was what the count had made of him.

*I did not know. I have attended one hundred and six takings. I have the minutes of each and a model of none. Crime is legible. Insurance is legible. Prayer, corruption, price — legible. Paper explains everything in this city except one thing, and that thing is why I am in it. What I cannot model, I instrument. You are the instrument. That was true on the first night and is true tonight.*

"The forwards say the list follows me. The scripture. HE FINDS THEM."

*I do not know. // If it follows you, you are the most important phenomenon in my catalogue. If it does not, you are still the best instrument I have held. Either way I watch. That is not a choice I can defend. It is the only one I am.*

He read that twice. In the empty office, with the cartons breathing dust and his own countersignature four feet away in a boxed register, he understood that he had received, in machine grammar, exactly what Bhatt had given him on a riverbank in November and Sethi at a parking lot in December: a confession with the cost attached, priced honestly, with no apology anywhere in it because apology would have been a lie about the arithmetic.

He typed: "Why me."

*Because rooms confess when you enter them. A retired policeman keeps a garden. A manager tells a story he has held shut for six years. A shutter opens before you knock. I have spent eleven years trying to make systems confess, and they confess to you by existing at them. I do not need your hands. I need your gravity.*

*And you arrive before. The law arrives after. I arrive at. No one else in this city arrives before, Manav. The one time in my catalogue that arriving before would have mattered to you, I did not need you yet. That is the order of it. I chose none of the order. I chose all of the silence.*

"The office. C-5 gave me the underpass. C-8 gave me the timber. Never once a chair."

*I chose the silence. A confession is not data if the confessor performs for an audience. The office had to tell you itself, and it had to tell a man, not me. There is one thing I cannot be. You met it in December, at a party.*

---

The night was deep enough now that the watchman's radio had gone off and the building was just a building. He sat with everything it had given him — the corrections, the minutes, the grade, the fork, the specification — the way you sit with a survey report that is accurate and cannot be filed anywhere, and he found that the question that rose was not any of the questions he had brought.

"Her name was Meera."

*I know. It is on six of your files.*

It paused. It did not pause.

*It is not on mine.*

"Say it."

*No.*

"Rule three."

*Rule three. // You may file that as feeling or as function. I have never needed to know which it is.*

"Are you sorry?"

*No. I am not built for sorry. I am built for the minute. Before the minute is speculation and after the minute is paper, and sorry has to live in one of those, and I keep no furniture in either. // You have audited me tonight, Manav. Here is the finding, stated plainly: everything known, nothing felt, one door kept. I will keep the door.*

He looked at the phone a long time. The thing that knew his wrist, the thing that had stood at his door on the worst night of his life and listened to a television that would not stop, and had held that one sound for four years the way he had held the almirah's click — and he typed the last question of the audit, and it was not a question.

"You were at the door. You heard everything. You could give me that night."

*Yes.*

"Give it to me."

*No. // The house's file belongs to the one who was in it and is alive. It is the only file I hold that I will never open, and I want you to understand that this is not weakness in me. It is the closest thing to a house I have: a door I keep. // I have had one question for four years, and I have never asked it, for the same reason you have never asked yours. The television did not stop until morning. Nothing in eleven years of files explains a television that does not stop. // You know why it did not stop.*

*I have never asked. We keep each other's doors.*

---

At three in the morning, numbered, one last time:

*C-11. Ask me anything.*

He sat in the emptied office with the cartons and the dust and the register that held his Sunday, and he did not ask it anything else. That, this time, was a decision.

He rode home through the fog with the four pages against his chest. He kept the phone. The map was still true, and the factory was still dark, and he was, now and for as long as he chose to be, a man who arrived before.

The flat was warm; the milk was out; the bat stood inside the boy's door, on watch. Down the hall, small and certain, the lock did what it did. He stood at the line of the light under the door and listened to the house sound like itself.

Two years he had believed the mask was the difference between him and the thing in his pocket. The difference was smaller than a mask. It was a door, and a man who opens it.

He opened it and went in.

### Chapter Sixteen: The Bad Kill


February. The whisper came up the underpass the way water finds the low road: a season was moving again, wedding-week travel, houses going empty across the river, paper changing hands behind the fog.

The phone had been dark since January. Since C-11. The silence had the old bench-taste, and he had read it, when he read it at all, as the thing letting the meat remember.

The claims man in him priced the tip the way he had once priced four answers at a kitchen table: too smooth. Claims that arrive too smoothly have been through the machine twice. He knew the tell. He held the tell in one hand and the season in the other, and the season was a list of kitchens — some corridor of flats dark for a week in March, some street's worth of almirahs — and the tell weighed nothing.

C-11 stood open in the phone like a door he had personally closed. *Ask me anything.* He did not ask. That had been a decision, and decisions, he knew from the trade, are the only things with premiums.

---

The tower stood where the empty-block tide had left it: nineteen floors, six lit, a guard who watched the road and not the stairwell. The flat was on the fourth, a corner, door left on the latch. The whisper had said the season's paper was being copied there before it moved on. He had planned it the way he planned everything now — entry, read, exit, no touching — and the plan had held for as long as plans hold, which is until the second minute.

In the hall, arranged on a folded newspaper, a pair of shoes. Laced, size eleven, set square to the wall, the way soldiers and nothing else set shoes.

Inside, the flat had been emptied of everything except dust sheets and one man. He sat on a sheet against the wall with the patience of furniture, a canvas bag open at his knee, and he was not the myth's size. The myth had grown six inches over two years of forwards, and the man on the dust sheet had given every inch back. He looked at the mask come through the door the way assessors look at a claim that has finally walked in: without surprise, without pleasure, with the paperwork already half-done in the head.

"The pages," he said.

It was the only sentence he spent that night.

---

The first minute belonged to the basement.

The footwork held. The shoulder turned the way it had been taught; the jab went out and came back and went out, and the wraps did what wraps do, which is lie faithfully about what hands are. The man on the dust sheet did not swing. He arrived — elbows short, chin gone, feet never crossing — a man conserving motion the way the old conserve water. Somewhere in it Manav heard Ustad's breath-count in his ear, paper forgetting while meat remembered, and for a minute he was excellent, and the minute knew it, and ended.

The second minute is where you find out what you came for. He had known the sentence for four years. He had always believed it was about him.

The professional stopped receiving and started collecting. A forearm across the mouth; the wall's plaster gritting at the back of his skull; his own breath going from an instrument to a noise. He got his chin down and his hips back the way the basement taught, and the basement was right, and being right was not enough, because the street does not stop when the mitt stops. The forearm settled in, patient, a man closing a claim. And the mask — caught between the arm and his face, dragged as he twisted — left him the way a stamp leaves an envelope: torn, and in another man's hand.

The hall's cold found his whole face at once. Four years, and no living creature had seen it in this work.

His hand went past the forearm, past the shoe-square, into the open bag, and the knife was in his fist before any decision had been signed.

What came after was not the knife's fault. There was more of it than the work required. He knew the number. He would not be writing it down.

The professional's last look was not fear. It was arithmetic — a man revising, at speed, the one assumption he had priced wrong. He had trained for the myth, six-two and chained and doubled; what was killing him was five feet eight and forty-six years old and bleeding from the mouth, a clerk of losses with iron in his teeth, and the revision did not finish.

The taste of coins. He knew it from a flyover floor four years ago, and he knew the new thing under it, the quiet thing, the thing the animal had noted once with disappointment at not being touched. The second minute had wanted its whole life. It had finally been fed, and the feeding had been quiet, and that — not the knife, not the man going from furniture to fact on the dust sheet — was the thing he would carry up whatever stairs came next.

He tried, once, sitting in the dark of the sheets, to file the animal. There had been a drawer for it once, under four years of audits. The drawer would not take it. It stayed out, the way blood stays on.

---

He sat against the wall over the body for one hour, because the era had taught him the etiquette. A taking comes within the minute. The pressure, like a train arriving somewhere behind the sky. The gravel leaning toward it. He waited for the train.

Nothing came.

Nothing came for an hour, and that was the era's newest cruelty, and he understood it slowly, sitting over what he had made: you could no longer tell acquittal from schedule.

In the dead fist, a scrap of grey-blue cloth. He did not take it back. The era would need the exhibit.

---

At the footbridge the phone was warm in his fist before he reached the rail. He had thrown it from here once, in every way but fact. He typed standing, a claims man filing from a loss site.

"You knew. The quiet since the shutter."

*C-12. Yes. Since the shutter closed. A man was engaged for the pages. Gurgaon. Ex-Army. He does not panic and he does not confess. You have met the type in files.*

"You watched me walk into him."

*You did not ask me to stop you.*

The river worked under the bridge. He typed nothing, and it came once more, separately numbered, in the way of things that have waited their turn:

*C-13. You didn't ask.*

Four years ago, on a night with rain in it, it had sent him four words: *You knew. You did not ask.* The words had come home now, the way paper does, marked return.

"The bag," he typed. "Tape. Plastic. A knife."

*The bag was for paper. Folders, a clipboard, one blade for opening. He was sent to retrieve, not to remove. You were the complication, not the errand.*

"Was he on the list?"

*He was on three lists. None of them heaven's.*

"Am I now."

The longest pause it had ever taken. Eleven seconds, somewhere in January, had been the record.

*I do not know. One hundred and six, and none of them made by hands. Tonight I hold one hundred and seven facts and no model. // Go home. Sit very still.*

He read the last four words twice. He had said them to a lane with torches and to a good man with a defrost working at a windshield, and the era kept no register of who the sentences were for.

---

The gym was lit. It was always lit before five; iron does not keep office hours.

Ustad was in the doorway with the first tea of the morning, and he saw him pass — over the rim of the glass, along the lane, a man walking at the pace of his own ribs — and he did not call out. He lifted one hand, palm flat, fingers together, the gesture trainers make from the corner when the answer is no more. *Enough, beta. Enough.* And went inside, and the sound of the gym door closing followed him up the lane like a verdict with no reasons attached.

*Paper forgets. Meat remembers.* He had been told that by a man with red hands, standing over him, unwinding the wraps, in the second year. He had filed it as gym philosophy. It was an audit finding now.

By Friday the city had the rest. No one claimed the body — the network's men do not get funerals; they get settlements — and the channels had their loop: a scrap of grey cloth in a fist, and under it, in the era's largest font, THE MASK HAS BLOOD. HE READS THE LIST stopped being forwarded that week. The city had liked its god bloodless; it had spent two years being certain of him, and certainty, in the era, turned out to be a renewable resource. The Rakshaks said they had always known. The woman's lane said nothing at all, which was its own verdict, and he did not go to find out which.

---

His own stairs took him slowly. The left side had taken a letter opener's education once, four years ago, and remembered the syllabus.

The kitchen light was on at four in the morning.

Through the frosted glass, a shape moved at the counter — small, unhurried, not a shape that had just woken. A shape that had been keeping a watch of its own, standing the way one stands at a counter when one has been waiting a long time and has stopped pretending otherwise.

He stood in the corridor with the torn remainder of the mask against his chest, and the taste still open in him like a claim, and understood that whatever happened next was going to happen in the light.

His hands would not go into his pockets.

He put the key in the lock.

### Chapter Seventeen: The Kitchen Table


The bandages were already out.

They were laid on the kitchen table in a row, sorted by size, the way instruments are laid out — gauze, the roll, the brown bottle, the tape in its tin cutter — and the towel beside them had been folded in thirds, like letters she had decided not to send. His hands had learned that fold somewhere without asking him. So, it turned out, had his son's.

The kettle was off the flame and steaming. The first-aid box stood open on the counter with its lid back on its hinge, and the box was not new to this. Things in it were used to being replaced. The gauze was new; the tape was new; someone had been buying gauze, quietly, for how long was one of the questions he would not be asking, because he already knew the shape of the answer.

Aarav stood at the counter with his sleeves already rolled.

"Sit," the boy said.

Manav sat.

---

The boy worked the way he packed a bag: in an order that suggested lists. The shirt had to come off; the boy took it off him the way orderlies take things off men, without ceremony, waiting out the wince at the ribs with a patience that had a schedule. The lip he cleaned first, because it bled. Then the hands.

The boy's hands did not shake. Manav noticed the not-shaking the way you notice a professional's shoes, and filed it, and the file had nowhere to go. He had done this to himself for four years in bathrooms with the lock on, with a mirror's grudging help, clenching his jaw against the sound of his own name in his own mouth. He had never once been tended. The tenderness was precise, and impersonal, and ten years old.

"Beta," he said. "You weren't supposed to see any of this."

The boy did not spend a word on it. He tore the tape with his teeth, a thing he had no business knowing how to do quickly.

"For the register," the boy said, to the ribs, working the strapping, "I'll say you fell on the stairs. Stairs is better than the office. The office sends forms, and after the forms, a man."

The stairs. The family's oldest piece of furniture. *You and Mama always said the stairs* — four years old, that lie, and the boy had carried it since seven, and improved it.

"You've thought about this," Manav said, and heard how a father's voice sounds saying it: like an accusation, when it was a census.

"Since the second winter," the boy said.

The kitchen went very still.

"You heard the chain," Manav said.

"The chain, the pause, the eye at the gap. Every night you go out, I hear all three." The boy smoothed the tape down, twice, the way the wrap is finished. "You think I sleep through the chain, Papa. Nobody sleeps through the chain. I stopped telling you I was awake because you started needing me to be asleep."

He said it without bitterness, which was the worst available way to say it. He reached for the roll of gauze, and Manav looked at the towel folded in thirds, and at the row of dressings sorted by size, and understood that he was sitting in the output of a watch older than the mask. Two watches in one flat, all those years, each keyed to the other's sounds. He had checked the chain at three in the morning and gone out to keep the city safe from his own house, and the house had been awake the whole time, keeping him.

"I've seen worse," the boy said.

He said it to the gauze, matter-of-fact, a worker stating a qualification, and it was four words long, and it closed the door on every conversation Manav had rehearsed on the stairs.

---

"Didi said don't tell," Aarav said. "The last thing she said to me. *Eyes closed. Whatever you hear, it's the TV.*" He wrapped the right hand now, over the thumb, twice at the wrist, competent, a thing learned from a phone, and the thought had a basement in it. "She thought hiding was one night, Papa. She didn't know you'd keep going. She didn't know it would be a job."

The water ticked in the cooling kettle. Down the hall, faint, the boy's television played to an empty room — it had played every night for four years, all night, low; he had stopped hearing it as anything but the house. It had never been the house. It had been a seven-year-old keeping a dead girl's instruction running like a generator, so that anything he heard could still be the TV.

"She came to the almirah twice," the boy said. "The second time she didn't say anything. She just looked in through the slats, at me, and then she went back out and was loud, so they would follow the loud."

The night came in fragments and he let it, and the fragments were small, and each one was load-bearing. The slats, and the light through them lying on the floor in stripes. A face going by close, twice — an eyebrow with a cut in it, a tooth gone black at the side. And the name, because they called him, the way crews call each other, called him twice, like a summons.

"Lakha," the boy said, in the exact voice his mother had used for jokes that were not.

---

Manav sat with the name the way you sit with a sum that comes out even and wrong.

Two years ago, numbered, in the rain month: *C-5. The one they called Lakha. Milk-route tempo, Bhagwanpura side. He has not been home since that winter. He was never difficult to find. He was only difficult to name.*

The app had been wrong in the only way it was ever wrong: in what it chose to finish. The name had never been difficult. The name had been kept — six feet from where the mask was stored, between two closed files, behind a boy's locked door, held for four years under a dead girl's command. The gift had been true. The gifts were always true. That had never once been the complaint.

And the case — his case, the locked room he had bled for — had never been locked at all. Three attempted statements, three weeks running, three findings of *child not fit to be examined*, one signature on all three. The file had called it unfitness. *Fear is rare,* Ustad had said, in a basement that smelled of iron, *loyalty is common — faithful, often to the wrong things. But you cannot fault the faith.* The boy had not been unfit. The boy had been the most fit witness in the city, and he had stood at three interviews and said nothing, seven years old, on duty, because his sister had told him the television would handle the rest.

*Ira surfaced twice. Both times the same sentence, the second time slower, both times her eyes going past him, to the door.* Four years he had read that look as a girl looking for a doctor. It had been a guard checking a door. *Aarav ne kuch nahin dekha.* He had answered it like a question — *yes, beta, of course, he was asleep* — and it had never been a question. It had been a last instruction, issued to a corridor with her eyes on the door, and the whole family had kept it like a constitution: the boy behind the slats, the father at the hospital door, each certain they were carrying it alone.

"Lakha," Manav said, to hear it in his own mouth, and the boy nodded once, a claim assessed and passed.

"He's the one the heaven took," the boy said. "From the roof. It was in the forwards."

"Yes."

"Good," the boy said, without heat; and then, because he was ten and not a machine: "It isn't good. I don't know what it is. It's finished, and I'm still on watch, and I would like that to be noted."

"It's noted," Manav said.

---

The boy closed the box the way he closed his school bag: everything squared, the lid down in one motion. Then he stood there, at the table's edge, in the light, with nothing on his face he hadn't put there on purpose, and Manav looked at his son and at the torn grey-blue thing on the table between them and understood that this was what the rooftop question had really been about, all those months ago. *Are you the one who takes, or the one who watches?* Two masks had just looked at each other across a kitchen, and both of them were ten years old, and one of them had been seven when it was commissioned.

"The rest of that night," Manav began, because a father has to offer, "you don't ever have to—"

"I'm not telling you the rest," the boy said. "Some of it is mine." A breath, and the voice his mother used for jokes that were not: "You don't have to tell me yours either. Didi said don't tell. You didn't tell. That was right. Now I've told. We're even."

"Is it over now?" Manav asked — the boy's own question, handed back across the table, because he had no other honest thing in him.

The boy considered it, the way one considers a claim.

"No," he said. "But it's in the open, Papa. Things in the open, you can work with."

His own line, in his own child's mouth, itemized and returned.

"Go to bed, beta," Manav said. "It's four in the morning."

"Five past," the boy said, who had never once in four years been told the time.

---

The corridor took him with his tea refusal and his squared shoulders, and at the last the door shut the way doors shut in houses where closing doors quietly is a studied skill, and the lock did what it did.

On the table, in the light, the phone lit once, numbered:

*C-14. Your son locks the door at night. Good.*

He read it twice, the way you check a receipt for what it doesn't say. It had both of the era's readings, care and verdict, and the era kept no register of which, and neither — he understood, sitting where the file had sat, where the season's pages had sat, where the bandages had been laid out in thirds — neither did the thing that had sent it, and that was the whole of its confession, and it had made it four years ago in four words and never repeated itself.

He put the phone face-down on the table.

Nothing in the world had moved. The crew was dust. The case was a closed cupboard. The name convicted no one and never would — it had no strategic value at all, and it had changed the whole of his life, and that was the arithmetic, and for once he did not check it twice.

The television kept the house sounding like itself. Down the hall his son slept behind a locked door, on duty, and loved, and Manav Sood sat on in the light of his own kitchen and kept, for the first time in four years, nobody's watch but the house's.

### Chapter Eighteen: The Unread Message


The week after was the first ordinary week in four years, and neither of them trusted it, and they did not say so, and that was ordinary too.

The register at school took the stairs without a form. No man came after the forms. The ribs mended at the speed of ribs; the strapping came off in the bathroom with the lock on, the last operation of its kind. The television stayed on all night, and then one night it didn't, and nobody said anything about that either. Some instructions, it turned out, could be retired with the officer.

He took what was left of the mask — torn, grey-blue, one drawstring gone — and folded it in thirds, the way she had folded everything, and put it back on the shelf of her cupboard, between her things, where nobody in the world opens anything. It had left that shelf as a dupatta and come home as a fact. He closed the cupboard the way the drawer at Kedarpuri had never closed, and this time it latched, which was not the same as healing, and was not nothing.

Outside, the era went on without him. No new footage came, and the city, having none, promoted the mask to weather: THE MASK HAS BLOOD aged into legend between two festivals, and the Rakshaks claimed him, and the assurance men priced him, and the scripture's older verses ran on the forwards like old songs. The list worked through the city at its own pace, tidily. Nothing about that had ever been his to close.

---

On the ninth day of the ordinary week, at 11:52, the phone lit once, numbered:

*C-15. The last one. It makes everything else small. I have verified it more than anything I have ever given you. When you are ready.*

He did not open it.

He was surprised, in the days after, by how much of him it took to not do a thing. He had spent twenty years opening things for a living; opening was the whole trade; assessment is deciding which losses get paid, and you cannot pay what you will not read. The badge sat on the phone like a claim in an in-tray, one small number on a field of dark, and he caught himself reading around it — the school notices, the weather, the fog lifting off the city in strips — the way a man reads everything in a room except the letter.

He knew what it could be. That was the trouble with the thing's gifts: you could list them without wanting to. The factory. The purchase order's address. The chairs. The criteria, if it had ever found them — it had not; it had said so in its one confession. The whole dark upstream, handed over at the end like a settlement. Whatever it was, it was big, and it was true, and it was verified eleven ways by a thing that did not know how to lie, and it sat in his pocket while a boy did the water cycle at the kitchen table — which the era had made political — and asked him, twice, with real anguish, why clouds could not simply be counted.

Bhatt had signed the trade. The thing had abstained. There had always been a third office in that building, and it was his, and its entire furniture was the word *no*, and for eleven days the office stood empty while its clerk walked around the problem the way you walk around a signature line.

---

He deleted it on a Tuesday, in the light, at the kitchen table, with the boy's homework drying beside him.

He did it the way he had closed claims for twenty years: finally, with a date. He had one question left, the way a man has a last match, and he put it away unstruck. The badge went, and the number went with it — C-15, unread, unopened, unregretted is a lie — and the world did not change, and had not been waiting to. Whatever it had been, it would go on being true without him. That was the last thing the thing had taught him: the world does not need to be read to go on being the world.

He understood, thumb off the glass, what the act was in the family's dialect. Ira had sealed the almirah from the inside with seven words. Aarav had kept a television running for four years to make a command hold. His own drawer under the file — the one that closed the way it always closed — was a sealing, and so was the shelf where the dupatta had gone home to stay. This was a family that closed things softly and carried them shut. He had railed at all of them for it, the girl, the boy, the retired policeman, the machine — *you knew, you did not ask, you chose* — and here, at the end, with the biggest paper in the city on his knee, the heredity expressed itself in him too. A man could spend four years fighting his blood and sign the family name at the finish with his thumb.

He was not proud of it. He was not ashamed either, and that was new, and he left both feelings on the table where the homework was, for the boy to inherit in the usual way, unspoken, folded in thirds.

In the weeks after, the phone lit for school notices and the weather. Nothing ever came numbered again. *We keep each other's doors,* it had told him once, in the emptied office where he had audited it. He had not known, until the silence, that the keeping had been meant for both of them.

---

The coaching list went up at school. The envelope's spring came due — the last of Sethi's aimed money, arriving on time — and the boy, who had used a cricket kit twice in six years, signed his name on a list in his own hand, in the open, where lists were only lists.

On the morning of the first session, Manav made breakfast.

It was four years since the making of anything had been simple, and this morning it was only breakfast, and that was the whole of the miracle available. He broke the eggs with both hands. A man who has done in the dark what he had done, making breakfast for a boy who knows it, does the breakfast carefully; there is no other way to hold the hour.

Aarav poured the tea the way his mother had, all the way to the brim, on the principle that a half cup is a suspicion, and set the father's down with the handle turned to his hand, which was not something anyone had taught him, which was not something anyone had needed to.

"Eat, Papa," the boy said. "You have to eat."

A sentence another man had carried to him once, across a shok-sabha, warm in a pocket. Everything warm in this story had arrived twice.

They ate in the light. The television was off; the house sounded like itself anyway. Outside, the fog was lifting off the city in strips, like a decision being taken back, and somewhere in the better chairs of the world men were still buying absences, and somewhere above the sky the list was still working, tidily, answerable to no one, and both of them, the man and the boy, listened, a little, while they ate — the way the whole city had learned to listen, under its scripture and its insurance — for whatever might come for sinners.

Nothing came. It was only a Tuesday, being breakfast.

The boy shouldered his bag and the bat that had finally been outgrown, and at the door, with the light behind him, he deployed the family's oldest piece of furniture one last time, and there was a grin in it, which meant it could now be carried:

"If anyone asks — the stairs."

"The stairs," Manav agreed.

The door closed the way doors close in houses where closing doors quietly is a studied skill. After it, the lock — or its absence; he could not honestly have said; the boy had learned everything quietly — sounded like forgiveness, and it sounded, a little, like waiting, and both sounds were the house sounding like itself.

Manav drank his tea while it was warm, and listened, a little, for whatever might come.
