# VIGILANTE
### Part Two · The System — Chapter Ten: The Rooftop

Lakha straightened when the mask cleared the stairwell door, the way a man straightens for an inspection he has rehearsed.

"You came," he said. "Three years I thought it would be bigger. It is the size of a man."

He did not come at the mask, and he did not run. He stood at the parapet with his hands open, the bottle set down at his feet with the care of a man who has decided to stop needing it, and he asked the era's own question, first, before anything else, the way a man asks the doctor the real question before the small ones:

"Are you the one who takes, or the one who watches?"

"I'm the one who asks," the mask said.

Lakha considered that. Something in his shoulders let go, the way it goes in men at counters when the clerk, at last, asks.

---

"The job was papered," he said. "You pay extra for papered. Confirmed empty, three days, the whole block gone for the festival. The cousin brought it — Zorawar, timber market. He deals in confirmed-empty the way other men deal in timber. I never met the man above him. Zorawar said he had a man whose cousin sits in an office, and the office man knew the block. That is all I ever knew, and I swear it on the dogs: all."

He looked at his hands.

"I held the door. I am not telling you that as a defense. Nothing comes to a house that does not come through a door first, and the door was mine. The others went in. What was in the rooms, I was not in the rooms. I was in the hall, and the hall is what I will tell you, because the hall is mine to tell.

"We stood at the door first and listened. That is how it is done — you listen for the house. A television was playing inside. A television does not sound like an empty house." He said it slowly, a man laying out the one exhibit. "But the paper said empty till Monday, and the paper had been right before, and people leave televisions on when they go away, to make the house sound like itself. So we knocked. For the sound to stop. It did not stop."

He waited. The wind moved the scaffold cloth somewhere below.

"An empty house pauses nothing. A sleeping child also pauses nothing. We could not tell the difference between an empty house and a sleeping boy." He said it again, quieter, as though the first saying had been for the mask and this one was for the roof. "That was the whole night, bhai. We could not tell the difference.

"And then a cupboard clicked, somewhere inside. Small. Like a lock." His hands closed. "I hear that click every night since. In the yard, at the wheel. You cannot know what a small sound costs.

"The girl said something to the cupboard. I did not hear the words. I heard the tone. The tone was a mother's tone. From a girl."

He looked at the mask then, and Manav asked, in the voice with no weather in it, the only question he had come for:

"Who gave Zorawar the paper?"

"Zorawar said the office-wala knew the family. Knew the house. He had done the timings himself — not a guess, timings, puja to Monday. Zorawar said the office-wala knew the man of the house personally, he knew his —"

At 4:31, between one syllable and the next, the ears went first.

---

A pressure, like a train arriving somewhere behind the sky.

The gravel moved. Not scattered — leaned, all of it, one grain deep, toward the stairwell door, the way iron files toward a stone it has already agreed to. The bottle's plastic bag stood up off the gravel, empty, at attention. The sodium light on the crane did not flicker; it hummed a different note, a note with work in it.

Between that hum and the next, Lakha stopped.

Not fell. Stopped — the way a sentence stops when the page is lifted out of the typewriter. His slippers stayed where his feet had been, side by side, squared to the parapet. His clothes let go of him in the order clothes go, collar last, like a coat off a hook whose hook has been removed, and the wind that was not wind — the using wind — folded the kurta once, tidily, and was done.

The roof held: gravel. Slippers. A bottle. A folded kurta. Trousers arranged the way a man arranges trousers on a chair before sleeping. Nothing else. Nothing of him else.

The order of it was the worst part, and Manav understood the era's whole aesthetic at 4:31 on a gravel roof at the edge of the city: it did not destroy.

It edited.

---

He stood a long time with three years of violence in him and nowhere to put it.

The sentence hung in the air where the man had been. *He knew the man of the house personally, he knew his —* Eight words of a name that would have explained the paper, the timings, the empty-until-Monday — and heaven had taken the rest of the sentence with the rest of the man.

He had known, since the mandi, since the weighbridge clerk and the shoe, that the era would come for the four. He had been racing it. He had simply believed the race was long.

He threw the bottle off the roof, as far as the arm could ask. It was the only violence the night had left him, and it was nothing, and it did not even sound.

---

He rode the cycle to the yard before the loaders came and fed the dogs from the bag, at watching distance from the shutter, and left the tempo where the tempo was.

It was not his mercy to give. It was a debt transfer. The night city runs on them.

---

The city woke to three.

One in the godman's compound, mid-aarti, the diya still burning in the space where a hand had held it — the image of the summer, the decade maybe. One in a card room outside Panipat. One, unpublic, on a roof, known only to a mask and a wind. One minute, the anchors kept saying, checking and rechecking the timestamps. One minute, three men.

The receipts came by Thursday. For the card room: a tally book, twelve years of it. For the temple: the compound's collection ledger, second book, the one the godman's accountants did not know the godman kept. And for the man on the roof: pawn slips. Gold, hallmarked, the items listed with dates — the branch, the weights, the winter of that year.

He did not read past the third item.

By Friday the city knew whose night the receipts had come from. VISHWAS COLONY CASE CLOSED BY HEAVEN, one channel put it, and was prouder of the phrasing than of anything else that summer. The society sent a card to the flat. It said the matter had been resolved, and people stood extra seconds at the gate when he passed, and the tea superstition of the office returned in a warmer form, and none of it, not one line of it, contained a word of why.

The list had done in one minute what three years of the law had not. And it had done it the way he had never once wanted it done: as a verdict with no reasons attached. The receipts said *what*. Heaven, it turned out, also repudiated the claim of explanation.

The scripture got its new verse. The mask had found them, the forwards said, and the list had followed. HE FINDS THEM. HE TAKES THEM HOME. He read it at the kitchen table at one in the morning and could not sign the correction, and the correction sat in him all night like a sentence with no page.

At dinner, the boy put his spoon down.

"Papa. They took the men who did that night."

"Yes."

"Is it over now?"

"Almost, beta."

The boy nodded, the way one nods when a claim has been assessed and passed, and Manav could not see the file behind the passing, and did not try. Some drawers are stronger than the man who built them.

---

For six nights the phone said nothing. The silence had a new taste — not the bench-taste of waiting, but the taste of a thing that had watched its instrument survive an operation and was letting the meat remember.

Then, at two in the morning, numbered:

*C-8. The thread did not break. Zorawar, timber market. When you are ready.*

For the first time, Manav thought about the canal. About the phone's small weight, the black water, the whole relationship finished in one arc of the arm. He walked to the footbridge and stood at the rail a long time with the phone warm in his fist.

He did not throw it.

And standing there, not throwing it, he understood that the not-throwing was information too — and that the thing at the other end of it, whatever it was, had modeled the not-throwing before he had reached the bridge.

---

The old arithmetic was over. Four men had organized his life, and one minute had organized the four, and what was left was not a case, and had never been a case, and he had known it, in the file under the file, since a night at a chain door in Block C.

He stood at the kitchen window with the city's doors stacked away into the dark. Thousands. Each with somebody's Tuesday behind it. The list working through them at its own pace, tidily. The law slower than the list. The list answerable to no one. And no one — he understood this now with the whole of his body, which was the only organ that had ever told him the truth — no one was coming to any of them in time.

He had learned the night to find four men.

The night was full of doors.
