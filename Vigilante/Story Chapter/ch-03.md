# VIGILANTE
### Part One · The House — Chapter Three: First Night

The mask left the flat the way it had entered the office bag: quietly, between things.

At one in the morning the hall television was still murmuring at its lowest number, and the boy slept inside the noise of it, and Manav stood in the doorway with his boots on and the bag over his shoulder, doing the arithmetic he now allowed himself. School van, 7:20. Office, 9:40. The gym closed on Sundays. A man could be back by four.

He had told himself he would only look at the night. Not go into it. Look.

Two lanes from home, in the dark between parked scooters, kneeling as if to a shoelace, he put it on for the street. The city arrived slightly too wide, in stereo, through eye-holes a fish would have wanted adjusted. His breath came back at him warm off the cloth. Cold went in at the jaw. For the first hundred meters the mask was all he could think about; by the second hundred the city had swallowed it, and him, and that was the night's first lesson: nobody was looking. The night had room.

---

Under the canal flyover, the vegetable market stood down, and in its vacancy a second market had set up. Farid's thela under a blue tarpaulin. A kettle. A transistor radio playing songs at the volume of memory. Truck men on short leave from their routes. Two watchmen sharing a bench and a silence. A boy with newspaper bundles who ate three biscuits and paid for one cup with the seriousness of a man closing a merger.

The city's other shift, the one the day never counted, doing its rounds. Manav sat at the end of the bench with his back to the tarpaulin wall and was furniture in it.

Across the lane stood the paan counter that was not only a paan counter: a lit doorway, a staircase going up, men entering with the walk of people who have already decided and leaving with other walks. Three weeks of colleagues' burglary files had got him the bench. The Diwali-week signature — blocks emptied for the festival, flats hit in the first days of the break — went back four years, and the office had a name for it the way offices have names, which is a code in a column, meaning nothing to anyone in a position to act on it. He had taken the code and read the claims the way he had once read the field diaries: as a map. Sixteen houses in four years, one colony after another around the ring. And in a November claim two streets from Vishwas Colony, a neighbor's statement nobody had followed up — *a blue scooter with a taped mirror, seen twice in the week before.*

He had found a blue scooter with a taped mirror outside this paan counter on a Tuesday in January, and had sat on Farid's bench every second night since, being a customer. Learning, in the language of the muscles of his neck, to hold stillness ninety seconds at a time. Nobody had told him. His neck had told him.

Tonight, at half past one, the scooter had a rider.

---

He followed on foot and did everything wrong, gracefully, like a man performing a dance he had only read about. He crossed when the rider crossed. He stood when the rider stood. He checked the phone-light twice for a street that did not need checking. The rider walked the scooter its first hundred meters, as they do in lanes, and Manav held his distance behind a shuttered egg-wallah's, and the distance was honest, and then the rider leaned the scooter to the wall where the flyover's dark began, and went into the dark on foot with a wrapped bundle under his arm, and Manav followed him into it, and this — he would file it later, in the section for findings — this was the moment the claim went bad.

The underpass at that hour is a place light has agreed to give up. He heard the man's footsteps stop, and then a voice, unhurried, saying a name into the dark — "Chotu. Chotu aa gaya" — and the dark produced company.

There were three of them, and one of him, and the first minute went almost to plan. He got his arms up the way the basement had taught him; he turned the shoulder the way the basement had taught him; he even heard Ustad's breath-count in his ear, paper forgetting while meat remembered. The problem was the second minute. Nobody trains the second minute. The mitt under the ribs he knew from the basement — but the mitt stops, and the street does not, and the shot went into his left side like a letter opener and took his legs out from under him, and after that the night was mostly geography. A boot. The taste of coins. The flyover's ceiling, far away, ridged like a sheet.

One of them took hold of the mask.

He held it the way the drowning hold wood. He could not have said why, with three men on him and the arithmetic of the situation advising surrender of everything — wallet, phone, the shoes — the one fact he would not let the night have was the cloth on his face. He curled around it and around his ribs, and took what there was to take on his back and his arms, and somewhere in it he understood, with the strange calm of a man reading his own claim file as it is being written: *they are beating a mask.* Not a man. There is no name anywhere on a mask. No address, no society, no son. Whatever else the night did to him, it could not do the one thing that mattered.

Then, from the mouth of the underpass, at parade volume, a kettle announced itself.

"Chai! Garam chai! Oye, thand hai, chai piyo—!"

Farid did not cross the road. He did not run at anyone. He stood where his cart stood, under his own lamp, and made the dark a shopfront: calling, pouring, clinking cup on cup, being *seen* at industrial strength. Three men whose work requires the opposite of attention did their arithmetic — the street's arithmetic, older than the city — and were gone into it, taking nothing, because a beating interrupted is a debt uncollected, and debts like that are never forgotten, only deferred.

The underpass got its echo back. Farid's radio played on, at the volume of memory.

---

Farid cleaned him at the back of the thela with the water he had been saving for tea, which Manav understood was a thing, and did not say. He worked the way he poured: without comment, without hurry, warm cloth, one pass, done. The lamp hissed. The ribs on the left had closed like a fist and would not be asked to help with anything for a while.

"You are not police," Farid said. It was not a question, and it was not the beginning of a conversation; it was a line item.

"No," Manav said.

Farid nodded, as if a small accounting question had settled itself, and poured.

At the end, Manav took out his wallet. Farid looked at it the way you look at a document in the wrong language.

"First chai free," he said. "After that, you pay by sitting where I can see you."

Then he wiped the counter where the blood had got to it, in the manner of a man who had wiped worse, or would, and turned up the radio half a number, and the night resumed its rounds.

Manav walked home inside the city's first shift. Milk cans on a cycle. Newspaper bundles at the society gate, warm as bread. A scooter carrying two lives somewhere early. The audit came on him around the second kilometer, the way audits do: politely, at an inconvenient hour.

Findings, if a colleague had signed them: *subject proceeded on a scooter, a taped mirror, and a feeling. No corroboration. No second source. The accused is a runner for a betting room, not, on any evidence shown, a housebreaker; and the scooter may be any scooter. Claim repudiated. Cause of loss: own negligence.*

Any assessor in Tejgarh would have thrown it out. He would have thrown it out. He had gone out with a thread and called it a case, and the thread had snapped back and opened his ribs.

And underneath the findings, in the space where assessors write the part they know and do not say: the mask had worked. Beaten, emptied, seventy rupees lighter in chai — no one had asked his name. There was no claim number on him anywhere. The fact of it sat under his ribs on the side opposite the pain, and kept him walking the last kilometer home.

---

The school van came at 7:20 with its two notes. He had the compress off and the story on by then.

"Stairs," he said, in answer to the boy's looking. "The society stairs. Carrying the bucket."

Aarav looked at the cheek the way he looked at everything now. Entirely.

"You and Mama always said the stairs," he said, and went in to the television for his cereal.

Manav stood in the kitchen a while after the van had gone. He had been unable to finish that sentence, three months ago, at dinner. The boy had finished it for him this morning, at the top of the stairs, with the bucket in it, and had paid something small to do it — you could hear the toll in the flat voice — and there was no box in any of Manav's files for what that meant. He called the office and told Sethi about something he had eaten. Sethi told him to take the week, said *I'll handle the office* the way he had handled the office for two years, and Manav lay on his good side and let the boy's television come through the wall, and slept four hours without the arithmetic coming even once, which the body had arranged without him.

---

It was in the canteen, that week, on page seven, between a wedding and a municipal notice: **FINANCIER MISSING FROM KISHANGANJ.** Police were looking, the brief said, at his debts. A shop shuttered since Tuesday night. Neighbors believed he had gone to his village. No one had complained.

Six lines. He read all six, because he read everything now, and filed them under nothing, and turned the page, and the page, as pages do, went on.

---

Friday, when the ribs had gone from a fist to a held breath, he was back on the bench at half past one, sitting where he could be seen. Farid poured without being asked and set the cup down and said nothing, and the paan counter went on with its lit doorway and its two kinds of walk, and the night went on being roomy.

He had wanted the night to give him a man. It had given him a bench instead.

He sat, and drank slowly, and began at the age of forty-four to learn the trade he had actually come for: waiting.
