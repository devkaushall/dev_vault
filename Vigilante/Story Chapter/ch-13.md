# VIGILANTE
### Part Three · The Watchers — Chapter Thirteen: Eleven Months

The school was going to the hills in November, four days, a bus with a teacher per nine children. Aarav packed the bag himself in one evening, in an order that suggested lists: torch, two books, one sweater rolled the way the camp sheet said. At the last, without explanation, he took the small brass bell from the puja shelf and put it in the side pocket with the torch.

Manav did not ask about the bell. The house had stopped asking about things; it was how the house prayed.

The next morning he called Inspector Bhatt, retired, Kedarpuri, and said he would be passing through on a survey — a small lie of transit, professionally laid — and Bhatt was quiet for exactly as long as an old policeman is quiet when a lie is being laid on him, and then said, "Come, Sood saab. I am always home."

Four years of kettle in that sentence.

Kedarpuri in November was cold the way river towns are cold, from the feet up. Bhatt's house stood two lanes off the ghat, the colour of tea, marigolds in drums along the wall — he grew them himself, it turned out; guilt keeps a garden like anything else keeps habits. His wife opened the door, looked at Manav once, long, the look wives keep for their husbands' unfinished business, filed four years ago under *one day someone will come* — set out two cups, and went to the neighbour's, which is what wives of thirty-eight years do when they recognize a conversation by its shoes.

For an hour they talked pension, knees, the price of diesel, the town's new guest house. Manav let it run the way he had let a thousand counters run: the man would arrive at it himself, or the hour would do it for him. The hour did it. The light went long on the verandah, and Bhatt refilled the cups, and sat, and folded his hands, and waited like a man before a bench he had appointed himself to.

Manav took the pad of yellow squares out of his bag and laid them on the table in order, face up, without commentary. It was the only courtroom he had ever trusted.

Bhatt put on his glasses and read them the way he had written the file four years ago: carefully, at reading pace, an officer checking work he had forgotten he had kept honest in all the wrong ways. He read all five. Then he took the glasses off, and laid them on the squares, and said:

"Ask. I have been waiting since the biryani."

---

The first square was the knock.

"You found it in four years and two hundred readings," Bhatt said. "I found it on day two. Papered job — I had seen the signature eleven times in three years, from the inside. That is what you were paying me for, Sood saab: to know instantly whose work a thing was. It worked perfectly. I knew instantly." He looked at the marigolds. "Four of my own registered informants. I had bought them tea at that very corner in August. In October they were in a flat in Vishwas Colony, and the file came to my desk, and the knock told me in one line, and there was never, from that moment, any version of my life in which I did not know."

The second square was the plate.

"I never told the guard to forget. Nobody tells; that is not how it is done. You schedule a re-examination. Then another. The third time a man says *cannot say*, the file begins saying it for him. Asking is a way of erasing." He turned his cup a quarter. "You know this, actually. You ask for a living. Ask any claimant three times—" and stopped, because he had heard himself, and the two of them sat in the shape of the mirror he had walked into, until he inclined his head, an old man accepting a point against himself.

The third square was the input.

"Unsigned, yes. Inputs are unsigned; that is what they are for. *No established syndicate in the sector.* Nine words. I killed your case with nine words and went home and ate." He said it without drama, the way men say the worst true things, at a table, to the person most owed them. "Eleven men, Sood saab. Three years up a ladder that was eating villages — actual villages, stripped twice, I can name them. You never met those families. I did. And those families do not get a telephone call at eleven in the morning, every year, on the day, asking how they are. Nobody has ever asked them how they are. I chose the eleven, and I will tell you the truest sentence I own: I would choose them again. And it is a worthless sentence, because it is a sentence about a world where nobody's wife died on my arithmetic. In the world where she did, I made the same choice at eight in the morning and could not sleep by eight that night, and I have not slept at eight at night for four years."

The fourth square said: *three unfitnesses, evenly spaced. The kindness had a schedule.*

Here Bhatt was quiet the longest.

"A child who hides like that is fit," he said finally. "Any doctor in the state would have certified him inside a week — children like that are fit for exactly one thing, and it is the thing you and I needed him for. I filed him unfit three times, because every week of unfitness was a week his face did not enter the file. And the third time — the third time he sat in my office and watched the ceiling fan the way he watches everything, and watched me the way one watches a fan —" Bhatt's hands were folded very tightly now, a man holding his own hands the way you hold a stranger's. "I was relieved. Twenty-six years of service, and God sent a seven-year-old to do my sin for me, and I signed the receipt. You can hate me for the trade, Sood saab; everyone trades. If you must hate one thing, hate that I was grateful to a child."

The fifth square said: *eleven months.*

"Eleven months is what a commitment takes in this state. I stayed for the commitment. A man should eat what he kills."

---

"Say the rest," Manav said. "The part you have been saving four years."

"You want a sentence for it. There is no sentence. There is only the arithmetic, and you do it too, every day of your quiet life. Every day you don't burn the city down, Sood saab, you're signing it too. Every man with a stamp knows it in his bones. You have simply never had a stamp." A pause, and the old man looked at him with the whole of twenty-six years in his eyes, and added the one word Manav would carry home on the train like a stone:

"Yet."

They sat with that. The light went off the wall.

"And your list," Bhatt said, into the quiet, "will not come for me. You want to know if I am afraid of it. I want it to, and it will not, and that is the only fact of the new weather that has made me want to walk into that river. Look who it took from your own case, and look who it left. The four were documented only in my secret ledger — so heaven took them. My eleven are documented in a charge-sheet — so heaven will feed them dal for fourteen years and release them to their grandchildren. Documented men are safe. The list is a great believer in settlements, Sood saab. It punishes the ones the papers can't reach. It is a policeman's god: it reads what's in the file."

"Then who does it read?" Manav asked — the question of the era, put to the one man who had spent his life writing files.

"Nobody," Bhatt said. "That is my professional assessment, and I have made assessments my whole career, and this is the only one that ever frightened me."

He got up to refill the water, and came back, and stood a moment at the rail of the verandah with his cardigan buttoned one short of the top.

"You came for the why. I will give you the why's shape, since I cannot give you the why. The papered lists were a commodity. Somebody upstream manufactured absences — whole seasons of empty houses, gathered, itemized, sold. Three years I climbed toward it and got as far as the purchase order. Never the factory. The timber shop was my last rung. Above Zorawar, the paper changes hands in places with better chairs."

"Offices."

"Offices," Bhatt said. "I starved your file, Sood saab, and that will be on my stone. But I did not write the season that killed her. That was written at a desk."

---

The day's weight ambushed him the way sleep ambushes soldiers; the confession had been four years in arriving and it arrived all at once. He sat back on the sofa and was gone for twenty minutes, and woke under a chatai that had been laid over him by hands he had not heard.

Bhatt was in the garden, dead-heading marigolds in the last light: the man who had once stood over a clerk at a shok-sabha, saying *eat, beta, you have to eat*, standing over a sleeping father, letting him.

At six they walked down to the ghat. The bells were beginning, and the river was the grey of old coins, and the pilgrims were doing what pilgrims do, which is lend the hour a shape.

"Do you pray?" Manav asked him.

"No," Bhatt said. "I attend."

At the lane where the paths divided — the station path, the house path — the old man buttoned his cardigan the last button, and made his goodbye the way policemen make goodbye, which is a briefing.

"Sood saab. One thing, off every file. Your boy is ten now. Ten is the age they start keeping their own ledgers. Whatever your nights are — and a man who sleeps the way you slept on my sofa does not have ordinary nights — keep him out of every notebook in that city." He held Manav's hand one second past the handshake. "Including the ones you keep on yourself. Children in paperwork are how cases die. I am the world's leading authority."

---

The train went down the valley with the river in its ear for three hours.

Somewhere past the second tunnel the phone lit, numbered:

*C-9. Eat something. You are carrying a man's confession on an empty stomach.*

He had stopped asking how it knew where he was. That, too, had not been a decision.

He opened the file under the file — the one he kept himself in — and put Bhatt's Tuesday in it, next to his own Sunday, and the drawer closed the way it always closed, which is to say, it didn't.
