# VIGILANTE
### Part Two · The System — Chapter Seven: CORRECTIONS

By May the whole city had started wearing his mask.

Not cloth — nobody else had cloth. Manners. The sudden, loud, public goodness of a town that had learned it was being graded: prayers said where prayers could be seen, tithes announced at volume, old debts settled by men who wanted the settling witnessed. The rickshaw pullers had a joke with no laugh in it — *bhagwan bhi line mein hai*, God's also in the queue — and the forwards had found a word for what the mobs did: they called it a correction.

Meanwhile, half the old night trade had gone quiet. Men who worked in the dark had discovered the dark had management now. The betting room's Tuesdays had thinned; the runners ran less; the drop-house behind the marble market kept its lights longer, counting nothing. The world had not gotten kinder. Violence had simply changed costume — from balaclavas to processions — and the money was learning to follow.

Manav's nights had split in two. The old hunt — the network, the fence, the four — ran on its rails, slower now, racing a clock whose face he could not read. And the new weather ran beside it: he had begun, without deciding to, a second ledger. The mobs were not spontaneous. His count said so. Three corrections in three weeks, and the same grey shirt in the crowd each time, on the same scooter, arriving early and leaving paid — mobs were becoming a service someone purchased, and he followed the service provider the way he had once followed a blue scooter with a taped mirror, because a pattern is a door even when you do not yet know the room.

The fourth mob was at a school.

---

Government senior secondary, Sector 19, 7:40 of a May evening, result season. Two hundred men by his count, growing at the rate rumours grow, and at the gate, sitting on the ground with his back against the bars and chalk still on his fingers, a chemistry teacher called Upadhyay-ji, who had taught three generations of the district their periodic tables and was now, by the light of a hundred phone torches, guilty.

The receipt was a photograph, forwarded and forwarded: a handwritten page, purportedly in his hand, pricing the chemistry board paper at eight hundred rupees a student. It had arrived from nowhere in particular, which is where the era's most trusted documents came from. Nobody had seen an original. Nobody needed one. The list had taught the city that the paper always comes *after* — and the city had improved on the lesson: why wait for heaven's audit when a correction could be held tonight, by hand, at the gate of the guilty?

Upadhyay-ji was saying his own name to whoever would hold it. The mob's front rank — young men with lathis and puja marks, a cohort of the publicly good — had begun the small tidal movements crowds make before they close.

Manav stood in the paan-stall dark across the road, unmasked, and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was all wrong.

Go in as a man, and he was one body against two hundred, and a man with a face besides. Go in as the mask, and — and this was the trap his own winter had built, one lie at a chain door at a time — the mask would arrive like scripture. *HE IS OUT THERE. HE SEES.* The mob would freeze; the mob would part; the mob would look from the mask to the teacher and draw the only conclusion the era allowed: the mask had come for Upadhyay-ji. They would not fight him. They would *help*. They would push the teacher forward, offer him up, stand back to witness the judgment — and if the mask refused to judge, refused aloud, said *this man is innocent* in a stranger's cloth —

He could see it. The whole machine of it, clear as a claim diagram. His myth had no vocabulary for *not guilty*. The mask could not save the man. The mask could only convict him.

His hand was already going to his pocket — the mask was carried now, not filed; carried is closer to worn — when the phone lit up in the same hand.

An app he had never installed.

White screen. Small grey type. No logo, no greeting, no name but the one under the icon, which was the era's own word for a beating:

**CORRECTIONS**

*C-1. The mask will convict him. They will offer him to you. Grey shirt, scooter, by the paan counter — paid to lead this. Ask him, in front of them, what he was paid. He runs. Ninety seconds.*

He read it twice. It took three seconds. Across the road the crowd's tide had started its closing run, and he had eighty-seven seconds and no theory of where the note had come from, and twenty-one years of counters had taught him the two kinds of angry man: the one who wants the thing, and the one who wants to be watched. The second kind leads mobs. A mob is a sentence looking for its verb.

He crossed the road as a man — office walk, office shirt, a face like a Wednesday — and walked into the crowd's middle as though called to the counter, and a crowd will always make room for a man who behaves as if he has business, because no one wants to discover the business.

"Bhai!" One voice, an office voice, a *next please* voice. "The grey shirt! On the scooter!" — and every head turned, because the sentence had found its verb — "Ask him what he was paid! He's been paid to be here! Ask him! Ask him in front of everyone!"

A mob is a sentence looking for its verb, and this one had just been handed a better one than *beat the teacher*. Two hundred men turned as one organism toward the paan counter, where a man in a grey shirt was already, before the sentence finished, reaching for his scooter's handle.

He ran. That was the proof. No innocent man on a scooter leaves a riot he has organized, at a jog, sideways, before the question is even finished being asked — but a paid man does, because a paid man's job has a clause in it about exactly this. The mob watched him go, and the mob understood what it had just been told about itself, and mobs cannot bear to know what they cost.

The circle broke into arithmetic. The producer was gone; the theater found the exits. Two colleagues of Upadhyay-ji's came over the gate like men remembering their profession and took the teacher under the arms, chalk and all, back into the school's dark, where a headmaster was already on the phone to somebody's uncle, who was a magistrate.

The watch on Manav's wrist said ninety seconds. This time the watch was right.

---

The phone lit again in his pocket, on the walk home, at the corner where the streetlights gave up.

*C-2. The receipt was drawn by hand, cut from a register. The register is under the quilts in the boot of a white Santro, TG-3-, parked behind the school. It belongs to a parent whose son has failed twice. The police will find it at 6:40 tomorrow if someone tells them where. Anyone can call.*

Anyone can call. He stood under the dead streetlight and understood that the sentence was the sender's whole character in three words: it did not do things. It corrected, and it left the verb to him.

He found a PCO in the old quarter — one of the last, a cabinet of black phones run by a man too stubborn to die politely — and called the police control room from a public phone, on a public street, a citizen's voice with no name it needed, and gave the Santro, the quilts, the register, the school. Then he went home and did not sleep, and at 6:40 in the morning his phone lit with the news, as clocks do.

The register had been found. The forger had confessed by 9. Upadhyay-ji was cleared. A small item, page five, no photograph. The teacher, the papers said, would return to his classroom on Monday, and had asked reporters, politely, to let his students be. He had begun, someone wrote, marking in pencil.

---

At the kitchen table, that night, Manav audited the app the way he had audited everything since October.

It had no install date. It held no permissions — none for mic, none for camera, none for data. It used no data. It had no size. In the ledger of the phone it weighed nothing and arrived never; it was the first thing he had ever held that declined, entirely, to exist on paper. The era's mobs called beatings corrections, and here was a stranger in the era's own language, offering corrections for free, timed to the second, right about the minute, right about the register, right about the grey shirt — and asking for nothing, which was the most expensive thing anything had ever asked him for.

He had spent his life verifying claimants. There is no such thing as a free surveyor; every man at every counter is paid by somebody to be there. The finding was obvious, and he wrote it on a fresh page of the ledger that lived where the mask lived:

*SOURCES —*
*1. CORRECTIONS. Unverified claimant. No face, no office, no address. Two corrections, both paid out to the minute. Motive unstated. Treat as adverse party.*

He underlined *adverse party* twice, and felt the night tilt a little, the way a file tilts when a party with no name enters the case.

The phone lit, once more, on the table, face-up between him and the ledger.

*C-3. Your wrist. Ice it.*

The wrist — the left one, the one that had caught a man in Sector 14 and held an old man and had been quietly swelling since Thursday, all of it unmentioned, all of it unwitnessed, known to no one in the world, including, until that moment, himself.

He sat for a long time with the phone face-up, being cared for by something with no face.

He did not fetch the ice. Obeying, he understood, would be the first payment — and he put the phone face-down beside the ledger, and sat in the kitchen with his bad wrist and his two ledgers and the beginning of a very long case, and did not do any arithmetic at all, because the arithmetic, for the first time in three years, was being done for him.
