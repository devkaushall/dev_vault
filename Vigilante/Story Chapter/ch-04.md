# VIGILANTE
### Part One · The House — Chapter Four: First Success

Between Block B and Block C ran a gully three feet wide, built for air and used, like everything built for air in that city, for sound.

He had been hearing it since November, the way everyone had been hearing it since November, which is to say officially not at all. A burst. A lull. In the lulls, a television. Domestic violence, it turns out, has a rhythm, the way rain has a rhythm, and a building learns it the way hills learn a river: nothing to be done, the shape is simply the country's. On the nights he was home to hear it, Manav lay on his good side and listened with the whole city block and did what the whole city block did, which was to certify the sound as weather.

The man was Deepak-ji, second floor, the flat whose window came second from the gully end. Accounts man on the block committee — the society trusted him with its money, its receipts, its Diwali fund. A certified man. He did not shout; that was the terrible thing. What came through the gully was administrative. Somewhere in those rooms was a system, patient, itemized, and in the lulls the television played for the neighbors.

He did not know her name. He knew Deepak-ji's name; everyone did. That is how that works.

What finally moved him, on a Tuesday in February, was not her sound. The rhythm changed — burst, lull, burst, and then in a lull a third sound, small, a child's voice cut short like a thread — and he was off the bed with his heart going before his mind had voted. It was not her sound that moved him, and he knew what that said about him, and where he filed it, and he got the bag.

---

The society's one camera watched the main gate with the fixed devotion of the poorly mounted. He had studied its aim the way he studied everything now, without deciding to, and he went out the long way, by the water tank, where the camera's faith did not extend. Block C's stairwell door had not latched since the intercom died — the building's whole security system, one broken latch, faithful as everything else.

Second floor. Second door from the gully end. Behind the inner door of the flat, a study lamp burned. Exams, the lamp said. February. Boys study through everything; it is the one thing adults never believe.

He knocked.

The door opened two inches, to the chain. A vest, a calm face, a dish towel over one shoulder. Clean eyes. Sober eyes. The worst news yet.

"Kya chahiye?"

"We can hear it," the mask said. "From C. All of us."

It was the first sentence the mask ever spoke in the world, and every word of it was false except the hearing. There was no all. There was no us. There was one man in a dead woman's cloth on a landing at two in the morning, borrowing a plural because the singular was unbearable.

Deepak-ji looked at the mask the way a treasurer looks at an unaudited entry. "There is no sound," he said, and men of that size of confidence do not call for help; help is for other people. He shut the chain and slid it and opened the door to take the intruder's collar, and the night moved into the flat.

It went to plan for exactly the first minute. The body did what the basement had put in it: weight forward, inside the arms, the shoulders doing their arithmetic. Deepak-ji was strong the way committee men are strong — from carrying certified things — and he got one hand on the counter and came back with the kitchen knife, and here the night paused for its examination question: the knife moved, and Manav went in under it rather than back, and took a line of fire along the left forearm, and the blade caught the mask's jaw, cloth only, a seam's width — and then the knife was on the floor and the man was folded over the sink and the room was over except for the part that wasn't.

Nobody trains the second minute. The second minute is where you find out what you came for.

He found out. He hit the folded man twice more than the fight asked for, and would have gone to three, and it was not rage exactly — rage is hot and this had no temperature at all — and then the woman screamed at him to stop, one word, twice, and the word was not for Deepak-ji. It was for him. She was between them with her arm out, guarding the man who beat her, positioned like the last wall of a fort that had chosen its side, and the room went still with the lamp burning behind the inner door and the boy standing in it.

Seven or eight. Eyes entirely open. The boy did not scream either. The boy watched him the way children watch when they are filing the world for later, and then stepped back, and closed the inner door — softly, one hinge-squeak, the way doors are closed in houses where closing doors quietly is a studied skill.

The woman's mouth was bleeding at the corner; the room was squared and gleaming, cushions squared, show-cabinet glass shining, a flat kept the way flats are kept when the world is invited in; the violence was already back in its drawer. She stood in the middle of her cage with her arm out and her breath going, forty-one years old perhaps, protecting it.

"I'll call the police," Manav said.

"And then what?"

He had prepared nothing after that. There is nothing after that. The nothing after *then* was the exact country he had been living in for two years — she was only quoting his own file back to him, and she had the better of the argument, and both of them knew it, and Deepak-ji, getting to one knee on the floor, smiled at his wife with blood in his teeth: the smile of a man whose alibi had just walked in wearing a mask.

Manav left the way he had come, past the dead intercom, out through the door that never latched.

---

By morning the society knew everything except what had happened.

A thief, masked. Tall as the door. Six feet, said the messages, and by noon six-two, and one man said knife and another said gun, and the error became history. The guard was doubled, the gate got a second chain, and it is remarkable what a second chain does for a building's opinion of itself. The committee expressed sympathy for its treasurer. A constable came, was given chai at the gate, took down six feet of description, and promised patrol; the patrolling began that night and ended that night. Somewhere a file was not born.

In the lift, Manav — five feet eight in shoes since 1999 — agreed gravely that the masked man sounded like somebody.

Days later, at the gate, the woman passed him with a bag of groceries, and for the length of one question mark she looked at him. The question got as far as the first word. Then the sentence closed over it, the way sentences do in that country, and she looked through him, and he let her. She had seen a body come through a door, that was all. Every body in that society was somebody's, and the wrongness of his walk could be filed under nothing, and was.

---

At home, at three, he cleaned the forearm with the water he had been saving for tea, because Farid had taught him nothing out loud and everything by example.

The mask's jaw had been opened in a clean seam. He sat in the kitchen with her needle and her grey thread and mended it, and the stitches went in straight. Every one. Nine nights of crooked and straight, a seismograph of the mind; and now, an hour after a man's mouth, straight as ruled work. He sat looking at that for a long time after, and the looking was its own finding.

By every number he owned, the night was a success. Crossed a room. Took a knife off a bad man with his body. Won. The numbers sat there being right. And underneath them, in the space where assessors write what they know and do not say, the finding that mattered: *the second minute had wanted its whole life.* Not the knife, not Deepak-ji — the taste. That was the true danger, and he put it where he put things now, knowing the filing solved nothing.

---

At the gym, one night at the wraps, he asked Ustad the question obliquely, the way one files a query.

"In your experience," he said. "A man who sees a wrong and says nothing. Fear?"

Ustad wrapped a boy's hands through the turnstile of his fingers, not looking up. The radio played at the volume of memory. Somewhere up the stairs a scooter went by with its whole life ahead of it.

"Fear is rare," Ustad said at last. "Loyalty is common. Men are not cowards, mostly. They are faithful — often to the wrong things. But you cannot fault the faith."

Whatever Ustad understood about the new stillness in his student, about ribs that healed in January and forearms that were cut in February, he left on the landing, as was the rule.

---

He came home to the television's lowest number and the boy asleep inside the noise of it, and stood in the doorway of the hall doing the new arithmetic.

In one building tonight, a boy had closed a door so softly only the hinge knew. In this one, a boy slept inside a sound, for reasons no file had ever held. *Witnesses become afraid* — the old sentence, worn smooth as river stone by two years of handling. He stood looking at his son and began, for the first time, to imagine the witnesses in his own file faithful instead — loyal to something, each of them, faithful to the wrong things — and the file grew heavier by a page.

He put the page where the mask lived, between two closed files, where nobody in the world opens anything.
